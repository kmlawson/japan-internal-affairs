# Yokohama-Palau Airline Closed to Foreigners

In a further effort to make a flight to the Mandated Islands, the Assistant for Air addressed a letter to the Japan Air Transport Company on 11 March 1940, requesting permission to take passage on the newly opened air line to Saipan and Palau. On 13 March, Mr. Gunji Hosono, foreign relations counsellor of the company, and ex-Foreign Office official, called in person at the Embassy.

His statements were frank and outspoken, and substantially as follows:

While he, and the Japan Air Transport Company, welcome any passengers on all their airlines, the military authorities have positively forbidden travel from Yokohama to Saipan and Palau to all, including Japanese, except military and naval officers. Only mail and some packages are carried by the Saipan-Palau line.

On every other air route, military permission must first be obtained before any foreigner may fly.

On the Tokyo-Fukuoka-Taihoku-Canton-Bangkok route, no Japanese or foreigner (special exemptions) are permitted on the Taihoku to Canton leg.

The Japan Air Transport Company is very short of planes, they have poor and inadequate maintenance facilities, and traffic is hampered by military regulations.

Recent crashes, he observed, were due to lack of repair and maintenance upkeep, and scarcity of replacement items.

Japan is desirous of extending a feeder line to Manila, and is prepared to grant full reciprocity rights to the United States, i.e., an American flight to Taihoku and Yokohama for each Japanese flight to Manila. Mr. Hosono requested our aid in furthering the project and was referred to Mr. Dooman, counsellor of the Embassy.

In parting, Mr. Hosono remarked that the only recourse open to allow a flight or trip to the Mandated Islands would be a formal request from the Embassy directed to the Japanese Foreign Office.

Subject: Air Transportation (Scheduled)
Japan—Thailand Air Service

According to a home dispatch of June 7, 1940, regular commercial air service will be established between Japan and Siam on June 10, 1940. Planes will leave Taihoku, Formosa, at 8:00 a.m. and arrive at Canton at 11:30 a.m. They will leave Canton "early" the next morning and reach Bangkok about 3:30 p.m. Returning they will leave Bangkok at 6:30 a.m. and arrive at Canton in the afternoon. Three planes including the Boyokase, Yamato and Matsukake of the Japan Airways will be used. These planes have been used for long range flights. One has been around the world, another has explored the Japanese mandated islands, and the third has already flown to Thailand and China.

An agreement for the establishment of this service was reached with Thailand last year. The stumbling block was the long hop between Canton and Bangkok. The Japanese have made repeated efforts to negotiate landing facilities in French Indo-China for this route. Since the Japanese bombing of the French railway in Yunnan, the negotiations have come to a halt. It is reported that the French authorities in Indo-China have categorically refused permission for Japanese planes to fly over their territory. This means that the Japanese will have to make an exceedingly long roundabout over-water hop between Canton and Bangkok in which the amount of fuel required will cut the pay load to minute proportions. The American Airways were worried for a long time that this route if established would cut into their profitable transpacific mail business. They feared that mail for the United States and Canada from India, the East Indies, the Malay States and Siam would be routed via the Japanese air line to Kobe and thence across the Pacific via steamer. However, as a result of the French opposition, the amount of mail that can be carried profitably over the long route is problematic.

Source: Press and study in this office.

November 18, 1941

MEMORANDUM

The following observations of an individual who recently visited Maizuru have been reported to the Federal Bureau of Investigation by an outside confidential and reliable source:

Large numbers of small craft are congregated in the harbor. These include submarines, destroyers and light cruisers.

The navy air base is situated at Uchuira on Uchuira Bay east of Maizuru.

The navy yard is building submarines and destroyers, being unable to handle anything larger. Four submarines are almost complete as well as one or two destroyers.

Considerable work is being done on port facilities. Piers and wharves are being built and repaired in preparation for large troop embarkation for Northern Korea, Manchuria and Siberia in the event of hostilities in that area. Maizuru will be used as a supplement to Tsuruga.

Strict control is exercised over the harbor, only local and other fishermen known to the authorities being allowed to enter.

A shortage of skilled labor exists as the higher wages in Kobe and Tokyo attract men away from Maizuru. Over 3,000 workmen are continuously employed.

The general feeling in the town of Maizuru is that war with Russia is coming.

It is also asserted that Mitsubishi aircraft factory in Nagoya is being changed over to the construction of tanks. The Nakajima Aircraft Company at Ochi in Gunma-ken has been expanded.

It has been alleged that the Japanese Navy has ceased attempting to reconstruct the new N. Y. K. liner "Izumo Maru" as an aircraft carrier. In Kobe it was said this was due to the ship's thin plating and other construction which does not permit of armor being used; therefore, she is now to be constructed as a hospital ship.

Reference Lisbon's despatch no. 292, December 1, 1941, "Portuguese Press Comment Regarding Japanese Airline at Timor and British Reaction".

A Portuguese newspaper, reprinting an editorial from the London Daily Telegraph expressing anxiety lest Portuguese Timor be transformed into another Indochina (in view of the establishment of the Japanese airline), comments that the Portuguese Government has maintained and will maintain an "unchangedly scrupulous" attitude. The Diaro de Notícias points out that the agreement between Japan and Portugal in regard to the airline is identical to that entered into by Portugal and Australia and that Portugal has never ceased to assert its decision to defend its possessions against attacks or abusive interference from any power.

LIBBON, December 1, 1941.
Subject: Portuguese Press Comment Regarding Japanese Airline at Timor and British Reaction

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that the opening of the service of the Japanese National Airline to the Portuguese Colony of Timor in accordance with the agreement recently concluded between the two countries continues to cause lively speculation and comment in the Portuguese press. The first plane was flown on November 24, and on November 28 the local newspapers carried a telegram from Canberra reporting that Dr. H. V. Evatt, the Australian Minister for Foreign Affairs, in the course of a review of external affairs at the House of Representatives had said that "the Australian Government is directly interested in the complete independence of Timor. It is certain that Timor may justly be considered as one of the main highways leading to Australia. The Australian Government has bestowed full consular powers upon its representative at Dilli, where Japan is likewise represented by a consul. It is clear that Australia cannot look with indifference upon the development of a threat against the integrity of this parcel of the Portuguese Empire."

On the following day there was prominently printed an Exchange Telegraph report of the following editorial which appeared in the London Daily Telegraph:

"Inasmuch as the import and export trade of Portuguese Timor does not normally exceed 100,000 pounds, the argument that this airline has any connection with commerce, does not deserve a moment of investigation. What Japan is seeking in Timor is to create unrest and not business. Japan's interest in the island is due to the latter's geographical position and not to its economical importance. Japan will bring intrigue,

espionage

espionage and other sinister designs to the only advanced post in the heart of the East Indies archipelago, at only 400 miles distance from Australia. Under the circumstances, the British Empire cannot permit that Portuguese Timor be transformed into another Indochina which, in principle, is apparent from the creation of that airline."

Following the publication of this press agency telegram, Diario de Noticias printed the following editorial footnote:

"It is difficult to leave without protest the statements of the Daily Telegraph referred to in the telegram reproduced above. These statements certainly do not express the thought of that newspaper but, even so, they must be adequately answered.

"The writer of the article in the Daily Telegraph must no doubt know that: (1) The agreement entered into between the Portuguese Government and Japan as to airlines in Timor, is identical to the agreement entered into between Portugal and Australia; (2) That Portugal has never ceased to assert, and in every circumstance to confirm, its decision to defend its possessions and its sovereignty against attacks or abusive interference on the part of anyone; (3) That in all questions regarding the complete independence of the Colonies, the Portuguese Government has maintained, maintains, and will maintain an unchangeably scrupulous attitude.

"After these statements--which appear to us sufficient to set at rest the fears of the Daily Telegraph--may we be permitted to add that if the purpose of the well-known London paper was to cause us to perceive the promise of a possible British assistance in the case of emergencies which it deems possible, that promise, in the terms in which it is made, hardly corresponds to the discernment, susceptibilities and reciprocity of duties which a secular alliance--often reaffirmed--imposes upon both countries. We feel certain that the British Government--knowing the constant correctness and loyalty which the Portuguese Government uses in fulfilling its historical undertakings--will agree with us."

While it is evident that the Portuguese are not particularly happy over having the Japanese in Timor, they resent any attempt of the British to involve them in difficulties with Japan and point out that having once granted a concession to the British, which has not yet been utilized, they could scarcely depart from this precedent and refuse a similar concession to another country since such action could be interpreted as a violation of their cherished policy of neutrality.

Respectfully yours,

Bert Fish

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

With reference to my despatch No. 292 of December 1, 1941, containing press comment on British criticism of the recently concluded air agreement between Japan and Portugal governing flights into the Portuguese colony of Timor, I have the honor to quote the following editorial from the evening paper, DIARIO DE LISBOA, in its edition of December 2.

The Portuguese Government has authorized Japan to establish an air line to Timor and has made a similar concession to the Australian Government. The negotiations were motivated by one sole purpose - to animate the affairs and the trade of Timor and to contribute, by free agreement, toward the development of air navigation. It is out of the question to believe that Portugal showed more favor to one of the grantees than it did to the other, considering the scrupulous attitude of the party which, on our behalf, conducted the negotiations. Why, then, does the DAILY TELEGRAPH maliciously suggest that our Government did not weigh carefully what it did? We know, of course, that one newspaper is not the whole British press and even less the whole of British opinion. Japan must fulfil the clauses of the agreement because if she does not do so she will suffer the stipulated consequences. As the DAILY TELEGRAPH will have occasion to observe, Portugal's best defense lies in the very loyal manner in which our authorities at Dili take care of Portuguese prestige."

Respectfully yours,

Bert Fish

Secretary General of the Foreign Office has categorically assured me that there are no secret clauses, reservations or engagements in the agreements between Portugal and Japan regarding air service to Timor. Sir:

I have the honor to refer to numbered paragraph 3 of the Department's instruction No. 73 of June 23, 1942 and to report further on the "changed circumstances" which make it impractical to recommend that the block of LAB stock owned by Junkers be paid for with the money owed to LAB by German insurance companies.

A8

=== Page 18 === (confidence 0.98)
-2-

At the time the suggestion was made it was thought that a friendly arrangement could be made with the German insurance companies to pay Junkers in return for the cancellation of LAB's insurance claim. Such an arrangement would naturally involve the agreement of all parties concerned, for there would be no way to force Junkers to accept the proposal. From the Junkers point of view it is believed likely that (1) they do not particularly wish to part with the stock, and (2) they may wish to argue about the price at which they are forced to sell. The insurance companies would in all likelihood not be especially desirous of seeing the LAB claim settled, since they have the use of the money in the meantime. Furthermore, Bolivia has since broken relations with Germany, and there is no reason why German firms would want to be of assistance to Bolivia in settling financial matters even if requested to do so by the Bolivian Government; nor would the Bolivian Government be likely to make the request; nor would there now be a convenient means of handling the transaction even if it did. Also, considerable delay would be inevitable.

For the reasons outlined above, it is believed that the suggestion is impracticable.

Respectfully yours,

For the Ambassador:

Allan Dawson
First Secretary of Embassy.

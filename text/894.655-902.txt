PRODUCTION OF STAPLE FIBER (ARTIFICIAL WOOL) IN JAPAN
General Progress in Japan
Following the successful manufacture in Germany of
staple fiber, or so-called "artificial wool", which was
begun iurin the World War as the result of interruption
of wool imports, Japan. which proluced practically no
wool or cotton, became interested in the new product.
It is a species of fiber of vegetable origin with char-
acteristics similar to wool, and exhaustive studies in
the laboratories of the Tmperial universities of Japan
have been made regarding its practical possibilities.
With the increasing activity throu hout Japan of
the textile industry, and especially of the rayon indus-
try, the possibility of staple fiber production becoming
an independent inaustry was considered by textile cireles
as well as by various promoters.
It is now considered
in Japan as a newly estuulished inlustry with fair
prospects of extensive development.
Although staple
fiber is yet in the experimental stage, both as to manu-
facture end possible uses, new factories have been es-
tablished at a number of places, and its production in
textile factories already established has been found
feasible, and is increasing.
The total daily production for Japan is estimated
at present as being four or five tons.
It has been es-
timated, however, that by the middle of 1935 the daily
production will be increased to fifty tons.
Reports From Chief Industrial Centers
According to a report from the Consulate at Kobe,
Japan, a synthetio fiber called "artificial wool" was

reliable statistics are obtainable, as production is

consisting largely in discussion and investigation of
manufacturing possibilities.
According to a report from the Consulate at Yokohama.

The manufacture of the product, however,
has not passed the experimen tal stage, and locally manu-

in commercial quantities.

Production of Staple Fibre.
Staple fibre is now produced on a commercial
scale in Japan by seven companies with an estimated
maximun total daily capacity of thirty-six and a half
metric tons.
Actual daily production is estimated
to reach 22.5 metric tons which represent about 60%
of capacity.
In addition to the co anies above men-
tioned which are actuelly producing on a comnercial
scale, there are some twelve companies with an esti-
mated maximum total daily cavacity of 4l.5 metric
tons now engaged in eyperimental production but whose
products h:ve not been placed on the market.
Jnclud-
ed in this number are six rayon producing compa: es
which have converted existing rayon machinery and in-
stalled new machinery for the manufacture of staple
fibre.
Only that portion of their machinery which
has actually been converted to the manufacture of sta-
ple fibre has been included in the total productive
capacity of 4l.5 metric tons above stated.
In addition to the companies now producing on a
commercial scale and those the production of which is
still experimental but nevertheless in actual opera-
tion, plans are on foot for the establishment of five
more companies which, if carried out as now intended,
would add a daily production of 17 metric tons to the
total already given.
It will thus be seen that to
the total of 36.5 metric tons daily capacity now in
commercial operation must be added a total of 4l.5
metric tons which may be placed on the market in the
near future, plus a total of 17 metric tons daily
capacity

Whether all of the experimental capacity will actu-
ally ever become conmercial or whether any of the
planned establishments will ever begin operation
is somewhat problematical, as will be seen from the
following discussion of the actual demand which may
be anticipated in the future.
Even as it exists at present the production of

In May, 1934, the daily production was

Consumption and Demand for Staple Fibre.
Staple fibre is used in Japan today both as a
yarn and for mixing with wool, silk, cotton, or
linen when woven into yarn.
When used alone as a
yarn the resultant fabric may be either heavy or
light.
The light fabric is siiuilar to woolen muslin
in appearance. Printed light fabrics are used in
the manufacture of women's and children's kimonos
while plain and dyed light fabrics are used as lin-
ings for winter kimonos and men's foreign-style
suits.
The heavy staple fibre fabric is similar
to woolen serge in appearance and is intended for
the same uses.
Owing to faulty appearance and
wearing

larity, although a strong potential demand may be
met if experiments now being carried on are success-
ful in improving the appearance and wearing quali-
ties of the synthetic woolen serge.
Of the total aaily production of staple fibre
of 22.5 metric tons, approximately 2.5 tons are used
in the manufacture of light fabrics in which the fi-
bre is the only material used, 15.5 tons are marketed
in the unfinished state for use in mixing with silk,
rayon, cotton, and linen, and 4.5 tons are marketed
as staple fibre yarn for later manufacture into
either light or heavy fabrics.
A further anount
estimated to reach two tons daily is manufactured
and used directly by a woolen textile manufacturing
company for mixing with wool.
The production of
staple fibre by this concern is tept confidential
and is unknown to trade circles, so it has not been
included in the above mentioned totals of daily pro-
duotion.
Exports of staple fibre are estimated to have
reached 300,000 pounds a month recently, which would
be equivalent to about 4.5 metric tons a day, an amount
equivalent to 2o of the total production of staple
fibre and products manufactured therefrom and to 30%
of the quantity of staple fibre now offered on the
market.
Germany is the most important market and
is estimated to consume at present about 200,000
pounds a month, followed by the United States to which
50,000 pounds a month are now exported, with India,

Future Prospects for the Staple Fibre Industry.
As has been noted, the consumption of staple fi
bre in Japan, both for domestic usage and for export,
does not exceed 22.5 metric tons per day, which re-
quires but 62% of the capacity of firms now actually
manufacturing on a commercial scale.
The demand has
increased enormously in recent months, but even so
has not kopt pace with the extension of manufacturing
facilities.
.The capacity now devoted to experimental
purposes is over double that of the capacity already
existing which is operating only at 62% of capacity.
If the plants now experimenting should, at the present
stage of demand, begin commercial production, the re-
sultant price drop caused by over-production would
severely hamper the industry.
The obviousness of this
conclusion is no guarantee that the situation will not
arise, as may be seen from the fact that similar over-
production caused by the too rapid expansion of produc-
ing facilities has already caused trouble in the rayon
industry which has been compelled to institute a ration-
ing system.
Trade circles believe, however, that a serious situa-
tion may not develop, as it is anticipated that the
demand

higher prices for cotton anā wool, staple fibre pro-

expensive natural materials.
It is not beyond the
bounds of possibility that with continued improvement
in staple fibre products, cotton and woolen textiles
may suffer the same competition that silk textiles
encountered with the introduction of rayon.
While
in the case of rayon the advance of the rayon indus-
try in J pan was somewhet to the detriment of another
national industry, that of sericulture, the competi-
tion of staple fibre would be witi cotton and wool,
both imported products for which Japan is dependent
on foreign sources of supply.
It is largely for

substantial encouragement to the staple fibre indus-
try and has encouraged industrialists to risk possible
losses in the present in order that Jupan may become
the world leader in the future if the industry dev-

Government encouragement to the
industry has oxtended beyond moral support and in-
cludes actual subsidy as well as the purchase of
comparatively large amounts of staple fibre by the
Clothing Department of the rmy.

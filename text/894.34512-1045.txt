Dear Sir:-

I have definite information concerning a Naval Base for the Japanese Government in Lower California, Mexico, and others, that will be backed up by an affidavit and cooperated. I want to get in touch with some one in authority and see that it reaches the proper authority, as soon as possible, as I got a lot of additional information yesterday thru personal contact with the party, and can get a lot more as soon as I hear from you.

DEPARTMENT OF STATE

MEMORANDUM

September 12, 1938.

Mr. Duggan:

In the attached confidential despatch of September 2 the Consul at Mexicali reports having received information from a friendly contact residing at Mexicali and engaged in fishing activities that about 7 months ago his men saw a Japanese submarine in the Bay of California. The Consul's informant expects to purchase a vessel from some Japanese fisherman and if the purchase is effected he will try the vessel out along practically the entire west coast. The informant believes that a Japanese naval or other military base may be hidden away at some uninhabited place. Should he make such a cruise the informant will be glad to have an observer go along with him in the guise of a worker or tourist.

The observer would travel without any charge. The Consul states he would be able and ready to go along with the informant for observation purposes, leaving the Vice Consul in charge.

R. C. Tanis

SUBJECT: Possibility of Japanese Naval Base in Gulf of California.

THE SECRETARY OF STATE Copy to ONI
WASHINGTON 129 4/7/38

SIR:

I have the honor to inform the Department that the Consulate has for some years been in friendly contact with a Mexicali resident of been perception and intelligence engaged with his own vessels in autumn fishing activities near San Felipe, on the Gulf of California, and from this man frequently received information valuable to the services performed by this office. He is well and favorable known to the United States Immigration and Naturalization Service at this border point of entry. He does not, however, for political reasons desire that his identity be revealed, and adds that to his knowledge a number of officials in Mexico City are "in league" with the Japanese.

The Consulate's informant states that about seven months ago his men saw a Japanese submarine on the surface of the water in the Bay of California, not far from Angel de la Guarda Island; that the submarine approached a Japanese vessel anchored there; that he does not

=== Page 5 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

not recall the name of the vessel but that it ended in "Maru"; that there was conversation between the crew of the submarine and the crew of the vessel; that the submarine then submerged and disappeared; that his (the informant's) fishermen saw marked activity on the part of Japanese vessels in that area from time to time; that these activities were not those of fishermen; that the Japanese vessels would allow no Mexican fishing craft to approach them, and that since that part of the Gulf between San Felipe and Angel de la Guarda Island is isolated and the shores entirely uninhabited for miles, he believes that a Japanese naval or other military base may be hidden away there. He states that it is less likely that hidden bases are to be found below Angel de la Guarda Island for the reason that, from that point south, the waters of the Gulf and the shores bordering it are frequented regularly by residents of that district. He advises, further, that there are several spots in the area first mentioned where aeroplanes could land and take off if necessary, and that he is convinced this is one of the best hiding places in Mexico for a Japanese espionage base.

The informant states that his company expects to purchase a certain vessel from some Japanese fisherman, and that, if the sale is effected, as he hopes, within the next two months, he will try the vessel out along practically the entire coast of the secluded area referred to. Should he do so, he would be glad to have an observer go along with him in the guise of a worker or tourist, and that no charge would be incurred as far as he was concerned. Were the same trip to be made expressly for

for the purpose of observation, a cost of about $150 would be incurred, counting the cost of gasoline, the pay of three or four men and their food for 10 or 15 days. I am confident that the informant had no real desire to arrange an excursion for his own profit, but was informed by him that any other craft than a Mexican fishing vessel in those waters would arouse suspicion on the part of any Japanese there, and might occasion their complete concealment. He said he was satisfied that a listening post or posts for aeroplanes was being maintained, or had been maintained.

Should the Department think best, and the informant make the trip discussed, I would be able and ready to go with him for observation purposes, leaving Vice Consul Myers in charge, and of course taking particular care not to mention to any one the reason for my absence. It would seem doubtful if an expenditure of $150 in order to make the trip by means of another vessel (than the one which may be purchased as explained) would be justified.

There are said to be about 2500 Japanese farmers in this district, many of whom are now banded together for agricultural advancement under the leadership of the local Japanese Consular representative. Mr. Naoya Nagamine, whose card reads "Jefe del Sucursal del Consulado General del Japon."

Mr. Nagamine, who is married to a (white) Argentine lady, has shown himself for no particularly good reason very friendly to this office and insisted upon offering me a dinner to which Vice Consul Myers and Mr. De Los Cobos, Clerk, were invited. In view of Mr. Nagamine's statement to

Mexicali, B.C., Mexico, September 22, 1938.

James B. Stewart, Esquire,
American Consul General,
Mexico, D.F., Mexico.

Sir:

I take pleasure in acknowledging your letter dated September 14, 1938, File 102.2/850.101, marked confidential, regarding a statement on page 3 of a despatch to the Department from this office, No. 10, dated September 2, 1938, File 800., that there are said to be 2,500 Japanese farmers in this district. You point out that Consul Bowman in a letter to you dated May 26, 1937, File 102.2/850.101, reported the number as 200, and ask me to supply you with any comment I deem pertinent.

The figure given in my despatch as the reported number of Japanese farmers in this district was supplied by Mr. Carlos Gil Hinojosa, Mexicali, actively engaged in fishing activities, well and favorably known to this office for some time and also to the officers of the United States Immigration and Naturalization Service, Calexico and Los Angeles, to whom he has, from time to time, supplied information. I have taken the matter up with him again and find that he meant to refer to the entire Baja California region and not to this district. The opportunity has been found to discuss the situation (casually) with representative Mexicans and Americans living or working in this city most of whom I have come in easy contact with through membership in the Mexicali Rotary Club. The replies received applicable to the total number of Japanese in the district varied between 250 and 2,500, and were of no particular assistance. The registry office at the Palacio, Mexicali, reports that 350 Japanese subjects are registered but adds (1) that these figures apply to Japanese residing in or near Mexicali only, that (2) they do no refer to Japanese who have acquired Mexican citizenship or (3) those who have been born here. The office also admits that its data is far from complete.

have so far had no good opportunity to approach the local Japanese Consul but will undoubtedly be able to learn something from him at a later date. It has, however, been readily possible to question the Secretary of the Mexicali Japanese Farmers' Association, since he visits the Consulate frequently, and it is learned from him that there are "about" 700 Japanese, all told, in this district, that of these about 300 are male adults, and that in all of Baja California there are, in his opinion, not over 1,200. He said that there were now not over 3 or 4 Japanese families living in or near San Luis Sonora, and that not more than 15 families were planning to move onto lands between the old Colorado River bed and the Rio Pescadero on the edge of the State of Sonora. The manager of the local Banco Mercantil has advised me that his bank has a full record of the plan for colonization by Japanese of this district referred to, and that at least 150 Japanese are counted upon for the project. I feel that my Japanese informant has, for the ideals of patriotism, somewhat scaled down his figures for my benefit. Just how much he has done so is difficult to state.

As a final step, I reviewed and discussed the matter in confidence with Manager Stone, Compania Industrial Jabonera del Pacifico, S.A., Mexicali, in many ways the outstanding American business man in Mexicali, and readily arrived at what I believe to be a close estimate. It may be added that Mr. Stone is in close contact with agrarians and farmers throughout all of this district. There are believed to be not less than 500 Japanese adult farmers in the district at this time, as compared with 1,500 Chinese farmers. The total number of Japanese would bring the number up to 800 or 1,000. The number is undoubtedly increasing by clandestine entries to Mexico.

This office is very glad that you raised the subject and in order that my despatch may be amended and made of more value a copy of this letter is being mailed to the Department.

Very truly yours,

Horatio Mooers
American Consul

SIR:

I have the honor to refer to my confidential despatch, No. 10, of September 2, 1938, entitled "Possibility of Japanese Naval Base in Gulf of California", and to the Department's reply thereto, dated September 16, 1938, (File 894.34512/4).

The Mexicali ship-owner referred to has advised that he expects very soon to make a leisurely journey along the coast of the Gulf, and has promised to report whatever he may observe. It may be added that the appearance of the Japanese submarine in the Gulf of California has been vouched for by others in this district and Calexico, and it appears from conversations with an officer of the American Government working in Southern California that the Intelligence Department of our government has already been acquainted with the fact.

Any additional information of interest that may be learned will be reported promptly.

For the Department's possible assistance, it may be added

=== Page 12 === (confidence 0.97)
-2-

added that a German engineer now residing in Mexicali for a number of years, and well known to this office, told the Consulate yesterday that between Tijuana and Ensenada there are "a number" of Japanese homesteads of which the floors are solid concrete or cement with depressions or slots for the quick erection of artillery. The informant says that last year he happened to gain entry to one of these houses during a business call, and verified what he had been told. Mexicans also told him there were "a number" of Japanese farms nearby with identical floors. He said that he would be able to indicate on maps, made use of in his work, just where the Japanese farm visited is situated. It may be added that this man was formerly a Major in the German Army, later an instructor and officer in the Mexican Army, and at present is an employee of the Mexicali Public Works Department.

He fails to see what particular use would be put to gun bases at the interior points where they are said to be erected, unless they may have been conceived as bases for anti-aircraft guns.

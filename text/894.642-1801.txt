to date are for two whale catcher boats of 420 gross tons
each, the keels for which were laid in June, but a larger
number of contracts were expected to be signed during the
last part of July. No vessels of 6,000 gross tons or
over are under construction for Japanese account and none
are contemplated in the 1948 Japanese shipbuilding pro-
gram.
The principal consideration causing the Supreme
Commander to authorize the construction in Japan of ships
for foreign account is that suggested in the Embassy's
aide-mémoire, namely, the necessity for improving Japan's
balance of payments position. Considering that one of
the principal objectives of the occupation is the eco-
nomic recovery of Japan to a self-supporting status, it
is essential that all possible Japanese resources be
utilized to produce foreign exchange income. This
principle

=== Page 23 === (confidence 0.99)
-3
principle is being applied not merely to shipbuilding
but also to other industries subject to reparations
removals. By utilizing materials furnished in many
instances by prospective purchasers, and which would not
otherwise be obtainable, it has been found possible to
build merchant ships and other manufactured products in
Japan at a profit in foreign exchange. The income thus
derived can be used to purchase raw materials for the
production of other export items, thereby contributing
to general industrial recovery, reduction of occupation
costs and the eventual attainment by Japan of a self-
supporting status.
The British Government is of course keenly aware of
the continuing acute world shortage of tankers and some
other types of merchant ships. A second consideration
leading to the Supreme Commander's decision to authorize
the construction of ships for export has been a desire
to utilize facilities available in Japan to help meet
this need. It might be noted in this conneetion that
United Kingdom

in Jepanese yards.
The Government of the United States cannot agree
with the contention of the British Government that the
building by the Japanese of merchant ships for export
constitutes a security threat. The Supreme Commander
for the Allied Powers, who is keenly alive to any pos-
sible security threat, has stated categorically that no
such threat exists. This opinion is concurred in by the
Department of the Navy.
As already stated, no large vessels are being con-
structed for Japanese account and none are contemplated
in the 1948 shipbuilding program. Considering that the
ships for export are being built to customer specifica-
tions, it is clear that they will not augment Japan's
maritime strength now or in future. No reserve force
of skilled workmen in excess of Japan's peaceful needs
will be created, because the trained workmen are already
available from prewar days when Japan employed over a
half

half million persons in the shipbuilding and repair
industry. While some workers who might not otherwise
re-enter the shipbuilding industry may do so as a
consequence of the export program, and so renew their
skills, this will be of little consequence from a secu-
rity point of view if the maximum shipbuilding capaeity
finally prescribed for Japan is insufficient to permit

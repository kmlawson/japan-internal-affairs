Silk:

Although the Government in its efforts toward rationalization and relief of unemployment has as yet accomplished little more than formation of plans for further discussions in its efforts to alleviate depression in the raw silk industry it made a definite step in March. Silk is Japan's most important industry. Approximately two million households are engaged in raising cocoons. The condition of the raw silk market is therefore of vital importance. During the several preceding months the price of raw silk had steadily declined until on March 1st it fell to the neighborhood of Yen 1,100 per bale, the lowest quotation since 1921. Sericulturists effected a curtailment of raw silk production and undertook to withdraw available output from the market by means of joint storage. To support them the Government brought into force the law of monetary compensation for the regulation of the price of raw silk, the object being to prevent a violent slump in the price of raw silk and to stabilize the market. The effect is as follows: The Government will assist the raw silk industry through guaranteeing banks losses to the extent of Yen 30,000,000 but not to exceed Yen 1,900 per bale. It is understood in making these

these loans that the banks are to consider the market value of the silks to be Yen 1,250 per bale, based on Saiyu grade. All loans are to be made before June 10th and only for the 1929 crop. The inspection is the visibility test sponsored primarily by the American Silk Association.

The bringing into effect of the law in question, which was the outstanding step of the Government in connection with its economic policies during March, was the subject of adverse criticism in the press. The Tokyo JIJI, one of the most influential papers, is opposed to discrimination in favor of the silk industry. This industry, it feels, has not been properly managed, especially in its failure to build up a proper reserve fund. It quotes figures with respect to both the silk and the cotton industries in which the management of the former is shown to compare unfavorably with that of the latter. The present dilemma of the silk industry, in the opinion of the JIJI, is the result of many years of poor conduct. The attitude of the Tokyo ASAHI, another of the important papers of Japan, is similar. The silk industry has always been too prone to run to the Government for aid instead of devising its own relief measures. The Government's action it regards as a blunder, as unfair favoritism, and as a contradiction of the

From The Japan Advertiser, Tokyo, July 30, 1930.

# NEW COCOON DRYING SYSTEM ANNOUNCED

Professor Miake Claims Invention Will Save Silk Industry
¥200,000,000

CUTS DOWN LABOR COSTS

Practical Tests Now Being Made With Final Result Known in February

A new method of preserving the raw cocoon, which will mean, it is said, a saving to the Japanese silk industry of approximately ¥200,000,000 a year, has been announced by Professor Bunjiro Miake, of the Tokyo Higher Sercultural School.

The new method is known as the Adozol Cooling System. The system is now under experimentation at the Ishiwara factory in Kumagaya, Saitama Prefecture, the plant of the Katakura Silk Reeling and Spinning Company. Twelve tons of cocoons are now being cooled by the system in a warehouse. It is stated that results so far have been eminently satisfactory, but that the final result will not be known until February next.

Professor Miake, it was stated, has tested his system repeatedly under various conditions, during the past seven years and has found it absolutely satisfactory.

The presented method of drying cocoons is by artificial heat. The new system cools the cocoon and removes all the moisture from it. The new method, it was stated, prevents wastage and cuts down the labor in preserving it, increasing efficiency approximately 45 per cent.

The expense of operating the system was placed at 3 sen per eight pounds and it is possible that this will be further reduced. This present rate cut the old method of preservation by 50 per cent. As Japan will produce approximately 360,000 tons of cocoons a year, the saving in this department alone will be around ¥7,000,000.

"The preservation of cocoons by the cooling system has been tried repeatedly," said Professor Miake, "and has always failed. Other systems advanced have either been unsuccessful or impractical due to excessive cost. My invention is based on the principal of constantly removing the moisture from the cocoons.

By my method, the volume of silk on a given amount of cocoon will be increased by 20 per cent. In addition to this the reeling of silk from the cocoon is much easier."

It is also claimed that the cocoon can be dried in a given area under the new system. The drying of these space is decreased by one third. The drying of cocoons by the reeling requires special drying. It is said that the system any warehouse has been cooled into an efficient drying process.

SECTION D - SILK:

It is impossible to give an accurate statement of the capital invested in the silk industry. The industry is primarily agricultural and the filatures who buy the cocoons from the farmers do not have extensive bank deposits but borrow from banks from time to time to finance their cocoon purchases. The value of the filature plants if available would not give any idea of the actual money tied up in the industry.

The Commercial Attache estimates the total cost to the silk industry outstanding at present to be in excess of 250,000,000 yen. Of this amount approximately 120,000,000 represents loans against silk in storage of which there are said to be over 100,000 bales of the 1929 reeling still on hand. There are some 3800 silk filatures (?) and the loans to these amount perhaps to 150,000,000 yen. In many instances the silk reelers have been

B. Silk.

The government's sale of 107,850 bales of raw silk to Mr. Pauline Corli, as reported in the month of April, seems to have brought on more complications in that industry instead of settling it. The details of the contract have never been made public but either the government or the buyers, or both, appear to have committed themselves to certain action which would have kept up the price of silk. Whatever was supposed to be done was not done or at least did not succeed and the price of silk has continued to drop. The problems are whether to go through with the deal or cancel the contract, how to dispose of the silk if the contract is cancelled, and how to keep up the price. The matter is undoubtedly one of the principal problems confronting the new government and any solution will

D. Silk.

Discussions of the raw silk problem which went on during the month of April culminated in a tremendous deal. Details are not yet known but it is reported that the government sold 107,830 bales of raw silk to Mr. Macline Cerli, president of the Silk Association of America, through his agents the Asahi Silk Corporation. The price is not definitely known, nor the method and rate of exchange of payment. It is reported, however, that the price was Yen 455 per bale, which brings the total value of the sale to over ,15,000,000.

It is estimated that the total loss which this sale will involve amounts to approximately Yen 100,000,000, of which the Government is bound for Yen 30,000,000 and the reelers and bankers for the rest. The sale was made for the purpose of getting rid of silk that was placed in storage during the past few years in an effort to try to prevent falling prices.

The silk industry as a whole suffered from the steady decline in prices which reached a level during the month below the prices prior to the replacing of the gold embargo.

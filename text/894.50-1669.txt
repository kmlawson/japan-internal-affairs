Subject: Five-Year Plan for Japanese Economic Rehabilitation Prepared by Economic Stabilization Board.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to this Mission's despatch No. 27 of January 9, 1948, transmitting a report by the Economic and Scientific Section, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, concerning the possibility of achieving a balanced Japanese economy and to forward a copy of a summary of a Five-Year Plan for Japanese Rehabilitation prepared by the Economic Stabilization Board of the Prime Minister's Office. It was given to an officer of this Mission by Mr. TSURU Shigeto, Deputy Secretary-General of the Board. The plan, which according to Mr. TSURU, has been in process of preparation since July 1947, has just been presented to the Economic and Scientific Section of this Headquarters in the form of the enclosed summary together with a text considerably more extended than the summary, though not comprising the fully detailed plan.

Mr. Tsuru said that he has knowledge that the Economic and Scientific Section has prepared a plan which has been taken to Washington by the Chief of the Research and Statistics Division of the Economic and Scientific Section. On the latter's return from Washington, the Economic and Scientific Section and the Japanese Government can, Mr. Tsuru believes, work out a plan which will be satisfactory. (It is of interest that Mr. Tsuru was at one time an adviser to the Economic and Scientific Section during which time he worked on the plan which he states has now been taken to Washington.) Although unaware of the details of the plan as finally evolved because of its confidential nature, he believes that it is more general than the Japanese plan.

The summary states that the Japanese five-year plan aims at a balanced and stabilized economy by achieving:

1. Minimum standard of living capable of supporting an efficient labor force.
2. Balance between exports and imports.
3. Level and structure of industries in equilibrium with minimum standard of living and import-export balance described in 1 and 2.
4. Reasonably full employment and rise of labor productivity to an internationally competitive level.

It is pointed out that these conditions may not be achieved simultaneously and that it is uncertain that everyone (presumably in the Japanese Government, in this Headquarters and in the Far Eastern Commission) can agree on exact quantitative contents of these conditions.

The Economic Stabilization Board, has, however, set the following targets:

1. A standard of living in 1952 approximately equal to the average of the period 1930-1934.
2. By 1952, approximate balance in Japanese international accounts.

In order to adjust these two recommended targets to take into account the expected increase in population, it is noted that Japan's stated real income in 1952 should attain a level twenty-five percent higher than that of the base period.

A preliminary study, the summary states, has indicated that the attainment of these goals is practically impossible because of:

1. Shortage of industrial power.
2. Difficulty of importing sufficient raw materials.
3. Difficulty of raising productivity of labor quickly.
4. Slowness of expanding agricultural production.

Downward adjustments have therefore been made in the Japanese plan in order to make the targets for 1952 as realistic as possible. It is also noted in the summary that the necessity for large

for large capital investment may result in fixing the consumption level somewhat lower than might be expected from such gain as may be attained in real national income.

The plan sets up basic economic target indexes (table 1) varying between ninety-four percent of the base period for domestic consumption of food and textiles and one hundred forty-one percent for the production of coal. In the summary there are estimates of population increases year by year (table 2) from an average of sixty-six million in the base period 1930-1934 to around eighty-three million in 1952. There is also set out a schedule of anticipated major industry components of national income during the years 1948-1952 (table 3). By 1952 it is estimated that the total national income will attain a level of one hundred twenty percent of the base period 1930-1934, but per capita real income a level of but ninety-six percent of the same period.

Recovery in mining and manufacturing, according to the summary, constitutes the most important aspect of the entire rehabilitation program. In connection with this aspect of rehabilitation, a supply program of basic industrial goods for 1952 has been compiled (table 4) showing production, imports, and exports. During the first two years, 1948 and 1949, emphasis is placed upon the expansion of production or generation of industrial power and the rehabilitation of transportation facilities. At the same time special attention is given to increasing the supply of basic consumer goods during the same years with a view to checking the inflationary trend.

From 1950 on, the emphasis turns toward expanding the level of industrial production in general and thus the level of exports. Unless the first part of the program is successful, the summary states that there is little chance of achieving the targets enumerated. If the targets for domestic production of staple foodstuffs (table 5) can be achieved and the estimated amounts of foods imported, per capita daily consumption of foodstuffs will amount in 1952 to 2,111 calories and 69 grams of protein, 94 and 103 percent respectively of the base period. This will enable the food ration of the non-agricultural population to be increased from a present level of 1,317 calories per person per day to 1,744 calories.

The plan envisages an approximate balance in international accounts for 1952 with 1,640 million dollars of visible exports and 20 million dollars invisible exports as against 1,657 million dollars of imports. The projected volume of trade is thus close to the level of the base period in real terms, but assumes a fairly optimistic set of conditions as regards international economic relations, particularly those in the Far East.

Textile production is calculated to occupy top position in exports (table 6) just as in pre-war years, with a decline in raw silk to one-fifth of the former years and an expansion in machinery exports of almost ten times the pre-war figure. Imports of goods for industrial use and of foodstuffs were approximately in the ratio of seven to three during the base period

1. Solving of domestic inflation.
2. Breaking the bottlenecks in industrial power and transportation.
3. Raising productivity of labor.
4. Aid from abroad.
5. Recovery of trade with Far Eastern countries.
6. Reparations.
7. Length and extent of occupation needs.
8. Internal political stability and leadership.

The summary ends by setting forth the extent of foreign economic assistance believed required to achieve the program (table 8). Private long-term investment, which is expected to commence prior to 1952 and thus aid further in a rehabilitation of the Japanese economy, remains outside the purview of this program. Hence, aside from ordinary private investment, the completion of the program requires assistance to the extent of 1,631 million dollars for the four years 1948 through 1951, and use of the revolving fund credit to the extent of 300 million dollars at the maximum.

FULLY CONFIDENTIAL 25 January 1948

THE FIVE YEAR PLAN
for
JAPAN'S ECONOMIC REHABILITATION

(Summary)

1. Basic conditions for a balanced and stabilized economy may be set down as follows:

(1) Securing of the minimum standard of living that enables the maintenance and reproduction of labor power.

(2) Exports sufficient to cover the necessary imports.

(3) The level and structure of industries that are in balance with the above two conditions.

(4) Attainment of reasonably full employment and the rise of labor productivity to an internationally competitive level.

These conditions may not be achieved simultaneously; nor is it certain that everyone can agree on exact quantitative contents of these conditions.

2. We have set down in this study as targets of quantitative requirements the following two desiderata:

(1) That we shall achieve in 1952 approximately the level of living standard enjoyed by Japanese during the period of 1930 to 1934.

(2) That we shall also achieve by 1952 an approximate balance in the country's international accounts.

Since the average population in the base period (1930-34) was 66 million and the population in 1952 is expected to be around 83 million, it means that real national income in 1952 has to attain the level 25 percent higher than that of the base period.

3. Our preliminary study based upon realistic prospects as to various objective conditions has indicated that the attainment of the above targets will be well-nigh impossible, limiting conditions being such factors as the shortage of industrial power, the difficulty of importing sufficient quantity of raw materials, the difficulty of raising the labor productivity fast enough, the slowness in the expansion of agricultural production, etc. Therefore, in this study we have

(5) Foreign Trade

International accounts for 1952 are assumed to achieve an approximate balance with 1,646 million dollars of exports, 1,657 million dollars of imports, and 20 million dollars of favorable invisible balance. The volume of trade assumed here is very close to the level of the base period in real terms. However, these figures constitute more of targets than probable prospects, and do assume a fairly optimistic set of conditions as regards international economic relations, in particular, as regards the conditions in the Far East. As for imports, the ratio of imports for industrial uses and those for food uses used to be about seven to three during the base period; such a ratio will be changed to six to four by 1952, reflecting the fact that the food production does not expand pari passu with the increase in population. The assumption that the "per worker productivity" in 1952 will recover to 90 percent of the base year is somewhat arbitrary. However, if this assumption is approximately correct, the unemployment residue in 1952 can be kept within manageable size. Even on this assumption, the intervening years present a serious problem of unemployment. If the trend of recovery in "per worker productivity" from 1948 on is assumed to be: 60%, 65%, 73%, 82%, and 90% of the base period, estimates of unemployment residue for intervening years are as high as 3,811,000 for 1948, 3,317,000 for 1949, and 2,867,000 for 1950. If we are to reduce these unemployment residues, we cannot hope for the recovery in productivity even of the magnitude assumed here.

5. The program presented here partakes more of the nature of targets rather than probable prospects. In order that these goals may be achieved, there are many conditions that have to be satisfied. Only to mention a few, there are such problems as: the solving of domestic inflation, breaking the bottleneck in industrial power and transportation, the raising of productivity of labor, various kinds of aid from abroad, recovery of trade with Far Eastern countries, the settlement of reparation, the length and extent of occupation needs, etc. Not the least of the conditions, of course, is the internal political stability and leadership.

# FERROUS METALLURGY IN JAPAN UNDER WAR CONDITIONS
By E. Pigulevskaya.

For a great number of years Japan has been preparing for war and collecting reserves of war materials and raw materials including iron ore, pig iron, steel and scrap iron. At the same time Japan pursued a policy of strict economy in the expenditure of raw materials for current needs and in fact even for the needs of the war in China. In his speech of March 10, 1942, General Sato stated that, from the beginning of the Sino-Japanese war until the beginning of the war in the Pacific, 40% of the war budget had been spent on the war in China and the remaining 60% on preparations for a new war. The expenditure of iron and other materials, he stated, was even less. Only 20% went on the war in China and the remaining 80% were reserved for increasing the stocks of war materials and for the manufacture of armaments. Since it is in the interests of the Japanese to create an exaggerated impression of Japan's strength and preparedness these statements are hardly reliable, but Japan undoubtedly reserved a considerable part of her resources for the new war which was in preparation. Many private concerns working on the needs of the war had orders to hold large supplies of these materials in reserve against emergencies; it was forbidden to use them for current production.

As Japan's sources of raw materials are in general limited the accumulation of reserves of metallurgical raw materials had to a certain extent a detrimental effect on the current work of the Japanese enterprises which were irregularly supplied. The metallurgical enterprises suffered from a chronic shortage of iron and steel scrap, iron ore, coke and various subsidiary materials (Diamondo - 1940, 1941).

The accumulation of reserves was, however, hindered by the economic blockade, introduced by Great Britain and the United States in answer to the occupation of South Indo-China by Japanese troops. The sequestration of Japanese funds in Great Britain and the United States at the end of July 1941 and the cessation of the import of the most important raw materials threatened Japan with the exhaustion of the accumulated stocks and even forced her to reduce her output of pig iron and steel. According to the "Far Eastern Survey" the output of steel was reduced by 15-20% in August and September 1941, mainly owing to the cessation of the import of iron and steel scrap from the U. S. A.

In spite of Japan's efforts her accumulated stocks are certainly insufficient and can last for a limited period only. In the opinion of foreign experts they are sufficient for 6 months or a year of war. In order to make up for the poorness of her own reserves Japan is trying to use the resources of the countries which she has occupied in the Pacific, but here she is meeting with a series of difficulties. In the course of 5 years of occupation of areas in North and Central China rich in raw materials Japan has failed to create there a solid base of raw material supplies for metallurgical production.

Japan has no large reserves of iron ore and coking coal. Her metallurgical industry worked chiefly on imported raw material. Even on the eve of the war in China, when Japan produced one and a half times less pig iron than she does now, 74% of her iron ore was imported, mainly from China and the South Seas. Over 50% of the iron and steel scrap needed for Japan's steel industry, about 20% of pig iron and a considerable amount of steel materials were also imported.

The production facilities of the Japanese metallurgical industry have expanded considerably within the last few years and its demand for raw materials has greatly increased. A new metallurgical plant has been built at Birobata in the prefecture of Hego with an output capacity of 700,000 tons of pig iron and 500,000 tons of steel a year; and large new blast furnaces have been constructed at the Yavata, Vanisi, Kendziho and Kamaisi plants belonging to the semi-official "Nihon Seitetsu" trust. The Nihon Kokai, Kokura Seyko, Nakayama and other plants have increased their capacity. The capacity of the "Seva Seiko" combined metallurgical plant in Manchuria has been increased to 1,700,000 tons of pig iron per year. These improvements however are far from corresponding to the plans of the Japanese government.

According to their plans the capacity of their productive equipment should have been increased by 1941 to 12,500,000 tons of pig iron (including Japan proper, Korea, Manchuria and North China) and 11,000,000 tons of steel. Now the dates at which the construction of blast furnaces and rolling mills is to be completed have been moved to the end of 1943 ("Asahi" May 8, 1942), but it is hardly possible that the Japanese will be able to fulfill their plan even in the course of that period.

According to various data, the productive capacity of the Japanese metallurgical industry at the beginning of the war in the Pacific amounted in all to about 6,000,000 tons of pig iron in Japan proper and Korea and about 2,000,000 tons in Manchuria, that is - a total of 8,000,000 tons. In practice the capacity was still less. The new blast furnaces could not be used to their full capacity owing to a shortage of

of subsidiary equipment, such as coking plants and equipment for sorting coal and ore. The utilization of the new equipment was also frequently hindered by the absence of raw materials.

According to estimated data the output of pig iron on the eve of the war did not exceed 3,000,000 tons in Japan proper and Korea, while Manchuria produced a little over 1,000,000 tons, thus making a total of about 4,500,000 tons. The output of steel in Japan, Korea and Manchuria amounted to about 7,000,000 tons. It must be remembered that at that time the United States produced about 88,000,000 tons of steel, that is twelve and a half times more than Japan.

In order to maintain the output of pig iron and steel at the pre-war level Japan must have an annual supply of from ten to twelve million tons of iron ore (dependent on its quality) and about 3,000,000 tons of iron and steel scrap. Only about 3,000,000 tons of iron ore in all are mined in Japan proper and Korea, and not more than 4,000,000 tons of low-grade ore in Manchuria per year.

It is highly improbable that the occupied areas of North and Central China are now yielding more than 2,000,000 tons of ore per year. Owing to this limited supply of ore Japan cannot count on making full use of her existing productive equipment, since to do so she would need no less than 20,000,000 tons of iron ore, which are not available to her. It is doubtful whether Japan has been able to accumulate any considerable stocks of iron ore.

Iron ore was imported by Japan mainly from the countries which are now in the zone of Japanese occupation. In 1940 Japan imported about 2,000,000 tons of iron ore from British Malaya where the Japanese held concessions, and 1,300,000 tons from the Philippines. The Japanese will naturally make every effort to re-establish the exploitation of the wealth of iron ore in the occupied areas as soon as possible, but the

re-establishment of mining in these countries on the pre-war scale would only give her 3,300,000 tons in all.

This fact, as well as the possibility that communications between Japan and the occupied countries may be broken, has led the Japanese military authorities to insist on an increase of ore mining in Japan proper, Korea, and Manchuria, and also on research for substitutes. Particular attention is devoted to the problem of supplying local iron ore to the Javata plants (north part of Kiushu*) - the largest metallurgical enterprise in Japan - which are mostly dependent on imported ore.

Before the war in China about 620,000 tons of ore were mined annually in Japan proper, and the Japanese press repeatedly stated that it would be difficult, under any circumstances, for Japan to produce more than 1,000,000 tons of iron ore per year.

The Japanese are therefore again planning, as they did after the first world war, to use their iron bearing sands. But before the war in China the output of iron sand did not exceed 6,000 tons a year. According to estimated data they have been mined recently to the amount of 100,000 tons which were mainly used for the manufacture of special steel.

Mosan ore, which has a low content of metal, has been mined in North Korea since 1937 by the Mitsubishi firm**. In recent years a concentrating plant has been built in Seisin. It was supposed that about six or eight million tons of low-grade ore would be mined in Korea, which according to the plan for 1940 would yield after concentration 2,500,000 tons of rich ore with a content of over 60% metal. Two-thirds of the concentrated ore was to be used in Japan (by the Hirobata plant) and the remainder was to be used for making steel by the Krupp method at the new plant in Seisin. This plan however has not been fulfilled; the amount of ore mined is much lower than the estimate. The Seisin plant has not been functioning for the production of steel at least not until recently.

In Manchuria also, as in Korea, the Japanese mine chiefly low-grade ore. Most of the ore mined in Manchuria is used locally.* The Japanese estimate the ore reserves of Manchuria at 1,700,000,000 tons, containing on an average about 32% metal and only 130,000,000 tons of rich ore.

About 80% of the pig iron produced in Manchuria is produced by the "Seva Seiko" combined metallurgical plant which is supplied by the Anshang Plants. Anshang ore contains about 35% metal which concentration brings up to 60%. A small amount of rich ore from the Myaoerguo mine is used by another Manchurian concern - the Bensihu. The wide plans for the development of metallurgical production at Dunbendao in East Manchuria, near the Korean frontier, where there are large reserves of rich ore, have not been carried out, and this deposit has been worked on a small scale only. New efforts are now being made to increase the output of ore from Dunbendao, but the exploitation of this deposit is impeded by the absence of capital and of the necessary equipment and building materials. The unceasing partisan warfare in that area also has considerable significance.

If in this war Japan has been unable to achieve, in the course of four years, any considerable increase in her output of iron ore, she can hardly expect to do it under war conditions.

The occupied areas of North and Central China were at first considered very important in the plans for supplying iron ore to Japan, but in January 1941 the director of one of the largest Japanese steel corporations stated that no important results had been obtained. ("North China Daily News", April 19, 1941.)

China has large reserves of iron ore. Her principal iron ore deposits were seized by the Japanese in 1938, but work at the most important old mines has not yet been fully restored. It is true that the Japanese have begun to work some new deposits on a small scale.

The Japanese have not sufficient capital, equipment, building materials or skilled labour for an extensive exploitation of the reserves of iron ore in the occupied parts of China. Most of the mines which are functioning are using old equipment. The small freight capacity of the Chinese railways makes it difficult to transport the ore to Japan, and partisan warfare is causing great damage to the work of the mines and railways.

The ore mined in China is mostly used by the Javata plants, which belong to the semi-official "Nihon Seitotsu" trust. The large iron ore mines in Dae (Hopei province), whose reserves are estimated at 40,000,000 tons, and which used to supply these plants with about 440,000 tons of ore in pre-war years, have not been fully restored. So far the Japanese have restored only a small group of mines in the area of Nanking (Anhwei province)*, which according to Japanese data produced 500,000 tons of ore in 1940.

Soon after the occupation the Japanese began to exploit a new deposit in Lunyan (southern part of the province of Chahar) which was thought to be very important. However the problem of the transport of the ore from Lunyan is a complicated one and its role in supplying Japan with iron ore has not so far been very great. The export of iron ore from Lunyan to Japan amounted to 100,000 tons in 1939, 225,000 tons in 1940, and 250,000-300,000 tons in 1941.

Iron ore mining in Shansi province is done partly by hand and is unimportant. In Shantung the Japanese tried to restore the working of the iron ore deposit in Tsinlinchen; which is in the hands of the "Luda" company ("Siuho", July 9, 1941); here the Japanese met with a whole row of serious obstacles and all their attempts to restart the mining of ore ended in failure.

In the Kiangsu province only the small deposits of Hunshansyan and Ligon are mined. The Japanese have also begun to work the iron ore deposits on Hainan Island; this is done by the Isihara and Nihon Tisso companies. The Japanese press states that in 1942 the output of iron ore from Hainan will be equal to that from Dae.

Japan is also trying to improve the supply of metallurgical coke and pig iron from China for her enterprises. After the occupation of North China they began restoring three metallurgical enterprises: in Taiwan and Yantsyuan at the end of 1939**, in Shansi province and in Shitsinshan (at the end of 1940), and in the area of Beipin in Hupeh province. In November 1940 a small new blast furnace was put in operation in Taiwan and the construction of an open hearth furnace was completed by the middle of 1941.

As however the enterprises of the occupied areas of North China have an inconsiderable productive capacity they do not play any important role in the supply of pig iron to Japan. The output of pig iron in North China may be estimated

estimated at a maximum of 150,000 tons a year. According to the Japanese press pig iron has been imported into Japan since the end of 1941, but the amount is still so small that it is far from being able to compensate for Japan's losses in connection with the cessation of the import of pig iron from other countries. (400,000 - 500,000 tons were imported annually from British India.)

By 1943 the Japanese hoped to produce in North China 800,000 tons of pig iron and 400,000 tons of steel, half of which was to be exported to Japan. It is quite evident that this plan has failed since the Japanese have not yet started the construction of the large plant, which they planned to erect in the neighbourhood of the Beipin-Tientsin railway.

Most of the metallurgical coke used in Japan was imported. Japan has hardly any good coking coal (Japanese coking coal is found only in Kuishu-Takasimi and Kagomati, and in Hokkaido; it is porous and friable). In 1936 Japan needed 2,300,000 tons of coke or 3,400,000 tons of coking coal. It takes at least 8,000,000 tons of coking coal to smelt 4,500,000 tons of pig iron. Coking coal is mined in the occupied areas of North China and to a small extent in Manchuria. Before the Sino-Japanese war Japan imported from Manchuria (Fushun and Bensihu) over 2,000,000 tons of coal for her metallurgical industry. Now that coal is used by the local metallurgical enterprises (Seva Seiko), and coking coal is even imported to Manchuria from North China, as the output of the South Manchurian coal regions (Fushun and Bensihu) is now insufficient, and the transportation of coking coal from the new deposits in Manchuria (Mishan and others) is very difficult. In the course of four years of occupation the output of coal in North and Central China has risen very little above the pre-war figure (in 1937 about 15,000,000 tons were mined in North China). In North China, where the Japanese concentrated on mining coke coal, they achieved better results than in Central China. The Hailan mines in Hupeh province, which continued to operate during the war and were, until December 1941, under the control of English capital, produced about half of the coal mined in North China in recent years. According to Japanese data their present annual output is 6,500,000 tons, of which one-third is exported to Japan. Japan's coal import from North China amounted to 2,400,000 tons in 1939 and 4,500,000 tons in 1941.

In the occupied areas of North China the Japanese concentrated on the development of the large deposits of coking coal in the coastal area and in particular of the Chunsin mines in Shantung province, which they succeeded in putting in operation only in 1940. Before 1937 these mines

mines produced over 1,000,000 tons of coking coal. According to the Japanese press about half of the coal at present mined is exported to Japan and about 15% is delivered to the Seiva Seiko plants in Manchuria.

Considerable importance was attached to the exploitation of the Tsinsin mines, in the neighbourhood of the Taiuan-Shintzenhuan railway, which are connected with Tsindao port and also with Tangu port (the latter by the Beipin-Tientsin railway). The metallurgical enterprises of Shitzinshan and Yantzuan work on Tsinsin coal.

The Japanese have also restored the comparatively large Datun coal mines in the northern part of Shansi province, where they mine about 1,000,000 tons of coal per year, of which about one-third is exported to Japan. They have also started mining lesser deposits in Hupen (Tisap), Shantung (Boshan, Tsichuan, and Davenko) and Hunan (Tzaotzo) provinces. It must be noted, however, that not all the coal mined in North China is fit for use in metallurgical plants.

Japan also gets anthracite and coking coal from French Indo-China, where the reserves amount to about 1,100,000,000 tons, but scale of mining is not large; the output amounted to about 2,500,000 tons per year, of which a considerable part was exported to Japan. Under war conditions the transportation of coal from Indo-China over a distance exceeding 1,500 miles is very difficult.

The exploitation of the iron ore resources of the countries of the South Seas is an important item in the Japanese plan of "development", oil and iron ore are the main objectives on which the Japanese military authorities now concentrate their attention.

However Japan meets here even greater difficulties than in China. In making their preparations for war on the Pacific the Japanese evacuated in good time the equipment as well as the Japanese workmen from the largest iron mines in British Malaya and in Dungun (Trenganu) where the concession was held by "Nihon Kogai" company. According to Japanese sources at the present time work at these mines has only been partially resumed. Second in size were the "Ilimedan" mines (Johore), which were worked by the "Isihara Sangai" company and had an output of 600,000 - 700,000 tons a year; these were completely destroyed by the British before their retreat and will be very difficult to restore.

According to Japanese sources mining has been partially restored in the Tamangan mines (Kelantan), formerly worked by the "Nane Kogai" company and the mines in Kemaman (Trenganu), which belonged to the "Isihara Sangai" company are in process of restoration, but the latter are very small and the annual output

output of ore mined in Kamaman before the war was only 100,000 - 200,000 tons. The restoration of iron ore mining in British Malaya to the pre-war level can therefore be achieved only after a considerable period of time.

The Japanese also propose to start the exploitation of the iron ore resources of the Philippines, where the annual output before the war was 1,300,000 tons of high-grade ore with a content of 61% metal. The ore was mined in the southeastern part of Luzon Island, in the province of Kamarines, on the islands of Samar, Marindo and others. The rich Surigao deposits on Mindanao Island, the reserves of which the Japanese estimate at 500,000,000 tons (54% metal content), were hardly mined at all. According to a statement published by the "Asahi" (May 10, 1942) the Japanese have commenced restoring the Kalnbayungan mines only (Kamarines province, Luzon Island). According to certain reports the American and Philippine troops continued for a long time to carry on partisan warfare which prevented restoration work.

The deposits of iron ore in the Netherlands East Indies, on the islands of Celebes and Sebuku, in the southeastern part of Borneo, and on other islands, were never worked. The long distances from the coast and the absence of means of transportation stand in the way of exploitation and of export of the ore. The iron ore resources of British Borneo are still more difficult to exploit and also the ore is not of a good quality. The output of iron ore in French Indo-China amounted to 72,000 tons only.

The Japanese metallurgical industry will unavoidably suffer serious difficulties also in the supply of iron and steel scrap. By constructing large blast furnaces and plants comprising the whole process of production Japan attempted to balance her steel and pig iron output and make herself less dependent on the import of scrap iron. A considerable increase of pig iron smelting might have reduced the demand for scrap iron for her steel industry. Japanese exports believed it technically possible that the expenditure of scrap iron for smelting steel could be reduced to 30%. But in 1940 50-70% of scrap iron was still used in the production of steel and the reduced consumption was only a matter of experiment. Japan imported over half of her supply of scrap metal, mainly from the United States (in 1940 Japan purchased in the United States about 1,000,000 tons of scrap iron).

The possibilities of using local scrap, and scrap which circulates in the steel smelting plants, are limited. In that respect internal resources were mobilized extensively in the very first years of the Chinese war. The internal resources of scrap metal in Japan before the war with China were estimated as amounting to 1,000,000 tons. At present they

they are much reduced. In an attempt to mobilize their metal resources the Japanese government ordered the removal of the metal parts of constructions in the system of electric and telephone wires, the cast-iron lids of sewage pipes, iron letter boxes, and even bicycle numbers. Now the campaign for collecting metal scrap is again in full swing. Wire, kitchen utensils, household articles, and pipes are collected, and railway cars are turned into scrap, but all these measures will yield very small results. The Japanese have also collected a "harvest" of metal scrap in the occupied areas of China. (In many factories they confiscated all the machines and equipment and transported them to Japan, where a special army department sold them to the Japanese capitalists; the Japanese looted all the shops and warehouses taking away all the metal to the last gram.) The Japanese are now making an attempt to get as much metal scrap as they can from the newly occupied countries; but the objectives for loot of metal are comparatively few, and this source of scrap will produce only a temporary alleviation.

As regards the reserves of iron and steel scrap which Japan has specially accumulated, they cannot be large since Japan is already forced to such extreme measures as to use the equipment of non-operating plants for scrap. The "Osaka Mainitski" (February 26, 1942) openly stated that unless extraordinary measures were taken Japan would most probably be short of iron for her war needs.

In the course of her preparations for war in the Pacific Japan adopted various measures with a view to increasing her metal resources. All these measures however are entirely inadequate as far as the solution of the problem of metal supply for the war industry on the basis of a long war on a large scale. Modern mechanized warfare consumes an enormous amount of metal and Japan with her limited reserves and comparatively weak metallurgical industry, will unavoidably meet with great difficulties.

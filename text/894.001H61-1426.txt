Mr. Kase wished to call to the Department's attention a cartoon labeled "Hot Mikado" appearing in the Chicago Daily News of January 29, 1940, and reproduced in the Washington Post of February 4, 1940, which the Japanese Embassy considered objectionable. January 30, 1940

Mr. Paul S. Mowrer, Editor
The Chicago Daily News
Chicago, Illinois

My dear Mr. Mowrer:

I regret to inform you that the cartoon published on the editorial page of your issue of January 29 has caused grief to me and to those of my countrymen who saw it.

I think I need scarcely point out to you that such satirical use of the likeness of the ruler of any country would be obnoxious to the people of that country. Particularly is this true in the case of the Japanese, who regard their ruler with especial reverence. This fact, I believe, is now quite well known outside of Japan, and I should thus expect that this sentiment might at least be understood and respected abroad.

I am chagrined, therefore, to find that such a leading newspaper as the Daily News should see fit to make such disrespectful use of the likeness of the ruler of a friendly nation, and I feel compelled to protest against it and to express the hope that the Daily News will avoid in the future any repetition of a publication of this nature.

Yours faithfully,

(Signed) HIROSI ACINO
Consul General of Japan

=== Page 11 === (confidence 0.99)
THE CHICAGO DAILY NEWS
400 West Madison Street
Chicago, Illinois

February 1, 1940

The Honorable Hirosi Acino
Consul General of Japan
1512 Tribune Tower
Chicago, Illinois

My dear Mr. Acino:

I acknowledge receipt of your letter of January 30, protesting against our cartoon of January 29 because it depicted the Emperor of Japan without especial reverence.

I am sorry this cartoon caused grief to you and to other Japanese. No offense was intended. Indeed, we had imagined that this good-humored cartoon was rather sympathetic to Japan in its various difficulties.

It is perhaps the nature of democracy to be somewhat irreverent. Our people do not regard their own rulers with reverence, and consequently it is hard for them to feel reverence for foreign rulers. We constantly publish cartoons of eminent and dignified heads of states, including our own. We have frequently cartooned Mr. Roosevelt, who is looked upon by many Americans with great respect, and Mr. Hitler, who is regarded by many Germans almost as a god.

What you are asking, therefore, is that we make a unique exception in the case of the Japanese emperor. In fairness to our readers, the vast majority of whom are Americans, unaccustomed to Japanese ways of thought, I cannot promise to do this.

However, in consideration of your personal courtesy, and of the peaceful relations at present existing between our two countries, I will be pleased to ask our cartoonists to refrain, in future, from depicting the Japanese Emperor in person, whenever they find that some other Japanese figure would serve their purpose as well.

Sincerely yours,

PAUL SCOTT MOWDER
EDITOR

DEPARTMENT OF STATE

January 30, 1941.

In regard to the form employed in addressing the Japanese Sovereign, Departmental practice has varied, the form "His Majesty Hirohito, Emperor of Japan" predominating in recent times although "His Majesty the Emperor of Japan" and "His Imperial Majesty the Emperor of Japan" have also been used. In addressing other sovereigns Departmental practice also appears to have varied. For example, the British Sovereign is addressed as "His Majesty, King George V, etc. etc." while the former ruler of Ethiopia was addressed as "His Imperial Majesty, Haile Selassie, Emperor of Ethiopia".

In matters of this kind the Department appears to be guided by the wishes of the forti court, or in the absence of such wishes, by general custom. In as much as the files of the Department fail to disclose anything definite as to the manner in which the Japanese Sovereign should be addressed, it is suggested that inquiry be made of the Embassy at Tokyo along lines of the attached draft instruction.

Sooner or later the Japanese would have to come to us to work out a permanent peace. The country would remain helpless in the meantime, and we would be masters of the situation. "Chaos" and revolution could go on for a protracted period without detriment to us. The time for us to extend the helping hand is as brothers after a permanent peace, not paternalistically during a period of occupation.

b. It is quite clear that there is no basis for the setting up of a republican form of government. It is equally clear that the main prerequisite for a constitutional monarchy is also absent. Constitutionalism is sine qua non of a republic or a constitutional monarchy, and Japan is a stranger to it. Neither a stable republic nor a stable constitutional monarchy can come about save by the gradual evolution of Japanese institutions. To assume that the existence of a monarchic institution of the Japanese type has already carried Japan well down the road toward constitutional monarchy, or given it a sort of headstart, or assurance of speedier progress, is wholly unwarranted.

c. It is equally unwarranted to assume that the institution of the Emperor is an equally apt instrument for good or evil. Neither history nor logic support such reasoning. In fact, the Emperorship gave little or no comfort to the brief liberal movement of the 1920's. On the contrary, it has always lent itself peculiarly to feudalism, dictatorship, and aggression--in varying degrees. The very existence of the institution, conceived as it is, is in derogation of all the fundamentals of freedom and democracy. That the British crown has evolved differently was in spite of the crown, not because of it.

Most of the arguments of Messrs. Grew, Ballantine and Pratt are based upon the impossible assumptions that we propose to destroy the institution of the Emperor during the period of occupation,

=== Page 30 === (confidence 0.99)
DEPARTMENT OF STATE
ASSISTANT SECRETARY
May 5, 1944
Mr. Berle

I commend to your attention the attached article, "Occupy Japan?", by Nathaniel Peffer. It does not detract from the cogency of his argument that our intentions are not precisely as he assumes.

He assumes, for example, that we intend to abolish the institution of the Emperor, and his argument against it is very good. It is the same argument that Mr. Grew and Mr. Ballantyne and Mr. Pratt have used. I do not quarrel with it, because it does not meet my point which is also Mr. Peffer's point, and, as I understand him, Mr. Dickover's, -- namely, that we should allow events to take their course after Japan has been thoroughly defeated. If Mr. Peffer knew that we intend to support the institution of the Emperor, instead of trying to destroy it, I have no doubt that he would consider it a futile and impossible procedure.

His argument against occupation is directed against any occupation of more than a few weeks. We undoubtedly propose much more, though less than he supposes.

The more I think of it, the more convinced I become that our proposed course will lead only to a humiliating involvement and disillusionment and reaction at home, with an ultimate loss of many of the fruits of victory. The issue is more than one of theory or even principle, it is one of the highest national interest. I cannot urge too strongly that the point of view represented by Mr. Peffer and Mr. Dickover should be given much more thorough consideration, and at higher levels, than it has yet received.

R. G. H. Jr.

ASSISTANT SECRETARY
April 22, 1944

Mr. Berle:

The attached, from the New Leader, April 22, is the best statement I have yet seen of the case for upsetting the institution of the Emperor in Japan. I think this is a matter of the utmost importance, and that it is not being sufficiently, if at all, considered. I believe the conclusion is inevitable from any proper understanding of the institution of the Emperor, -- on which institution there is no lack of evidence.

If you don't carry the ball on this in the Department, nobody else will; and the results, ultimately, will jeopardize the national security.

R. G. H. Jr.

USPOLAD reports the findings and recommendations made to the C of S Hq. SCAP, on the export of Japanese mine timbers to China, by the interested staff sections of this Hq. Included are copies of a letter to General Mueller, C of S. Hq, SCAP from General SHANG CHEN, Chief, Chinese Mission in Japan, and a Memorandum for the Record of July 12, 1948, outlining results of a conference on the export of mine timbers to China.

The Kailan Mining Administration estimates that it can not continue mining operations for more than two months beyond July 1, 1948 unless it obtains additional mining timbers. Since these mines are said to supply all of the coal utilized by railroads, ports, power plants, industries, etc. in Nationalist North China as well as 60 to 70% of the coal received in the Shanghai area, such a stoppage would have very serious political, economic, and military consequences. Kailan Mining Administration considers Japan the only practicable source of supply for these timbers. The time and cost factors make the US and Canada unlikely sources. The mining timbers must be delivered by December 1, 1948 before the harbor of Chingwantao freezes over. The quantity must be sufficient to meet operational requirements until mid-March 1949 when the port will reopen. Since China has no return cargo to offer ships carrying timber to China from the US and Canada costs would be high. Kailan Mining Administration doubts whether the Chinese Government would permit the expenditures of foreign exchange required for such a purchase. They also felt that political and financial reasons would permit purchasing the timber from potential sources in Manchuria, North Korea, and Sakhalin. In a conference on July 9, the Kailan Mining representative indicated that their organization would supply Japan with 100,000 tons of coking coal as a credit against their pit crop requirements of 250,000 toku (approximately 25,000,000 board feet) for the period, Dec. 1, 1948 through mid-March 1949.

In connection with the Kailan requirements, the Industry Division, ESS, Hq. SCAP, states that if the 250,000 koku of timbers were used in Japan 750,000 tons of coal could be produced, one koku of timber being used to mine three tons of coal.

JAPANESE MINING TIMBER FOR CHINA

Dear General Mueller:

Mr. Y.F. CHEN, Chief Secretary, and Mr. H. N. Faulkner, Sales Manager of the Kailan Mining Administration of China, have just arrived in Tokyo. Their special mission here is to appeal to the Supreme Commander for sanction in procuring Japanese mining timber for the KMA mines in Chinnungtao. In exchange therefor they propose to offer KMA coking coal which has been used in the iron and steel industries in Japan for the past 30 years. The following is a resume of the important points of the case as reported to me by the two KMA representatives.

The operation of the KMA coal mines in North China depends chiefly on mining timber. The supply of this critical material to China has had the cognizance of the Supreme Commander, and has constituted the first item in the China-Japan barter trade since the Japanese surrender. In 1947 approximately 5,000,000 pieces were shipped to China. For 1948, however, no further export of Japanese timber has been authorized. This will lead to a complete stoppage in the operation of the KMA mines.

The KMA is at present supplying fuel to the motive power of over 75% of China's public utilities, railways, coastal steamship services and industries. The U.S. Navy at Tsingtao also gets all its coal from the KMA. Should there be a serious interruption to the North China railways through lack of fuel, industries would have to be suspended and troop movements in the Communist Subversion Campaign would be dealt a severe blow.

In view of the seriousness of the situation which has gone beyond the scope of an ordinary commercial transaction, I shall deeply appreciate it if you will kindly bring the case to the

KAILAN MINING ADMINISTRATION, TINNTSIN, CHINA

July 2, 1948
Shiba Park Hotel, Tokyo

MEMORANDUM FOR: General Shang Chen

SUBJECT: The Barter of Mining Timber from Japan for The Knilan Special Slack (Coking Coal)

1. Since the Surrender and until the end of 1947 the Kailan Mining Administration had received their requirements of mining timber from Japan through the Central Trust which had a barter arrangement with SCAP. Under this arrangement, the K.M.A. paid cash direct to the Central Trust for the cost of the timber, and had no contact whatsoever with SCAP.

2. At the beginning of 1948 the Central Trust informed the K.M.A. that SCAP had demanded that future requirements of mining timber should be made against the barter of special slack as payment. To this the K.M.A. agreed and immediately accumulated and set in reserve at the loading port of Chinwangtae 10,000 tons of special slack specifically earmarked for the barter arrangement. Detailed specifications were submitted to SCAP through the Central Trust, and it was

hoped

hoped that the shipment of timber and coal could commence as soon as the Ice Season would finish, i.e. early March. However, up to the time of writing the Central Trust has been unable to complete the arrangement, and has, in fact, been told that Japan has no mining timber available for export to China.

3. Due to the failure to receive any mining timber during the first half of 1948, the K.M.A. has to resort to its emergency stocks (i.e. six months supply). The result is that the mines have only supply for two months as at July 1st.

4. Keilau coal has been used by the heavy industries of Japan for over 30 years. It is well known, and the continuity of supplies from the K.M.A. is earnestly desired by these industries.

5. If mining timber is not forthcoming from Japan, the K.M.A. will be forced to endeavor to obtain supplies from the U.S.A., or Canada at a prohibitive price. The reason is that at present China has no return cargo to offer the ships which will transport the timber from the Western Hemisphere, and K.M.A. will have to bear the cost of the return journey of these ships. This would be a very serious drain on China's limited foreign exchange, and we doubt whether the Chinese Government would agree to such an arrangement.

6. The K.M.A. is at present supplying fuel to the native power of over 75% of China's public utilities, railways, coastal steamship service, and industries. The power plants of Chinchou, Tungshan, Tientsin, Peiping, Tsingtao, Nanking and Shanghai reply solely on the K.M.A. for their fuel. The water works at these places are likewise dependent on K.M.A.

7. All the railways in North China are dependent on Keilau coal, and should the mines have to close for lack of mining timber it would be a most crippling blow to China's economy, and should there ever be a serious interruption to the railways through lack of fuel, industries will have to close, troop movements will greatly be impeded, and the Communist Suppression Movement in North China would come to a virtual standstill. Hence the situation in North China would be very grave indeed.

8. Tsingtao, which the U.S. Navy is using as the seat of the West Pacific Naval Command, gets all its coal from the Keilau mines. Light, water, industries and all the domestic requirements are met by the K.M.A. A stoppage of this supply of K.M.A. coal would therefore present a very serious problem to that port.

9. The K.M.A.'s normal consumption of mining timber to produce an output of 15,000/16,000 tons per day is approximately 420,000 Koku per year. To see us through the rest of this year and the Ice Season of 1949, we require a minimum of 200,000 koku. Some of which should be delivered as early as possible, and all of it must be delivered before the Ice Season at the end of November.

35. MR. CHEN: KMA bought machinery from Japan and also bought timber. During the last few years of the war I think Manchurian timber was not available.

36. MR. FAULKNER: I have never known in my 28 years, any timber to come to us from Manchuria or south Korea. I have never known a single pit prop to come from out of the whole of China.

37. LT COL DONALDSON: That answers my question. Col Marshall, do you have any question to ask regarding the timber?

41. MR WESTERBERGER: I figure it to be about 100,000 tons.

43. MR FAULKNER: Yes. The answer is in the affirmative.

46. MR CHEE: We hope that timber-carrying vessels coming from Japan to Tsingtao will be loaded to the fullest extent. On return we will always load as much coal as the ship's captain says he can safely take. 47. MR FAULKNER: That is what we mean.

48. MR CHEE: We are going later on to buy some other iron or steel products that Japan can offer. For anything we can buy in Japan we don't need to go to Europe. It is advantageous to us. But of course right now our most urgent item is timber.

49. LT COL MARSHALL: Are you figuring on furnishing the shipping?

50. MR CHEE: In the past year and the year before last, for all the ships that came to China, after discharge, we were charged with our currency according to price, including freight. KMA is not particularly interested who furnishes the shipping so long as we get the timber.

51. MR HO: I would like to add: if possible, some should be made available to Chinese ships, because if this shipping should be monopolized, it might cause a lot of trouble at home. I would like very much if it could be arranged between SCAP that this cargo be divided between the two. Otherwise, we have to ask the advice of the shipping people.

54. MR. CHEN: What is the opinion on SCAP's side? We would like to know.

59. MR. BAKER: I would like to have this clarified. It was my understanding that 5,000,000 tons of coal is mined in the particular area. And to mine 5,000,000 tons of coal you require 420,000 koku or 3,000,000 pieces of mine timbers? But, if you tell us you have nothing to spare, we are flying back as soon as possible and telling Hanking that we have to be prepared to close our mines. The Hanking Government will have to get busy to negotiate with Washington to get timber from America, but of course, practically speaking, and considering the time element, I know it is almost an impossibility.

67. MR. WYATT: If we make timber available, how much coal over and above the 100,000 tons would you be in a position to furnish Japan?

CHEN: Not this year - we are responsible to China for 100 percent - that is to north China from Peiping down, to the railways, industries, ships - and we are responsible for supply 60 to 70 per cent to Shanghai. What we can now barter to cover the timber is just what we can spare. Our Ministry of Industry will not permit us to export. We are not exporting to Hongkong, although in prewar times we always did. Last year we paid Central Trust in our currency, and they bought soy beans and sold to Japan. We have been informed this year for whatever props we might get we must offer our own merchandise.

69. LT COL MARSHALL: Has any attempt been made to secure mine props from Sakhalin?

70. MR. CHEN: There's not much there that I know of. We have nothing that we can ship out. Besides, we cannot sell coal to the Russians.

71. MR. FAULKNER: We have no contact in Sakhalin.

72. MR. CHEN: If we had it would be different. From Japan we know the freight will be balanced. Between Sakhalin - even assuming that we can get the credit or exchange to pay for the timber - the freight would be out of reason compared with here. To my mind the next only possibility would be timber from America.

73. LT COL MARSHALL: To summarize then, you as an individual company have no dollars with which to buy mine timbers. You can only trade your stock in trade, coal and the Chinese Government itself has not made available to you any funds for securing mine timbers?

74. MR. CHEN: We buy usually metals, steels from Europe and lubricants. This last half year we have not got a cent. The Chinese Government has permitted us to export this coal, offering it only to the Japanese iron and steel workers, but we have to pay for what we may be able to get.

75. MR. FAULKNER: The answer to the Colonel's question is that no foreign currency is made available.

76. LT COL MARSHALL: In a previous discussion with the Chinese Government we pointed out that we were very much interested in pig iron and iron ore rather than coal. Have you, Mr. Ho, from the Chinese Government side anything to on that to offer.

77. MR. HO: We have not been able to find out as yet in what way we can get any of those things. Pig iron and iron ore are under the control of Natural Resources, a different Government unit. Naturally, you have to make petition to the Central Government to requisition those things. In such case the KMA would have to do the negotiations, involving Central Trust.

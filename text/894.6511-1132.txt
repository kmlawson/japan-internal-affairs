Zino Produotion in Japan
Zinc smelting in Japan has not reached substantial
proportions.
Tables are appended furnishing data upon
the production of zinc are and zinc in Japan for the
years 1931, 1932, and 1933, the latter being the last
year upon which data are available.
In 1933 the total
amount of inc produced in this country was 30,658
metric tons, which was derived from 32,469 metric tons
of zinc ore produced in the Japanese Empire and 37,806
metric tons of zino ore imported from abroad. of the
total amount of zino consumed in Japan, over half is
imported, reaching in 1933 the amount of 32,526 metric
tons.
Number and Location of Zinc Smelters
There are four zinc smelters in Japan, of which
the smelter operated by the Mitsui Mining Company, Ltd.
at Omuta, Fukuoka-Ken, produced about two-thirds of
the total amount smelted in Japan or 21,289 metric
tons.
This smelter is located on the seacoast of
Fukuoka-Ken and no problem arises as to the escape of
gas from its smelter stacks to the detriment of forests
and nearby arable land.

Methods Used in Zinc Smelting Plants to Control
the Escape of Sulphuric Gas
All smelters in Japan have modern equipment and
employ modern smelting processes, either the so-called
wet or dry prooess.
Sulphurous acid discharged
from stacks in the form of gas is partially caught
and used in the manufacture of sulphuric acid al though
some gas is allowed to escape.
The exact nature cf
the processes used in particular instance is unobtain-
able as constituting a military secret.
The Consul-
ate General has, however, teen informed by an engineer
in Japan, not a Japanese subject, who is interested
financially in one of the smelting plants, that the
process generally used for the control of sulphur gas
is known as the Cotrell Process.
Methods Followed for Indemnification
of Owners of Property Damaged by
the Escape of Sulphurous Gas
As far as can be learned from responsible Japan-
ese Government Officials and other persons interested
in the zinc smelting business, no serious indemnifica-
tion problem has yet arisen in Jepan.
This is prob-
ably due to the fact that the largest zinc smelting
plant is located on the seacoast where fumes will not
cause injury to forests or orops and that in the case
of the other three plants most of the sulphurous gas
is caught and used in the production of sulphurio
aoid. In the event that trouble should arise botween
the managoment of zinc smelting plants and surrounding
property owmers, the first step in the solution of

is the smallest zinc smelter in Japan.

Dear Senator Donahey -
I am not writing this letter as the speaker of any
group such as the chamber of commerce, Board of trade or
any other organization except the members of the group
who are directly affected, namely the employees of the
United Engineering and Foundry Company.
I am thirty three years of age and a mechinist of
said Company. At the present time there are approximately
one hundred and fifty employees performing service here.
Many of my fellow workers do not know I am writing this
letter. Some of them do know and approve of it. I am
taking this responsibility upon myself because of the ap-
parent inaction of the men themselves and because I feel
it the duty of some one to bring this situation to the
attention of men in a position to do something about it,
if anything can be done.
Two days ago we received notice from officials of
the company, whose headquarters are located at Pittsburg,
that the branch factory they operate here will be mov ed
to Japan, lock, stock and barrel. The machines themselves
which are used to manufacture the United product, rolling
mill machinery, will be taken down and shipped to Japan
and be put in operation by Japanese labor. They give as
their reason that it is necessary to do this to meet com-
petition. If this be true it is the poorest possible way
out, looking at it from the Americn standard of view.
They know it is possible to obtain skilled labor in Japan
at one-tenth the cost of what we are being paid. Add to
that the fact that Japan is a member f the Rome-Berlin
Axis and the possibility in case of war of their using the
very product manufactured by these machines as weapons f
war against America. The officials make the statement
that these machines will not be used to manufacture war
mterials, but anyone with exen a limited imagination
knows

in Japau.

fifty employees and the business progress of our own
little community here, the principle of the thing is of
far greater significance.
If this practice is allowed to continue on a larger
scale how can we ever hope to reduce unemployment? Es-
pecially since it is apparently being done to satisfy the
greed for money of some few big industrial executives. I
feel sure it isnt being done with your approval nor the
approval of our President, Franklin D Roosevelt.
I do not have the feeling of concern for myself as
I do for the men employed in this plant who are fifty or

The removal of this plant from Wooster is also taking

(Editor's Note: Mr. Terry has just returned from his work with the Christian Lit-
erature Society in China. Recent trips, just before his return to the United
States, include visits to Kunning, Chungking, Chengtu, North China and Manchuria.
It is not difficult to understand Mr. Terry's feeling about the incident he de-

Wooster, Ohio, September 28, 1939.
Last evening a man called on me und introduced himself as Mr. X. He is about
to go to Japan, his first experiunce in travelling outside the United States, and
wanted advice about travel - did he reed a tuxedo on shipboard, in what form should
he carry his money, etc.
The factory in which he has been employed has been sold, bag and baggage, to
Japan, and he and three others are going there for one year to set it up and get
it runming, and to train the Jepanese to operate it. It took llo railway cars to
ship it from Wooster. it is a rolling mill machinery factory - that is, it is
equipped to manufacture the rolling mills in which steul and other metal products
are manufactured. It is a ready-made and complete instrument for multiplying in
Japan the factories in which the implenents of war can be fabricated from raw ma-
terials.
America, through such sales, is fortifying Japan against the day when there
may be an embargo on American goods to Japan. We have notified Japan that our
trade treaty of 19ll with her will expire in January. If further action toward
limiting war trade with Japan is talken after that America will take some smug sat-
isfaction from the fact that her fuctories are no longer supplying war materials
to the barbaric military of Japan. And America will forget that she has used these
six months to transfer to Japan the factories and the supor-factories which will
enable Japan to carry on her war of aggression without our help.
The Middle-West, more than any other part of America that I have seen, feels
remote from war, thinks of keeping herself out of war, and wishes her trade and
prosperity to continue undisturbed by war. Yet for a price in gold the Middle-West
is willing to build a war machine for a nation which will use it immediately and
directly to destroy in China those elements of peace and prosperity which the Amer-
ican people think to be of so great importance to thenselves.
Vooster is a happy little town of 8,Oo0, and I am familiar with no town more
beautiful anywhere in the world. It is in the second richest agricultural county
in the United States, I understand. The problems and sufferings which I know so
well in China are remote indeed from Wooster's people. Further, it is one of the
great church and missionary centers of the country, and students in its college are
being trained to carry the gospel of service and sacrifice and love to all parts of

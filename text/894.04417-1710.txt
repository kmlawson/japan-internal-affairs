# THE SECRETARY OF THE INTERIOR
## WASHINGTON

My dear Mr. Secretary:

Articles 81 through 87 of the Criminal Code of Japan (1923), copies of which are enclosed, provide that any Japanese national who assists an enemy in a war against Japan commits an offense punishable by death or imprisonment. So far as we can ascertain, these laws are still in effect. The provisions are very broad, and appear to be applicable to Japanese nationals wherever they may be.

There are many Japanese aliens in this country who aided the United States in the prosecution of the war against Japan. Some were accepted for military service. Others were employed as civilians in the Army Map Service, the Office of Strategic Services, and in War Department Ordnance depots, as well as in other Federal war agencies. Some taught the Japanese language in Army- or Navy-sponsored language schools for military or naval personnel. Many were employed in the maintenance of vital western railroad trunk lines, and in the harvesting of essential agricultural crops. Others found jobs in war industries.

Because of the racial restrictions upon naturalization, Japanese aliens (except for the few in the Armed Forces) cannot become American citizens and thus receive the protection of the United States Government against punitive action by Japan. Some of those who participated in our war program may be employed in the occupation of Japan or may wish at some future time to visit Japan for personal reasons. It seems to me highly desirable that arrangements be made to render Japanese aliens who are resident in this country immune to punishment by Japanese authorities for assistance that they may have given the United States during the war.

A similar letter is being addressed to the Secretary of War.

Sincerely yours,

Harold L. Gellman
Secretary of the Interior.

ARTICLES 81 to 87, CRIMINAL CODE OF JAPAN (1923)

Sec. 81. Every person who, by conspiring with a foreign power, has caused hostilities to commence against the Empire or who has joined an enemy power in taking action against the Empire shall be condemned to death.

Sec. 82. Every person who has delivered to an enemy power a fortress, camp, body of troops, or other place for military or naval use shall be condemned to death.

Every person who has delivered to an enemy power ammunition or other goods for military or naval use shall be punished with penal servitude.

Sec. 83. Every person who, for purposes of benefiting an enemy power, has damaged or rendered unfit for use a fortress, camp, vessel, arms, ammunition, train, railroad, telegraph, or other place or thing for military or naval use shall be condemned to death or punished with penal servitude for life.

Sec. 84. Every person who has delivered to an enemy power arms, ammunition or other thing which, though not in military use of the Empire, may be used in operations of war shall be punished with penal servitude for life or not less than three years.

Sec. 85. Every person who has acted as a spy for an enemy power or who has aided a spy shall be condemned to death or punished with penal servitude for life or not less than five years.

Sec. 86. Every person who, by methods other than those of the preceding articles, has given an enemy power any advantage or who has injured the interests of the Empire shall be punished with limited penal servitude for not less than two years.

Sec. 87. Any attempts of the crimes of the preceding articles shall be punished.

DEPARTMENT OF STATE
THE LEGAL ADVISER

JA - Mr. W. T. Turner:

Reference is made to your memorandum of February 13, 1946 and the underlying letter of February 6, 1946 from Secretary of the Interior Ickes, in which he suggests that arrangements should be made so that Japanese aliens resident in the United States be given immunity from punishment by the Japanese authorities for assistance which they may have given to the United States during the war. Mr. Ickes calls attention to provisions of the Japanese Criminal Code which might apparently subject such persons to suffer criminal penalties. You state that you admit the desirability of Mr. Ickes' proposal, but doubt its feasibility.

Although it is true that all states assume the right to punish traitors, I suppose there is nothing to prevent our including in the peace settlements an appropriate amnesty provision. Through the control exercised by General MacArthur we might also require that the Japanese Government give a complete and free pardon and amnesty to such persons. Under the circumstances of the war and the unconditional surrender, I believe there would be no legal impropriety in demanding an amnesty. On the other hand, I have no idea whether such a provision would have any practical effect in case the persons concerned should go back to Japan or otherwise place themselves in a position where the Japanese might take effective action against them. At any rate, this Government might then have done what it could to prevent the Japanese authorities from punishing those Japanese who have helped us.

My dear Mr. Secretary:

I refer to a letter of February 6, 1946 addressed
to me by Mr. Ickes in which he urged that arrangements
be made to render Japanese aliens resident in this
country immune to punishment by Japanese authorities
for assistance that they may have given the United
States during the war.

There is no question about the desirability of
protecting these people, many of whom have rendered
invaluable service to this country, from punishment
under the laws of their own land. We are in a position
to prevent the application of those laws so long as
our forces occupy Japan. Furthermore, under the cir-
cumstances of the Japanese surrender, there would
appear to be no legal impropriety in requiring the
Japanese Government, at the time of the peace settle-
ment, to give a complete and free pardon and amnesty
to Japanese subjects who aided this country during
the war.

On the other hand, it is questionable whether,
after the Japanese Government has regained its full
freedom of action, a provision such as that described
would have any practical effect in case the persons
concerned should return to Japan or otherwise place
themselves in a position where the Japanese Government
could take effective action against them. All coun-
tries regard the right to protect the State against
traitors

A-119, April 4, 1949

Reference airgram A-111, March 29, 1949, reporting the initiation of trials of persons accused of being pro-Japanese, and reference airgram A-98, March 21, 1949, listing persons arrested prior to March 21st for investigation under the National Traitors Act, there are listed below names of additional persons who have been reported arrested or questioned by the Special Investigation Board or its branches through March 31st.

It will be noted that no information is available either in the press or at the headquarters of the Special Investigation Board for ten of the persons below-named. These individuals have been arrested by provincial boards which have not yet rendered complete reports to Seoul. It should also be recalled that not all of the persons involved will necessarily be brought to trial since there have already been instances of suspects released after questioning. Although there has been a marked acceleration in the number of recent arrests, there is still no indication that the end of the purge is in sight, nor that the board has yet established any criteria which will show how extended the period of arrests and trials will be.

Further information will be submitted when available.

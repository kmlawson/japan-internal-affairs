# KOREAN IMMIGRATION INTO JAPAN.

From the earliest days of recorded history Korean travellers, scholars and immigrants have crossed the narrow straits to Japan. During the early years of the Christian Era, when Korean civilization was recognized by Japanese to be superior to their own, priests, artisans and savants from the peninsula were welcomed by the Japanese and given lands and honor. Many of the early Japanese families or clans as well as families of the nobility traced their descent from Korean ancestors.

During the latter days of the Korean kingdom, when after centuries of invasion and domestic misrule, civilization in the peninsula was at a low ebb and when there was little security of life or livelihood, the number of immigrants, political refugees and students crossing to Japan began to increase, until after the annexation of Korea by Japan with the resultant improvement of facilities of transportation the influx began to take on the aspects of a migration. With the outbreak of the Great War, Japan's industries were suddenly given a tremendous impetus and the demand for labor reached unprecedented proportions. All available native labor was employed in the countless new enterprises, and to meet the demand for further and unskilled labor thousands of Koreans crossed the channel to seek their fortunes.

This movement continued until 1925, when during the wave of hysteria following the earthquake disaster several hundred Koreans suspected of pillaging the prostrate cities were massacred in the Tokyo-Yokohama district.

district. Naturally, reports of these atrocities acted to deter immigration from Korea, and, coupled with certain restrictive measures adopted by the authorities to prevent indiscriminate immigration into Japan, resulted in the figures for entries from Korea during 1923 and 1924 showing a decided decrease from the preceding years, whereas departures from Japan to Korea showed a marked increase.

Later, after conditions had begun to return to normal in the Tokyo-Yokohama district and when the restrictions on immigration had been removed in 1924, there recommenced a great movement of Korean laborers toward Japan, attracted by the demand for labor in the districts undergoing reconstruction. In 1925, in consequence of the economic depression of the country and the resultant unemployment, the authorities decided to adopt a form of limitation in order to prevent indiscriminate immigration. In the few years previous to this time flood and drought in Korea had reduced certain districts to the verge of starvation, while at the same time the unsettled conditions in China had resulted in a large number of thrifty Chinese laborers immigrating into Korea and displacing the native laborers, many of whom were forced to emigrate to Manchuria and Siberia. Japan offered the most promising field for employment to these destitute peasants, and no measures short of exclusion which the authorities hesitate to adopt, could prevent their admission.

Again, during 1928 and 1929, the southern and most thickly populated provinces of Korea suffered a severe drought resulting in a further wave of emigration to Japan

than the number departing. At the end of June, 1928, the number of Koreans living in Japan was estimated to be: Males, 168,410; Females, 45,217; Total, 213,627. Districts in which Korean immigrants have chiefly settled are Osaka city and environs, 45,133; Fukuoka prefecture, 18,185; Tokyo city and environs, 17,334; Aichi prefecture (Nagoya district), 14,659; Kyoto city and environs, 13,157; Hyogo prefecture (Kobe district), 12,368; Yamaguchi prefecture, 8,289; Hokkaido, 8,125; Kanagawa prefecture (Yokohama district), 7,570; etc. It may be noted that these immigrants have settled for the most part in the crowded industrial districts, much as have immigrants from certain parts of Europe settled in the industrial cities of America. In the cities and towns of Japan where Koreans have settled in numbers, a Korean village or community encysted within the Japanese community is a common sight. Throughout the Empire, on construction projects, at wharfs, in mines, on roads, or in trains and boats, Korean laborers may be observed.

The social and economic problems resulting from the influx of a large foreign element, preponderantly male, which is largely ignorant, often unruly, and of a very low standard of living, has given much cause for concern to Japanese authorities. Crowded as is Japan and afflicted with a serious unemployment problem, the yearly addition of thousands of foreign laborers is hardly calculated to ease the situation. Very few of these laborers immigrate to Japan in response to the solicitations of Japanese industrialists, nor have they any definite

=== Page 6 === (confidence 1.00)
-5-

definite prospect of employment. Of extremely low standards of culture, practically none become "white-collar" workers, the great majority remaining unskilled or "raw" laborers. Even if qualified to take more skilled tasks the way is not easy because of the opposition of native Japanese labor. Domestic labor unions, harassed as they are by unemployment and lately by competition from the Koreans with their low standards of living and wages, are actively agitating against further admission of immigrants.

Generally speaking, the Korean immigrant laborer is regarded by Japanese as churlish, lacking in perseverance, and disinclined to settle down in one place. Doubtless some of these characteristics result from treatment by Japanese employees, but their lack of responsibility is a serious fault which they bring with them from their native country. Since the majority are day laborers, they lose their jobs on the completion of the work, and with the retrenchment in all undertakings resulting from the present depression the unemployment among these laborers is said to be very severe throughout the country. With no families to fall back on, the condition of these transient laborers is precarious and robberies and other crimes, as well as various instances of disturbances of peace and public morals, are extremely common among them.

In contrast to the gracious treatment accorded the early immigrants from Korea, who were largely of the literati class, Korean immigrant laborers are heartily despised by the modern Japanese. Koreans are discriminated against in many ways, and the word "Korean" is an opprobrious

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
-6-

ous epithet at which any Japanese schoolboy is willing to fight. Wages paid Korean laborers are usually about twenty per cent less than those paid Japanese laborers performing the same task, although this disparity in wage can hardly be accounted for by difference in efficiency. Newspapers are quick to fasten the blame for disturbances and crimes on Koreans. Mention has been made of the extraordinary flare-up of racial animosity following the great earthquake, in which hundreds of innocent Koreans perished.

Although there is no marked difference in racial physical characteristics between Japanese and Koreans, there are very few instances of interracial marriages. According to statistics prepared by Eisuke Zensei published in the "Diplomatic Review" for March 15, 1930, there have been only about five hundred instances of common law interracial unions in Japan, and the number of registered marriages is negligible. Whether because of race prejudice or because of difference in standards of living, the Koreans in Japan remain a separate and distinct people.

In spite of the agitation for exclusion of Korean laborers by Japanese labor, the Japanese authorities hesitate to take so drastic a step as that of limiting movement of Japanese subjects within the Empire. The problem is much the same as that of Filipinos in the western States of the Union. Many Japanese believe that the solution of the problem lies in the economic development of Korea itself. To quote from an article by Mr. Zensei in the "Diplomatic Review" for March 15, 1930, "From the standpoint of the spirit of the Korean annexation, subjects of the Empire should be at liberty to travel

=== Page 8 === (confidence 1.00)
-7-

travel and settle at will within the limits of the Empire, and to live unmolested wherever they wish. The proposition to restrain this liberty is one which requires careful study. *** The best way of succoring and leading these unfortunate people is not by enacting strict exclusive measures, but by providing suitable employment for them in Korea. *** Trying to prevent Koreans from going abroad to seek economic deliverance is like trying to stop water from flowing down hill. Making a problem of Korean immigration into Japan without undertaking to remedy economic conditions within the peninsula is confounding cause and effect. There are many conditions which need to be changed in order to provide certainty of livelihood to these unfortunate people who, when they immigrate to Manchuria, are oppressed by Chinese officials and who, when they immigrate to Japan, are regarded as a nuisance. Universal education and self-government are of course splendid things, but rather than expand an educational system which is hardly practical in view of the primitive conditions of the people and rather than institute political autonomy which cannot be cooked or eaten, is it not far more necessary for the twenty millions of Korea that they be given work and that their standards of living be raised to a decent level?"

# Korean Emigration to Japan

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

I have the honor to enclose summarized translations of certain articles which recently appeared in the local Korean press in regard to alleged restrictions on Korean emigration to Japan.

An effort was made to ascertain the number of Koreans at present residing in Japan Proper, but no recent information on the subject was found in available official or private statistical works. One Korean newspaper* puts the number of Koreans residing in the whole of Japan at 700,000, while another** states that 400,000 Koreans are to be found in Tokyo and Osaka alone. In any event, as there were only 40,000 Koreans in Japan in 1920 according to the national census of that year, it is evident that in the ensuing years there was a large migration of Koreans to Japan.

The movement of Koreans to Japan appears to have gained momentum with the demand for heavy unskilled labor, which the Koreans perform well and cheaply, for the reconstruction of Tokyo and the modernization of cities in Japan. The slackening of construction activity following the completion of the main programmes for civic rebuilding reduced the demand for this class of labor, and gradually unemployment set in among those engaged in it. Having no homes or relatives in Japan, the groups of homeless and unemployed Koreans gave rise to social problems of various kinds* which were aggravated by the arrival of fresh immigrants from Korea. Moreover, as the wage scale of Korean laborers is lower than that of Japanese laborers, and as unemployment, ambition and other causes were diverting them into new fields of labor, Korean laborers began to constitute a menace to Japanese labor.

In theory Koreans have full freedom to travel to and settle in Japan Proper, and it would appear that until about 1930 they were allowed and made use of this freedom. As their increasing numbers, however, threatened to cause social and economic difficulties in Japan, measures had to be taken to end their large-scale immigration. For obvious political reasons legislation or government regulations to restrict the movement of Koreans within the Empire could not be passed, and accordingly administrative practice was resorted to in order to meet the situation. consists of requiring every Korean desirous of proceeding to Japan to obtain from the police headquarters of his district a "toko shomeisho" (sailing certificate).

It is understood that "toko shomeisho" have been increasingly hard to obtain. A laborer is required to furnish proof that he has adequate means of his own or relatives in Japan with means who will take care of him or a contract of employment in Japan. Briefly, a Korean applicant for a "toko shomeisho" must satisfy the police authorities that he will not become a public charge.

The enclosed articles refer to alleged conferences between the central and Chosen authorities looking to virtual exclusion from Japan of new Korean laborers and deportation to Chosen of unemployed Koreans in Japan. References are also seen from time to time in the Korean press to efforts being made by the Government General to shift surplus labor from the southern to the northern provinces of Korea, where extensive harbor, railroad, and hydroelectric projects are under execution, and to a plan to create in the Government General a labor bureau to aid in the proper distribution of labor within the peninsula. In the past few days too, local newspapers reporting Governor General Ugaki's visit to Tokyo have stated that he conferred with the central authorities regarding the enforcement of plans to settle Koreans in Manchuria*. The heavy migration of Koreans to Manchuria, until the Japanese occupation at least, and to Japan in the past two decades is indicative of the unattractive conditions of life that have prevailed in their own country in this period. Chief among the causes of emigration has been the hopeless outlook of a vast portion of the population. Thus farmers who work their own lands constitute 43.6% of the farming population of Korea, while those who labor for others occupy 56.4%. Of the valuable paddy-fields, however, providing the cash for necessities, comfort and finer food -- generally speaking, Korean farmers raise rice for sale, eating millet and coarser grains themselves, -- 67.4% is owned by persons who do not work them. The phenomenon of the land situation in Korea is that year by year farm land has passed from the hands of the farmers to those of absentee landlords,

banks, loan associations, and usurers*. It is known that 80% of the farming population of Korea owns no land whatever.

Various explanations are given of the increase in the tenant-farmer population, such as shiftlessness, operations of Japanese land banks and colonization companies, et cetera, but the most obvious explanation is that the Korean farmer has always been so near the verge of poverty that the slightest unfavorable factor in his economy, such as higher cost of commodities or lower price of rice, has compelled him to mortgage or sell his land or, if a tenant, to hypothecate his share of the next harvest in order to obtain funds for his needs. With no surplus income with which to repay indebtedness or even interest, the burden of debt and consequent loss of land has steadily grown, and it is said that there are large number of tenants whose share in the crop is now worth less than the interest on their borrowings. With such a blank prospect in agriculture and with no opportunity for employment in the insignificant other industries of their country, it is only natural that the vast homeless and landless population of Korea should seek a happier state in other lands. The recently enacted Chosen Tenant Law** provides an opportunity to tenant farmers to rehabilitate themselves and will no doubt operate against the gravitation of Koreans toward Japan and Manchuria.

A discussion of Korean immigration into Japan at once turns one's thoughts to the population problem of Japan. There are in Korea 523,000 Japanese, of whom perhaps as many as half were born in Korea. On the other hand there are in Japan between 400,000 and 700,000 Koreans. As far as easing the pressure of population is concerned, therefore, it would seem that if anything Korea has been the gainer from annexation by Japan.

Enclosure No. 1 to Despatch No. 22 from Wm. R. Langdon, American Consul at Seoul, Chosen, dated June 8, 1934, on the subject "Korean Emigration to Japan".

SOURCE: DONG-A ILBO, Korean language daily in Seoul, May 22, 1934.

SUMMARY TRANSLATION

This is not the place to discuss the cause of the decline in the rural population, whether it is ignorance, improvidence, or absorption of lands and industries by capitalists. Be it what it may the fact remains that the ever increasing majority of the population is wandering in the borderland of starvation. These people own not an inch of land, and being unable to find employment in industry for lack of means or skill, they must sell their labor where they can. To do this they trek to Manchuria or cross over to Japan.

Employment is easier to find in Japan than in Manchuria, and in spite of the requirement to obtain a "Toko Shomeisho" (sailing certificate), the issuance of which is very limited and without which no Korean laborer is permitted to embark for Japan, Korean laborers in Japan now number 400,000. The wages of Korean laborers are much lower than those of Japanese and Korean laborers may therefore be a menace to Japanese laborers and give rise to troubles in Japan. However, Korean laborers in Japan are not concentrated in groups, but are scattered in cities all over the country in such a manner that they do not seriously affect the employment situation in Japan, and get on peacefully with Japanese laborers. In spite of this state of affairs, the colonial and home ministers under various pretexts and with the intention of stopping the entry of Korean laborers into Japan are making the "Toko shomeisho" system more rigid...

It is reported that the Home Office as a measure strictly to control Korean laborers from entering Japan is now conferring with the Government General regarding the advisability of (a) absolutely prohibiting Korean laborers from crossing over to Japan, and (b) returning to Chosen all Koreans who have already unlawfully (sic) crossed to Japan ...

Korean labor in Japan proper has become an important social as well as political issue in the metropolis, this being mainly attributed to the marked increase in recent years to the number of Koreans crossing aimlessly to Japan. How much importance intelligent persons in the metropolis are attaching to this issue is evident by the statement recently made in the Lower House by a member that even though Chosen should be granted autonomy (sic) Koreans should be absolutely prohibited from entering Japan.

From this point of view it is quite incomprehensible to us why Koreans alone should be prevented from crossing to Japan. It is said that the Home authorities are insisting on returning to Chosen all unemployed Koreans now in Japan. Should they do so for the reason that they are unemployed we are of the opinion that such step is illogical and unfair to fellow subjects ...

Enclosure No. 4 to Despatch No. 22 from Wm. R. Langdon, American Consul at Seoul, Chosen, dated June 8, 1934 on the subject "Korean Emigration to Japan".

SOURCE: CHOSEN ILBO, a Korean Language daily in Seoul, May 29, 1934

SUMMARY TRANSLATION

... So far as we have learned of the police conference recently held at the Home Office, Tokyo, in which police authorities of Chosen participated, the following matters were agreed upon:

Control of Korean laborers: The invasion of Korean laborers has been a troublesome question for a long time. As this invasion tends to grow larger and as it is increasingly hard for Korean laborers in Japan to make a living, stricter control must be exercised over those coming to Japan without definite aim, or secretly.

Relief of Koreans in Japan: About 700,000 Koreans are to be found in Japan. Unemployment questions in the large cities invariably involve Koreans, and some relief must be afforded them. To prevent crime among these people, arrangement to introduce them to employment should be made, while opium addicts among them should be treated and controlled.

Control of Korean Socialists: Koreans involved in communist cases in Japan are on the increase. In every case Koreans are among those arrested ...

Prevention of entry from foreign lands of Korean socialists: Korean socialists in Shanghai, Peiping, and Tientsin are attempting secret entry into Japan ... Arrangements should be made to prevent their entry and efforts should be made to arrest them (abroad) and bring them home for trial.

The Problem of Korean Laborers in Japan

The number of Korean laborers coming annually to Japan is considerable, resulting in an annual increase of 20,000 to 50,000 Koreans in Japan. The percentage of increase, moreover, has been becoming larger every year, as may be seen from the figures below, covering the years 1929-1933 inclusive.

This influx of Korean laborers into Japan proper, occurring just at the time of the economic depression, resulted in added unemployment and much distress to domestic

domestic labor. From early days Korean laborers with any special ability have been rare, the majority being coolies and day-laborers engaged in unskilled labor. Because of the low wages paid them and their physical endurance they have come not only to be used exclusively in work calling for unskilled labor, but recently they have begun to be employed as skilled laborers also, thus making the situation difficult for domestic laborers, until at present there is a tendency for Japanese laborers to be excluded altogether.

In spite of the actual conditions regarding Korean and Japanese laborers, the Koreans have from the beginning been treated as Japanese with no political or legal discrimination, owing to the annexation by Japan of Korea, which was regarded as an extension of Japan proper instead of as a colony. From the point of view of the Japanese, the problem of equal treatment for Koreans is one causing great anxiety. Other countries which have been faced with the same problem are said to have limited the immigration of colonists into the mother country. Up to the present Japan has made no restrictions with a view of controlling the entrance of Korean laborers, and there is no intention of doing so in the future.

Another aspect of the Korean problem is the question of protection for those who drift to Japan without any definite plan. Recently there have come to Japan numbers of Koreans who have had no knowledge of the increasingly severe business depression in Japan which caused

These people come seeking employment in Japan with no relatives or acquaintances whom they could approach. Aid must be given to such people, some of whose needs are particularly urgent. Among those who have come to Japan have been some who, finding themselves in great distress at not finding employment, have wandered from place to place leading a miserable life. The Chosen Government General has adopted the practice of urging those who can be seen to belong to this class to reconsider, before leaving Korea, their intention of coming to Japan without a definite plan. This friendly gesture of protection has been misunderstood in Korea and interpreted as an attempt to restrict the immigration of Korean laborers into Japan.

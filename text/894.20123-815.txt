# Confidential

"Three Peruvian Army officers arrived today at San-Pedro aboard the Japanese steamer BOKUYO MARU from Valparaiso enroute to Japan to witness annual fall maneuvers, so they say, of the Japanese Army. They are: Lieut. Colonel Felipe de la Barra, Infantry, Chief of Mission; Captain Juan Medoza A., Artillery; Captain Enrique Indacochea G., Artillery.

"They will go to San Francisco by rail tomorrow and depart that port for Yokohama on the 6th inst. aboard The Nippon Yusen Kaisha Liner ASAMA MARU. Colonel de la Barra, very chary about giving out information, says that they expect to be in Japan about three months.

"From another BOKUYO MARU passenger it was learned that these three officers comprise the second military mission sent to Japan since the present revolutionary government took control. This informant is E. Espantoso, former Peruvian consul at Seattle and now enroute back to Seattle to become Argentine consul there. Senor Espantoso declares that primary objective of the present mission will be the purchase in Japan of munitions and other war materials. Senor Espantoso desires that he be not disclosed as informant."

(f) Relations with Peru.

Early in October three Peruvian army officers arrived in Japan on an "official mission". The purpose of their visit has not been divulged in Tokyo but it is presumed that it is in connection with the contract between the Peruvian Government and the Japanese Government for an exchange of armaments for guano. The existence of this contract has been kept very secret in Japan and the Embassy has been unable to obtain any information here concerning it. It may possibly explain some of the activity which has been noticed lately in the armament industry in this country, especially as there is the implication that Japan has been trying to take advantage of the fall in the yen to sell armaments abroad. Here may be contracts with other countries of which we know nothing now.

Sir:

I wish to refer to the Department's instructions No. 119, of October 24, 1932, and No. 127, of November 7, 1932 (File Nos. 594.20123/1 and 721. 23, respectively), regarding the objects of the Peruvian Military Mission sent to Japan in September last. It appears from information which has reached the American Embassy in Lima that the Mission is visiting Japan for the purpose of negotiating an agreement for obtaining arms and ammunition

=== Page 13 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

ammunition in exchange for Peruvian guano and petroleum.

Members of my staff have been in touch both with members of the Mission and with Japanese officials conversant with the details of the negotiations and were informed as follows:

The purpose of the Peruvian Military Mission is not to purchase military equipment at the present time, but only to examine such equipment as the Japanese War Office is able to supply, ascertain the quality and price, and report its findings, with such recommendations as it may care to make, to the Peruvian Government. The Peruvian Government has already obtained prices on British and French military equipment and, according to members of the Mission, desires prices on military equipment of American manufacture. The Mission is dealing only with Japanese Government officials and not with individual manufacturers. The equipment desired is stated to be:

75 mm. field guns and ammunition therefor.
75 mm. mountain guns and ammunition therefor.
Light machine guns and ammunition therefor.
Heavy machine guns and ammunition therefor.
Military airplanes of all types.
Aircraft bombs of 25 and 50 lbs. weight.

The Mission has already inspected the equipment of these categories which the Japanese War Office is offering, but prices are not yet available.

The proposal to exchange Peruvian guano for Japanese arms has failed, due to the opposition of the synthetic fertilizer manufacturers of Japan. It seems that this was a proposition brought forward by the Okura Trading Company, which had tried to import Peruvian guano into Japan but had

=== Page 14 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

had not found the trade successful owing to the competition from Chilean nitrate and synthetic sulphate of ammonia. Recent events in Peru have greatly reduced the price of guano, and the Okura Trading Company hoped that the trade in guano could be reopened, provided a Japanese export to Peru could be arranged. The exchange situation has made trade in South America very difficult, and the Okura Trading Company apparently hoped that some sort of barter could be arranged to facilitate business between Japan and Peru. The idea of exchanging guano for arms having not proved successful, the Japanese firm is now apparently trying to barter arms for petroleum but, according to a Japanese official source, this is likely to be difficult, because American firms control the oil business in Peru and the Japanese have insufficient facilities to transport and store large quantities of petroleum. It is probable, according to a member of the Peruvian Mission, that any purchases made in Japan will have to be paid for in cash, Peru having little to offer Japan for bartering purposes.

The above appears to be a correct summary of the situation as it exists at the moment, although the Japanese and Peruvian authorities may be able to come to terms eventually on some contract of barter. From the above, however, it would seem that no diplomatic agreement is connected with the visit of the Peruvian Military Mission. The Peruvian Government simply wishes to obtain modern war equipment, to replace its present equipment, and is shopping

=== Page 15 === (confidence 0.97)
-4-

shopping to find the cheapest place in which to buy. The Peruvian Government would prefer, undoubtedly, to exchange commodities for the needed equipment, but this is apparently not a sine qua non. If the Peruvian Government finds it necessary to pay cash for its military purchases, it is probable that the Japanese Government will obtain all or a large part of the order, as the present very low valuation of the yen gives Japanese producers a heavy advantage over producers in other countries.

Respectfully yours,

Joseph C. Grew

Tokyo's despatch No. 222 of December 13, 1932, encloses a copy of a conversation between a member of the Embassy staff and the Peruvian Chargé d'Affaires in Tokyo. MEMORANDUM OF CONVERSATION

December 10, 1932.

"Mr. Lembcke called on me this morning to ask what truth if any there was in a report that he had heard to the effect that I had told people in the Foreign Office that the sale of Peruvian guano in Japan was making the Chileans very apprehensive. I told him that I had seen Mr. Kurusu, Chief of the Commercial Bureau, whose last post had been Minister to Peru, and had discussed to some extent the import of Chilean nitrates and guano here and that the conversation had covered the general question of nitrate products. (See despatch No. 216, 5 December 3, 1932). I told him that I could not understand how my conversation with Mr. Kurusu could possibly have been interpreted to mean that I was in any way expressing an opinion in regard to the relations between Peru and Chile; that I had no such intention and I wished to assure him that the relations between Peru and Chile or any other South American countries was something I did not know well enough to discuss and that even if I did I certainly would not discuss it with the Japanese Foreign Office.

He then told me that he was glad to hear that and referred to the Military Mission which is now in Tokyo from Peru. He said the purpose of that Mission was not

to

-2-

to engage in any such fantastic scheme as exchange of guano for firearms, that the two questions were not in any way related and that the purpose of the mission was purely one of study. I asked him if they were learning much and he said no. He added that what the Peruvians were interested in was small arms, particularly mountain guns and machine guns and that any models which the Japanese had for sale were too heavy to be of any use in Peru. He then told me that Peru was greatly concerned over the attitude of Colombia and that the Peruvian Government feared that the Colombians would attempt to obtain possession of the upper waters of the Amazon which Peru had held for a great many years. He said that the Colombians had recently been buying maritime equipment and arms and that they had gone so far as to purchase a gunboat in Spain and that they had already put armament on merchant ships which they had purchased in the United States and that the Peruvians feared that Colombia would send an expedition up the Amazon and attack Peru by water. I said that surely the Brazilians would not countenance an armed expedition going up the Amazon to attack a friendly nation. He said that Colombia had rights of navigation in peace or war on the Amazon secured by treaty. I asked him if the situation really was in his judgment as critical as his statements would indicate. He replied that the Peruvian Government was greatly concerned in the matter and was decidedly apprehensive that the Colombians

Reference is respectfully made to the Department's instructions No. 119 of October 24, 1932, and No. 127 of November 7, 1932, regarding the objects of the Peruvian Military Mission sent to Japan in September 1932, and to my several despatches on this subject.

The members of this mission were reported to have left Japan some time ago, apparently without accomplishing anything of importance. Recently the Military

Attaché

Confidential File

Attaché of this Embassy has learned, through contact with a diplomat from another South American country, of further developments of considerable interest.

Captain Indocochero (Gudacochea?) is reported to have remained in Japan, and to be living quietly in a suburb of Tokyo. An agreement is said to have been concluded whereby Japan is to furnish "several batteries" of the new Japanese 75 mm. field gun to the Peruvian army. These guns are said to be very effective.

General Araki is said to have persuaded the Supreme Military Council to the sale of these guns to Peru, despite the fact that this type of ordinance has not yet been supplied to the Japanese army. It seems that the Peruvians obtained these guns with the argument that they would be used for training, and that by the time the Japanese munition factories had completed the stock for the Japanese army, the Peruvian army would place a "large order" which would keep the factories occupied thereafter.

The Japanese Army is also supplying a quantity of the 37 mm. infantry gun and a combination hand and rifle grenade. Captain Indocochero is acting as inspector for all equipment sold to Peru.

The Military Attaché learns that this agreement was made in the greatest secrecy. It was particularly desired that the United States should not learn of the agreement, in view of the American policy of arms embargo to combatants and to the rumors of intended

My dear Mr. Parker:

This is in reply to your letter of December 11, 1946, addressed to Mr. Emerson of the Division of Japanese Affairs. Your letter made inquiry as to whether the Department of State would interpose objection to a private corporation entering one of the Japanese Mandated Islands for the purpose of mining bauxite. You indicated that such a concession would be granted only upon an open-bid basis, and upon the meeting of certain stipulated conditions as required by the Navy, by the bidder.

It is the intent of this Government to submit in the near future to the Security Council a proposed agreement for a strategic trusteeship for the Japanese Mandated Islands. The commercial policy pertaining to the Islands will of course be a subject of serious discussion. We are anxious in no way to prejudice our position on the trusteeship, and the Department of State therefore believes it would be unwise to proceed further until the international position of the Japanese Islands is clarified.

Sincerely yours,

Dear Mr. Emerson:

In the course of our very pleasant conversation Monday on the subject of Pacific island bauxite I promised to give you a memorandum outlining the present position.

For some years prior to the war the Japanese were engaged in bauxite mining on the Island of Babelthuap in the Palau Group. Recent investigation indicates that substantial reserves remain, although equipment and production facilities were largely destroyed by the war.

As you know, actual jurisdiction at the present time is with the United States Navy, with whom we have consulted on this subject. The question presented is whether or not and under what circumstances private enterprise might be permitted to enter the island and engage in the operation of mining bauxite. Obviously, adequate provision will be made for assigning such a concession on an open-bid basis, and the Navy would require certain stipulations as to the importation of labor, living quarters, and similar matters affecting the island.

The question on which we should greatly appreciate an expression of opinion from the Department of State is whether, assuming that all these matters and others were negotiated in a satisfactory manner, the Department of State would interpose objection to the entering of a private corporation at this time to one of the Japanese Mandated Islands for the purpose indicated.

Should further details be required, kindly communicate with me.

Sincerely yours,

G. R. Parker, Chief
Pacific Ocean Division

My dear Mr. Parker:

Your letter of December 11, 1946 to Mr. Emerson of the Division of Japanese Affairs requesting the position of the Department of State on the entering of a private corporation to one of the Japanese mandated islands for the purpose of engaging in bauxite mining has been referred to me for reply.

As you may be aware, the Department has already accepted the view that the Military Government, under certain conditions, may utilize the services of outside corporations on a contract basis for the mining of bauxite where local enterprise is not yet prepared to undertake such activities. Accordingly, the Department would probably not interpose an objection to your proposal provided that the conditions under which the contract is let are satisfactory to the Department. These conditions would be as follows:

1. There must be adequate compensation, held in trust for the natives, for the extraction of a irreplaceable resource. It is a basic economic policy of the Military Government strongly supported by this Department that the natives shall be protected against loss of ownership of their land and resources.

2. The terms under which laborers are imported into the mandated islands shall be in line with general Department policies relating to conditions under which labor may be employed by outside firms.

3. The contract and its award are not in conflict with the Department's position on the prevention of monopoly.

I desire to acknowledge your letter of February 22, in which you indicated that salt might be obtained in northern China and coal in Australia to provide for the manufacture of an exportable surplus of soda ash from Japan.

My dear Mr. Tiedemann:

With reference to our conversation concerning the future of the aluminum industry in Japan, the only information available on this subject relates to an action taken by the Far Eastern Commission on May 13, 1946. At this time the Far Eastern Commission approved a policy decision regarding interim reparations removals in the light metals industry.

This decision defined the light metal industry as "plants and establishments primarily engaged in the production of alumina, primary or secondary aluminum and magnesium, and in the rolling, extruding, drawing, forging, and casting of aluminum and magnesium and their alloys." As of May 13, 1946 all facilities identified within this category were to be made available for claim as reparations subject to the following limitations: (1) No facilities engaged in remelting light metal scrap into secondary ingot were to be made available for claim; (2) In cement plants converted to produce alumina from clays or shales, only the equipment introduced to effect such conversion was to be made available for claim; (3) Sufficient rolling and drawing equipment was to be retained to handle 15,000 metric tons per annum of fabricated aluminum. Such equipment was to be of a general purpose character and might be obtained from any surplus available in other branches of the non-ferrous metals industry.

Sincerely yours,

Hugh Porton
Chief

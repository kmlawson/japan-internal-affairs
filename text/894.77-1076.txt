COMPLETION OF RAILWAY LINE EXTENSION TO WHARF
AT NAGASAKI, JAPAN.

From Henry B. Hitchcock, Consul.
Nagasaki, Japan. Date of Completion April 1, 1930.
Date of Mailing April 1, 1930.

On March 18, 1930 took place the official opening of the extension of the railway from the central station at Nagasaki to the wharf at Deshima which is used by the express steamers plying between Nagasaki and Shanghai and between Nagasaki and Kobe. Passengers arriving by the express steamers from Shanghai may now board the train for central and eastern Japan at the wharf without the necessity of arranging local transportation for themselves and their effects to the central railway station.

This

=== Page 2 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

This extension is one step in the plan to connect the large cities of central and eastern Japan with Shanghai and China by the most rapid transportation facilities possible. The tunnel under the Moji-Shimonoseki Strait is another step for which preparations are now under way. When the express steamship service was inaugurated in 1923, it was the plan to commence the construction of the tunnel very soon thereafter but the great earthquake disaster of that year in Tokyo and Yokohama obliged the government to divert the funds appropriated for the tunnel to reconstruction work in the devastated area.

The plan has never been forgotten, however, during the intervening years and the present improvement in connections between rail and water is evidence of the resolution to carry it out. As further evidence may be cited the Ariake railway line now in course of construction which will, when completed in 1934, shorten the distance between Nagasaki and Moji by 60 kilometers. As the maximum gradients will be but 1.6 percent, as compared with the present maximum of 2.5 percent., there will be a saving in traveling time of from one hour and forty minutes to two hours. This line is to connect Isahaya with Hizen Yamaguchi by following the western coast line of Ariake Sea (or Chikushi Sea) between these two stations.

While Nagasaki has been more important in recent years as a shipbuilding and manufacturing center than as a port, the completion of the tunnel under the Moji-Shimonoseki Strait and the improvement in the railway facilities in the port and en route to central and eastern Japan will, it is believed, revive Nagasaki as a port of call for freight vessels since it will become in effect the western gateway of the railway system of Japan and the nearest port to central and southern China and southern Asia, as well as to the Philippine Islands, the East Indies and Australasia.

Manchuria Freight Conference

On January 19th the Freight Conference between Japan and Soviet Russia in Manchuria was opened, the session to last for two weeks. The proposal to open the conference came from the Russians, who desire to see restored the freight agreement between Japan and Russia which was made before the Soviet Revolution and which was discontinued because of the Great War and the Soviet Revolution. The organizations represented at the sessions included representatives of the South Manchuria Railway, the Chinese Eastern Railway, and the Ussuri Railway. As it was impossible for the representatives of the three railways to come to any agreement, the meeting was adjourned; but it was arranged that the freight question be held over to the next conference to be held some time in the future.

B. Resignation of Uchida.*

The resignation of Count Yasuya Uchida from the
Presidency of the South Manchuria Railway because of the
dismissal of his subordinate officer Eguchi caused a great
stir in political circles. After all efforts to persuade
him to stay in office seemed to have failed, Premier
Inukai turned to finding a suitable candidate. Factions
within the Seiyukai party immediately began urging the
candidacy of various men so strongly that Premier Inukai
found himself in a serious predicament. Meanwhile, the
Army was determined that Uchida should be persuaded to stay
on in spite of Seiyukai wishes to the contrary. The
Minister of War sent a representative to Dairen for the
purpose of persuading Uchida to stay at least for the
duration of the visit of the League's Commission of Inquiry.
The Seiyukai candidates refused to accept the office in
view of the Army's opposition and Uchida finally agreed to
stay on temporarily. Some newspapers referred to the
incident as showing to what extent the Cabinet was con-
trolled by the military.

February 15, 1939

RE: PA/H:

Mr. Frank C. Wright of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation called me by telephone this afternoon. He said that Japanese interests had offered to purchase from the Lehigh Valley Railroad sixty-two idle locomotives and so badly in need of repair that it would take six months to put them in running condition. It is presumed that the locomotives are for use on the South Manchuria Railroad. For these locomotives the Japanese have offered to pay $116,000 in advance and four annual payments of $150,000 at four percent. Mr. Wright explained that if the deal went through it would be very much to the advantage of the Lehigh Valley Railroad and also of possible advantage to the Government as arrangements could probably be made to have the Lehigh Valley Railroad apply a portion of these payments to its Government debt.

Mr. Wright said that the Lehigh Valley Railroad did not wish to enter into this transaction if there was any objection on the part of this Government and that the Secretary's office had asked him to take the matter up with me.

February 21, 1939.

Mr. Frank C. Wright of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation called me by telephone late this afternoon. He referred to our conversation on February 15 in regard to a proposed sale by the Lehigh Valley Railroad of 62 locomotives to Japanese interests. He said that he was leaving town in an hour or two; that he would see officers of the Lehigh Valley Railroad; and that he would greatly appreciate it if I could give him immediately an answer to the question which he raised in our conversation.

As Mr. Hamilton's memorandum of February 17 had not as yet reached Co, and as it could not be located immediately, I consulted Mr. Hornbeck, Mr. Feis, and Mr. Hamilton orally as to the reply which I should make to Mr. Wright. On the basis of their suggestions and at Mr. Feis's request, I called Mr. Wright by telephone and told him that I would prefer not to reply to his letter of February 15. I suggested, however, that he might wish to inform the appropriate official

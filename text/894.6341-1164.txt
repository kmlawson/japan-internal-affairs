Subject: Metallic Minerals.
Plan for increase in production.

1. The following article dealing with the subject of plans for the increase of gold production in the Japanese Empire, is a translation from the Bungei hunju magazine. The author, Ichiro Koyama, is a director of the Shoin Mining Company, is associated with the Mitsubishi Mining interests, and is recognized as an authority on mining in Japan. The criticism of present governmental policies which appears in the last paragraph, is interesting in that it points to hitherto undeveloped gold resources which are presumably those through which a production of ¥500,000,000 a year may in part be realized.

GOLD MINING INDUSTRY IS OF UP FOR THE CHINA, THE CURRENT JAPAN

"Whereas Japan spent only ¥200,000,000 and ¥1,200,000,000 respectively for the Sino-Japanese War (1894-95) and the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05), she has already appropriated the huge sum of ¥8,000,000,000 for the current fiscal year alone. Her foreign trade, which resulted in an adverse balance of ¥630,000,000 last year, is unlikely to improve this year. So long as Japan continues her military operations in China, she must needs import munition articles more and more. Besides, enormous funds are needed for her industrial expansion program and her development works in the continent. Under the circumstances, the importance of the gold mining industry requires no further expansion.

"The Japanese Government has been granting subsidies to the gold miners and refiners since June, 1933. The Commerce and Industry Ministry last year appropriated ¥4,823,000 for this purpose. On the other hand, the Gold Production Law was promulgated with the view to intensifying Government encouragement for the gold mining industry and placing all the gold output under Government control. Further, gold consumption has been strictly restricted since last fall. Such machinery and equipment as are necessary for gold mining have been exempt from the import duty. Railway fees for gold ores have been reduced by 40 per cent.

"More liberal protection and encouragement have been enforced by the Government-General of Korea since the early years of Japanese administration. In the case of poor ores, for instance, the expense for transportation from any mine to the nearest railway station is to be defrayed by the Government-General. All the protective

Besides, there are more than one hundred gold mines which produce slightly less than one million men's worth of gold a year. Gold resources being distributed widely in Korea, there are only two gun (minor administrative district) which have no gold mine-lots. The Daiyudo Gold Mine, although it was once operated by French interests, is now under management of the Nippon Mining Company. In 1936, the mine produced ¥4,000,000 as against ¥5,000,000 of the Unzan Gold Mine. But the former last year surpassed the latter in output as seen in the above table, and is expected to increase its production more and more. But the Unzan Mine, having exhausted all its rich lots by now, is unlikely to prosper for years to come. It was at one time under management of American interests.

North and Central China, however, have apparently no large gold deposits. Along the Great Wall and the boundary line with Jehol Province of Manchoukuo there are some promising gold mines in Hopei Province. They cannot be exploited effectively, so long as they are under Chinese management. It is reported that Japanese interests will extend technical and financial assistance to them. The eastern part of Shantung Province is the most important gold producing district in China, including such gold mines as Linglung, Pingtu and Mouping in Chaoyuan-hsien. With the only exception of the Linglung Gold Mine which has been operated under Sino-Japanese joint management since 1936, all the gold mines in this province were owned by the Shantung Provincial Government prior to the present incident. The output from these mines is estimated at about ¥300,000 or ¥400,000. The Linglung Mine commenced mining works on April 1, 1936, and its dressing and refining facilities, which had the capacity of treating 1,500,000 metric tons of gold ores a day, were completed in August last year. All of its equipment, however, has been damaged by the Chinese forces. They are now under repairs and will be put into operation in the middle of next year.

"Central China is particularly poor in gold resources. There is the Pingkiang Gold Mine in Hunan Province. But its scale is rather small, and its production is trifling. It is to be noted, on the other hand, that copper ores available along the middle stream of the Yangtze contain so large an amount of gold that the yellow metal may be refined from them as by-product. Although no efforts have been made as yet towards that direction, it is not impossible to make the best use of these copper ores for the reason that the transportation facilities are so well-developed in this part of China.

"All told, the total gold production of the Japanese Empire is estimated at 40 or 50 metric tons a year valued at approximately ¥200,000,000. In an attempt to boost the figure to ¥500,000,000, the Government has worked out a five-year plan for increase of gold output. The output quantity, it is projected, is to be expanded to 60 metric tons for Japan Proper and to 75 metric tons for Korea. In the event this plan is carried out with success, there would be no need for worry over the nation's international accounts and the quotations of the yen. Although such a large scale plan would be far from practicable in time of peace, it is not impossible to carry it out at the present moment when all the available measures, if necessary, must be mobilized for that purpose. The Gold Mining Promotion Company Law has been promulgated, calling for establishment of a company capitalized at ¥50,000,000 half of which is to be invested by the Government. Further, the Government will strengthen its encouragement and protection.

While only part of the mine-lots is actually exploited, the majority of them, owned by some leading gold companies, have been kept 'sleeping' without any attempt being made for prospecting and exploitation. Still worse, the owners are inclined neither to sell nor to rent such sleeping mine-lots. The Government should force them to start mining and refining works within a fixed date. Any adjacent mine-lots, if owned by different firms, should be operated on a joint management system. It is urgently necessary, too, for the Government to establish Central Pressing and Refining Plants for protecting the small scale gold miners who are selling their mined products to some leading refineries at considerably low prices."

2. Comments.

To what extent this plan is practical cannot be determined, though it is certain that no efforts will be spared to increase production in every way possible, in order to augment the supply of gold during the present emergency. While the necessary work in connection with the opening of new mines renders it unlikely that any increase realized under this plan will be apparent in the immediate future, it is felt that the efforts being made in this respect cannot but account for increases within an appropriate time, and that meanwhile, a certain increase may be counted upon through the more active operation of mines already in production.

SUBJECT: Japanese in British Columbia.

THE HONORABLE
Pierrepont Moffat,
American Minister,
Ottawa, Canada.

Sir:

I have the honor to give the following information regarding the Japanese in British Columbia and developments which have occurred since hostilities commenced between Canada and Japan.

The general position of the Japanese in British Columbia is doubtless known to the Legation. In this connection reference is made to confidential reports from this Consulate General to the Department dated April 25, 1932 and May 15, 1933 entitled "The Japanese in British Columbia" (File 840.1). Copies of these reports were forwarded to the Legation.

During the summer of 1941 the Royal Canadian Mounted Police undertook the registration of Japanese in British Columbia. The figures relating to this registration were released to the press on December 10th and a break-down of these figures is shown hereunder. The total of the Japanese population in British Columbia including Canadian-born, naturalized Canadians, Japanese nationals and others is 23,428. Following registration, Japanese were issued identification cards showing the bearer's photograph, fingerprint, et cetera. These cards are divided into three different categories i.e., for Canadian-born, for naturalized Canadians and for Japanese nationals.

Category X: Canadians who have become Japanese nationals through marriage.

Immediately following the declaration of war the Royal Canadian Mounted Police rounded up a few Japanese who they suspected of subversive activities. These are being detained by the Canadian Immigration and probably will be interned. Although the exact number of those held is not known, I was reliably informed that it is less than 30 in Vancouver. I have no information regarding the number in Victoria. Japanese newspapers of which there are three in Vancouver have been closed and the Japanese language schools have been closed throughout British Columbia and all Canada.

One of the greatest problems of the authorities in connection with the Japanese is the control of Japanese fishing boats. The exact number of these boats is not known to this office but may be roughly

roughly placed at about 1,000. Licenses for commercial fishing may be issued only to Canadians, i.e., Japanese who are either Canadian-born or naturalized. Thus Japanese nationals may not participate in this occupation as license holders. The number of fishing licenses issued to Canadian-born and naturalized Japanese according to latest figures available is 1,886. The Canadian Navy acting under the Defense of Canada Regulations has ordered all Japanese fishing boats to report at a number of specified stations along the British Columbia coast. These vessels are to be detained and, at least in Vancouver, they are being dismantled or rendered unfit for immediate use.

The Japanese Consulate in Vancouver consists of a Consul, Mr. Ichiro Kawasaki and five Japanese members of his staff. The office of the Consulate is in the Marine Building, 355 Burrard Street. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police, I am reliably informed, placed a plain-clothes man at the Consulate on December 7th and, under instructions from Ottawa, the Consulate is allowed to carry on its functions, or at least certain of them, until the end of this week, December 13th. After that, the Consul and his subordinates will be allowed to go to their homes but will be required to ask for an escort of police when going outside their homes. As I understand it, no guard will be placed at their homes according to present instructions.

There have been one or two very minor demonstrations such as window-breaking, et cetera, but on the whole the Japanese so far have been treated extraordinarily well. Japanese on the staff of the Vancouver Club, of whom there were several, have been dismissed and their places filled by whites.

The attitude of the local military and law enforcement officers toward the Japanese appears to be that so long as they behave themselves they should be let alone. Also there seems to be, to my mind, too much emphasis placed on the difference between Canadian-Japanese and Japanese nationals, and the fact that certain Japanese individuals are buying War Savings Certificates. Some local Canadian Japanese organizations have passed resolutions of loyalty to Canada including the Japanese branch of the local Canadian Legion. These resolutions have been given great publicity and it seems to me that they serve to lull the public into believing that these organizations are behind Canada 100% in this struggle, which, to anyone who knows Japanese character even slightly, is far from the case.

My statement above regarding the official attitude is based on what I have gathered from talks with the officer commanding the Royal Canadian Mounted Police and the Vancouver Military Defenses. The former added that personally he did not trust any of them, meaning both Canadian-Japanese and Japanese nationals. It may be that a greater degree of surveillance is now being exercised over Japanese generally than was indicated to me. I sincerely hope that such is the case because, owing to their large numbers, their varied occupations and scattered places of residence, they present a real problem to the authorities.

Respectfully yours,

Paul R. Josselyn,
American Consul General.

January 16, 1942.

The Honorable
Pierrepont Moffat,
American Minister,
Ottawa, Canada.

Dear Mr. Minister:

Please accept my thanks for your letter of December 19 dealing with the questions of American citizens in the Canadian armed forces and price control, and also your letter of December 24 regarding the treatment of Japanese.

The question of how best to deal with the Japanese on this coast still predominates all others and is widely discussed pro and con in private conversations, editorials, letters to the editor and meetings of all kind. Inasmuch as the actual decisions taken so far have been taken at Ottawa, I presume you are familiar with developments. However, I will try to summarize what has taken place.

Representatives of the Standing Committee on Oriental Affairs went to Ottawa during the first week in January to discuss with the Dominion Government their proposals to meet the situation. This Committee is headed by Mayor F. J. Hume of New Westminster. The Provincial Minister of Labor, George S. Pearson, also proceeded to Ottawa at the invitation of the Dominion Government as did also the Commissioner of British Columbia Police and others. Following conferences in Ottawa the Dominion Government issued a lengthy statement on January 14 the substance of which is that all enemy aliens, including Japanese, except those holding police permits to remain, are to be removed from so-called "protected areas" in Canada. Steps are being taken to define such areas and the date for removal will be subsequently announced. An organization to provide for the employment of adult male enemy aliens outside the protected areas on national work is to be created. A special office is to be set up in British Columbia for the sale or charter to suitable persons of fishing vessels and fishing equipment formerly used or owned by Canadian Japanese. (Japanese nationals have not been allowed to do commercial fishing.) The sale of gasoline and explosives to persons of Japanese origin will be controlled by the Mounted Police. Japanese nationals will be forbidden to possess or use short-wave radio receiving sets, radio transmitters and cameras. A copy of the text of the Government's statement as it appeared in the press will be forwarded to the

Legation and the Department by separate despatch. The Committee appointed to supervise the lease or sale of the immobilized Japanese fishing fleet will consist of Mr. Justice Sidney Smith of Vancouver who is the Judge of the Admiralty Court here, Commander B. L. Johnson formerly in charge of the Naval Control Office in Vancouver, only recently transferred to eastern Canada, and Mr. Kishizo Kimura, who is, I believe, a naturalized Canadian and connected with the fishing business. There are at present approximately 1,100 immobilized Japanese fishing vessels in British Columbia of which 980 are impounded in the mouth of the Fraser River and the remainder in Prince Rupert and other Pacific Coast ports. They have an estimated value of between two and three million dollars and it is understood that the competition for their lease, sale or charter will be brisk. Mr. G. W. MacPherson of the office of the Custodian of Enemy Property is at present in Vancouver in connection with Japanese property. He has informed me privately that he does not yet know where his Department stands in connection with the question of the removal of Japanese; that he has been beset by inquirers since the removal notice was published in the newspapers of the 14th, and that Japanese are being put under pressure to dispose to whites of their various properties, timber and mining rights, etc., sometimes at considerable sacrifice.

So far as can be learned the Japanese appear to have accepted the Ottawa order quietly. THE NEW CANADIAN, a Japanese newspaper published in English and the only Japanese newspaper still allowed to be published, has advised the Japanese to wait until all details of the regulations are worked out and has expressed confidence that the regulations will be administered fairly and in accordance with the principles of justice. The Okanagan Valley in eastern British Columbia, while it might welcome Japanese labor in the fruit and vegetable industry during the war, yet it wants to make certain that any Japanese sent there will not become a permanent resident. The Kelowna Board of Trade and other associations in the Okanagan have stated their position on the question in resolutions which they have forwarded to Ottawa and Victoria.

I understand that recently an officer or officers of the United States Army have arrived to be stationed in Vancouver. Their duties are, I believe, in connection with military intelligence.

The following figures regarding enlistments in British Columbia were given out by the headquarters of Military District #11 at Victoria shortly after the beginning of the year: Enlistments for active service since the beginning of the war - 24,760; in 1941 - 6,765. The number enlisted in British Columbia in the R.C.A.F. during 1941 is stated to be 6,300. No figures have been released regarding naval enlistments.

The British Columbia Legislature resumed its session January 8. The formal opening session had taken place on December 4 following which the Body adjourned to the above date. As you will recall, British Columbia now has a coalition government the opposition being furnished by the Cooperative Commonwealth Federation. The leader of the C.C.F. opposition, Mr. Harold Winch, announced his Party's war program in a speech at the opening of the session as follows:

1. Fixed limit on all Canadian incomes.

2. Compulsory interest-free loans.

3. Compulsory service by all able-bodied citizens in various branches of civilian protection work. (He charged that there is great inefficiency in war plants in Vancouver and Victoria and stated that workers in war industry recognized that few plants were working to capacity.)

4. Labor representation on all war boards.

5. Income tax revision including drastic increases in higher brackets.

6. Increase of excess profits tax to include all profits over 5% during the war.

7. Rationing as the only means combined with price-control to stem inflation.

8. Selective service for war industry and active service.

Mr. Winch has been described as a gadfly to the Government and I think that this is an apt designation. From the large number of "notices of questions," some of them embarrassing, which he prepares and fires at the various ministers every day, it is evident that the C.C.F. are pretty much on their toes. Bruce Hutchison, writing in the VANCOUVER SUN, believes that the present coalition government in British Columbia is tending toward the left; that this is the trend everywhere and that the phenomenal voting strength of the C.C.F. in the last election shows that if the coalition is to win in the next election it must halt the growth of the C.C.F. by satisfying those elements in the population which now see their only hope in the C.C.F.

Sincerely yours,

Paul R. Jesselyn,
American Consul General.

THE VANDOUVER DAILY PROVINCE (Conservative) dated January 14, 1942.

# TEXT OF STATEMENT

## Premier King Gives Reasons For Japanese Restrictions

(By Canadian Press.)

OTTAWA, Jan. 14.—Following is the text of a statement issued by Prime Minister-Mackenzie King today on Japanese and other aliens in Canada:

During recent weeks the Canadian Government has been giving detailed and careful thought to the problems created by the presence in British Columbia of a large number of persons of Japanese racial origin. On January 8 a conference was called in Ottawa to study and report on these problems.

At this conference the government had the benefit of the advice and assistance of representatives of the Government of British Columbia and of the members of the standing committee on Orientals of which Mayor F. J. Hume of New Westminster is the chairman. For the aid thus given the government is most grateful.

## Defense Services Checked Situation

Finally the government has received appreciations of the situation on the Pacific Coast from the representatives of the defense services. It has also been advised upon international complications to which certain courses of action might give rise.

As a consequence of these various contributions to its knowledge the government believes it is in possession of all relevant facts and that it is in the best position to judge as to the policy that should be adopted.

In announcing its program the government accordingly believes that it is justified in asking for, and in expecting to receive, the firm support of all Canadians.

The government has found no disposition in any responsible quarter to question the justice and validity of the fundamental principles upon which its policy in relation to the Japanese problem has been based.

## Principles Are Set To Handle Problems

These principles are now reaffirmed; in the future, as in the past, they will provide the standard against which all proposals relating to this problem will be measured.

In summary form they may be described as follows:

(a) National defense and victory are the first and overriding considerations.

(b) Canadians of Japanese racial origin and Japanese nationals resident in Canada will be justly treated.

## Warning Is Made On Demonstrations

a. Every feasible step should be taken to encourage the maintenance of a calm and reasonable attitude among Canadian citizens generally. The full force of the law will be invoked to prevent anti-Japanese demonstrations and to protect Canadian residents of Japanese race.

b. No action will be taken or permitted which would give any excuse to the government of Japan for mistreating Canadians under Japanese control. Nor will any action be taken or permitted which would help the Japanese anywhere to arouse Asiatic hostility against the white race.

c. Canada will continue to collaborate with Great Britain and the United States, with a view to the substantial coordination of their policies in relation to persons of Japanese racial origin within their respective jurisdictions.

In accordance with these principles and in the execution of plans worked out well in advance the Canadian Government, as a consequence of the new situation created by Japan's treacherous attack upon Pearl Harbor, at once immobilized all fishing vessels operated by persons of Japanese racial origin on the Pacific Coast.

All persons of Japanese racial origin will be prohibited, for the duration of the war against Japan, from fishing or serving on fishing vessels; or on other vessels operated by Japanese off the coast of British Columbia.

## Gas, Explosives Sale Controlled

For the same period the sale of gasoline and explosives to persons of Japanese racial origin will be directly controlled under conditions to be prescribed by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police.

Japanese nationals will be forbidden to possess or use aboveware radio receiving sets, radio transmitters and cameras.

The present intensive surveillance of Japanese nationals will be continued, and the Defense of Canada Regulations will be strictly enforced.

It is intended to organize a civilian corps of Canadian Japanese to be used on projects of value to the national cause, in value to the national cause, in the various groups of Canadian Japanese who have indicated their desire to serve.

Steps are being taken to provide for defining protected areas in Canada and, subsequent to a date to be announced, all enemy aliens (of whatever origin) except those who possess permits from the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, will require to have moved from such protected areas on the Pacific Coast as may be defined. Arrangements will be made by the Federal Government to provide accommodation for persons thus removed.

A separate organization will be created to provide opportunities for the employment of adult male enemy aliens outside the protected areas on work of national value.

In order that the Canadian war effort may not suffer because of necessary restrictions placed upon persons of Japanese origin, the government proposes to arrange at once, through the establishment of a special office in British Columbia, for the sale, lease, requisition or charter, on equitable terms and to suitable persons, of fishing or other vessels and fishing equipment heretofore used or owned by Canadians of Japanese racial origin, now immobilized on the Pacific Coast.

The removal of the Japanese fishermen will place upon the white fishermen and canners of the Pacific Coast a heavy responsibility for seeing that this removal does not reduce the total of fish caught or increase the prices at which the catch is made available to Canada, to the United Kingdom, and to the United Nations.

## Special Consideration for War Veterans

In the working out of its plans the government proposes to give special consideration to those persons of Japanese race who served in the Canadian expeditionary force during the world war.

The government is, of the opinion, and in this it has the concurrence of its technical advisors, that the arrangements outlined above, combined with the steps already taken are very much in the interests of the Japanese residents of British Columbia themselves.

It is believed that they will provide adequate safeguards against possible subversive activities, however, fomented, in British Columbia. The government proposes, therefore, to carry through its program with the utmost expedition and thoroughness.

It avails itself of this opportunity to reiterate what has already been said by O. J. Hime Minister that while the utmost precaution will be taken to see that no illegal acts are committed by Japanese or by other enemy aliens resident in Canada, those who conform loyally to the regulations set out for their guidance will be given every protection both for themselves and their property.

## Thank Fair Play Of B.C. Citizens

In conclusion the government wishes to express thanks to those public officers and private citizens in British Columbia who, without being blind to the dangers and uncertainties with which they are confronted, have nevertheless set so admirable an example of fair play in their attitude towards those persons of Japanese race now resident in that province.

In this connection the government wishes particularly to thank Hon. G. S. Pearson, minister of labor of the government of British Columbia, Mayor Hume, chairman of the standing committee on Orientals in British Columbia, and the other members of the committee, Mayor J. W. Cornett of Vancouver, and all others who have contributed to the steadying of public opinion along the Pacific coast. Similar thanks is due to the newspapers which almost without exception have realized the weight of their responsibility.

The standing committee on Orientals in British Columbia, to which reference has been made above, is composed of the following persons: Fred J. Hume, mayor of New Westminster, chairman; Professor H. F. Angus, Lt.-Col. Macgregor MacIntosh, Assistant Commissioner F. J. Mead, R.C.M.P.; Lt.-Col. A. W. Sparling.

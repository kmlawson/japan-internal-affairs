The Typhoon of September 21, 1934.

In the early morning of September 21, 1934 a typhoon of unequalled violence devastated the port of Osaka, Japan. A wind which at its apogee reached a recorded velocity of 100 miles an hour tore vessels from their moorings. Fired the mules from storage sheds, twisted steel cribber and the were made of lead pipe and, worst of all, buried against the vulnerable port a wall of water fifteen feet higher than normal flood tide.

Fifteen steamers of more than 1,000 gross tons were torn from their moorings by the wind and sea, or else dragged their buoys after the until they were beached or sank. One large freighter, the Batavia Maru of 4,392 gross tons, actually dragged her mooring and its huge mushroom and/or for a distance of a mile and a half, until she was buried ashore. Five larger vessels were beached, while small steamers of 1,500 tons were contemptuously tossed on what ordinarily is dry land. This havoc to shipping resulted not only because the typhoon was of unusual severity, but also in some measure it was due to defects in the construction of the harbor, which were pointed out in this Consulate's basic shipping report under reference.

It would appear needless in this supplementary report to repeat the conclusions given on pages 58 and 59 of the basic survey of March 1, 1934. The mistaken placing of the entrance to the harbor at the one point most vulnerable to prevailing wind and sea, the inadequate protection afforded by the low breakwaters, the

**Damage to the Port of Osaka.**

The principal damage to the port of Osaka was the loss of the main landing pier or so-called "Great Pier" (see main frontispiece, basic report). This structure, which extended from the Customs hole into the inner harbor, was 400 meters in length and 27.7 meters wide, rising 2.1 meters above flood water. It was used as the wharf to small passenger vessels in the coasting trade. After the typhoon the "Great Pier" had completely disappeared, save for a stump projecting a hundred feet from the Customs hole.

The modern docks Nos. 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 withstood the gale with only nominal damage. The principal destruction was caused by the flood water and the battering action of the debris-laden waves against the cargo sheds. Material damage was caused by sea water inundating these storage places. Cranes were in some cases destroyed by the waves and wind.

Next to the loss of the "Great Pier" the most serious destruction was wrought at the Mitsui Bussan Kaisha coal docks at Sakurajima. Of the nine gantry cranes reported in March as pertaining to this huge bunkering depot, but one was left intact after the typhoon had passed. The Sakurajima coal piers were directly in the path of the prodigious seas, as the low breakwaters of the port were utterly ineffectual in opposing the onrushing waves. The principal cause of

**Reconstruction.**

As pointed out in the basic shipping report under reference, the City of Osaka has manifested undiminished zeal for wresting a harbor from an inimical environment in spite of all obstacles. Although discomfited by proof that their sea port was not effectively designed, the municipal authorities undertook immediately the work of reconstruction and appointed experts to elaborate schemes which will in the future prevent or minimize similar disasters.

On October 8th a joint commission, composed of representatives of the municipal and prefectural governments, the Ministries of Home Affairs and Communications, local warehouse firms, shipping companies and insurance organizations, met to consider ways and means of improving the harbor. The principal item of the agenda was a project for the construction of breakwaters which should in the future prevent inundation of the port by seas sweeping down the length of the Bay of Osaka. (5) This could be accomplished by heightening the present breakwaters ten feet and

The work of Salvaging Ships has progressed rapidly, principally under the direction of the Japan Salvage Company. When the writer last visited the harbor, on October 17th, there were not two of the large sunken steamers which had not been raised and several smaller vessels which were still high and dry ashore.

As a special inducement to the speedy return of normal shipping activity at Osaka, the city has offered temporarily to reduce charges for buoys and wharfage by 50%. ## Return to Normal Conditions

Normal shipping conditions at Saka were partially restored during the first week of October. By October 8th there were 65 commercial vessels working cargo or taking on passengers at the port. In October 17th normal conditions had practically been regained save for crowding at the berths for small passenger liners due to the loss of the "Great Air", a shortage of lighters and the wreckage at the Sakaima coal docks. The resiliency with which Saka came back after such a terrific buffeting is a welcome injury of the future progress of the port. The vessels taught by the typhoon of September 21st were terrific and might have been partially obviated, but they have been learned and there will result a port of Saka more worthy of the name.

Subject: Ports and Harbors.

The Port of Tsuruga (Tsurugaminato).

1. Tsurugaminato, which is about three miles from the city of Tsuruga (population about 25,000), is one of the principal harbors on the northern coast of the main island of Japan facing the Japan Sea.

2. a. The attached panoramic photographs show the harbor as it exists today.

b. Location:

Tsuruga Bay is located at 35° 43' north latitude and 136° 04' east longitude, the southern portion of which is the harbor of Tsuruga. Distances (in land miles by rail) to the principal cities of Japan are:

Osaka 96 miles
Kobe 118
Nagoya 80
Tokyo 310

Distances to ports in Korea and Siberia (in nautical miles) are:

Seishin 467 miles
Rashin 496
Tuki 512
Vladivostok 595

c. Relation to principal ocean routes of commerce:

Tsuruga is probably the most important forwarding port in Japan for commerce to Vladivostok, north Korea and north Manchuria.

d. Land lines of communication:

Branch lines lead to Tsuruga harbor from the city of Tsuruga through which passes the Hokuriku main line. This railway runs along the northern coast of Japan facing the Japan Sea with a line extending south from Tsuruga to Seibara where it joins the Tokyo-Nagoya-Osaka-Kabe main line (Tokio).

e. Port Facilities:

The wharves and docks seen in the photographs attached are of concrete construction.

From: M/A. Tokyo. Report No. 8385 July 16, 1936.

f. Harbor:

Tsurugaminato is actually only the southern end of Tsuruga Bay, the latter being about six miles long in a north and south direction and about 3½ miles wide at the entrance between the two capes of Tateishi and Oka. Being open to the north, the gales of autumn and winter from that quarter cause swells but being landlocked by high ranges of mountains on all other sides is safe from other winds. Depths at the entrance to Tsuruga Bay is from 14 to 24 fathoms (25.6 to 43.9 meters) decreasing gradually inward to 12 to 15 fathoms (21.9 to 27.4 meters) at the head. The best anchorage for large vessels is about 600-800 yards westward of the wharf shown in the right of the upper photograph. The improved harbor (which has been dredged to 24 feet) and the wharf mentioned above can now accommodate 2 or 3 ships of 4000 tons. The wharf shown in the upper left of the photograph can handle four ships of 1500-2000 tons.

g. Storage Space:

The total storage space at Tsurugaminato is somewhat less than at Keishin (estimated to be between 15,000 and 20,000 sq. meters) but it is probable that other space is available in the city of Tsuruga itself.

h. Tidal Variation:

The mean high water interval in Tsuruga Bay is 2h.30m; the mean range of tide 0.5 ft.

i. Expansion:

There was no evidence of projected expansion at Tsurugaminato nor were additional wharves or storage space being built.

5. Conclusion.

Tsurugaminato is an excellent small harbor, well protected from winds and entirely ice free. To exploit the full possibilities of the harbor would require some dredging on the south end and the building of more wharves and warehouses.

AMERICAN CONSULATE,
Osaka, Japan, March 2, 1938.

STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL

The Honorable
Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador,
Tokyo.

Sir:-

I have the honor to report that I have just been advised from apparently reliable sources that the port of Kobe has been temporarily closed to foreign shipping. It is understood that the closing order became operative yesterday morning and that it will remain in force for a week or two.

It is said that foreign steamers which usually come up to the piers to work cargo have been instructed to anchor in the bay and work their cargo through lighters. Sightseers are being kept away from the pier-heads. The temporary closure of Kobe is said to be due to impending troop movements through that port. A body of infantry troops was seen marching towards a loading pier in the very early hours of yesterday morning. No information is available as to the number of soldiers in this group or the location of their home garrison, but it is believed that they came from Nagoya.

Another informant, a gentleman whose house overlooks the sea a few miles east of Kobe, states that there

=== Page 18 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

there has been considerable movement of red-cross ships within the past few days; several hospital ships have lately been observed steaming in both directions through the inland sea.

In the port of Osaka no unusual activity of any sort is visible; likewise practically no soldiers are seen on the city streets. As far as can be learned no troops are being concentrated here for overseas duty.

The fact that the port of Kobe is now temporarily closed and that several hospital ships have lately been seen steaming through the inland sea is construed by some observers here as an indication that another big Japanese drive is pending in China; in other words, some think that the Japanese are clearing out their base hospitals there and at the same time despatching new troops to the mainland.

Very respectfully yours,

George A. Makinson,
American Consul General

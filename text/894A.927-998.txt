I have the honor to report that on January 7, 1938 Paul King Benedict, 25, bearer of Passport No. 451740 issued January 26, 1937 by the Department registered at this office and presented credentials from Harvard University stating that he was qualified to pursue certain anthropological studies in work for his degree of Doctor of Philosophy.
Mr. Benedict had the idea, as did those in Harvard who had sponsored his work that all he needed do was to settle down in some town in China or Japan and begin his measurements and consult official town records to confirm statements that might be doubtful. He explained that he had gone to Shanghai last August with the intention of working on his thesis of finding out whether there might be some connection between a person's physical characteristics and his social standing by working on the male population between the ages of about 17 and 60 in various towns in China of about 5,000 population where certain conditions

Question. What are your general thoughts and impressions from
the time you arrived in Kotosho?

Answer
I have now been on Koto-sho for six weeks and throughout
that period I have enjoyed perfect health. The winter
climate on Kotosho is in many respects ideal and in
this respect the experience has been very much more
enjoyable than I had been led to expect. Wind and
rainstorms have been frequent but in other respects
conditions have been almost perfect. The whole
expedition has carried out regular anti-malaria
routine, but no sickness of any kind has been
experienced. Very few mosquitoes of the malaria
carrying type have been seen and it seems probable
that during the cool winter months danger from this
source is very slight. The expedition arrived in
Kotosho with full tent equipment, but on our arrival
the local police very kindly put at our disposal two
separate rooms in the "foreign style" building recently
erected by them. It is impossible to overestimate
the benefit that has accrued to the expedition
through thus acquiring the use of a permanent working
and sleeping headquarters. With the high winds
which have frequently occurred during our stay, tent
life would have been full of difficulties, and it is
certain that a very large amount of the work which
we have been able to do would have remained incomplete
if we had not had the use of this superior living
accommodation. I should like to take this opportunity
of expressing my personal thanks to the Koto-sho police
for their assistance in this as in all other respects.
Especial thanks are also due to Mr. Yoshimuna, Chief
of Police, for his most lucid and valuable advice
concerning Yami customs and legends and also to the
Doctor for his willing cooperation in the hypnosis
experiments which we have been able to carry out on
various Yami subjects. In conclusion I should like
to say how much I appreciate the honour that the
Japanese authorities have done to us in thus
permitting us to be the first foreign scientists to
make a protracted stay on Koto-sho.

Question. Is there anything of great or important interest
which you have discovered in the course of your studies?

Answer
The work which I have carried out here during my stay
has been done entirely at the instigation of Dr. Stewart,
and no independent study has been made apart from that
of the general work of the expedition. In the field
of Psycho-Ethnography Dr. Stewart has for some years
been collecting data in connection with the study
of dreams and dream symbolism. Such study is very
difficult in the case of members of advanced cultures
such as the Japanese or Europeans because owing to
the great range of an individuals experience the
details of his dream symbolism tend to become extremely
complicated. In a society such as the Yami where
environment, custom, and locality is completely
stereotyped

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

stereotyped it is natural to expect that the dream symbols employed will be much more limited in range and accordingly more adaptable to scientific analysis. During our stay here Dr. Stewart has been able to collect a very large amount of data in connection with Yami dreams and the limited range of symbolism, which we expected has in fact been found. A great deal of laborious work will have to be done on these data before any scientific deductions can be made. Dr. Stewart has already collected much similar material from other primitive tribes of the pacific area and he is confident that in due course, by analysis and comparison of all these various data, he will be able to arrive at facts of great scientific importance. It may be noted here that the hypnosis experiment previously mentioned was undertaken with a view to checking the reliability of the dreams which these same individuals had previously recorded in their waking state. Analysis under hypnosis confirmed the limited range of the Yami's mental symbolism.

**Question.** What is the comparison between the Yami and other foreign aborigines?

**Answer** We have made a large number of enquiries of a purely anthropological nature, but we have done this rather with a view to the light they would throw on Yami psychology than in connection with strict comparative ethnology as such. Excellent work carried out by various Japanese scientists has conclusively proved the close association between the inhabitants of Koto-sho and Batan; in particular it has been shown that the Yami language is virtually a dialect of Batanese. There is various evidence however that the present Yami race is the result of an earlier of two markedly dissimilar races. I have taken notes on a large number significant details connected with Yami culture and I am hopeful that on my return to England I shall be able to carry out research which will, link up the Yami with other areas besides Batan.

**Question.** What is the special difference between the Yami and other races which you know?

**Answer** This is a very difficult question to answer. Similarities to practically every detail of Yami culture can be found scattered among the various tribes of Indonesia, Malay, and Melanesia, and no single detail can be picked out as finally unique. Yami culture may however be characterised as very exceptionally well balanced and stable. The custom of changing the fathers name to correspond to that of his eldest son, and the rigidly effective tabo against the taking of human life are in my experience unique.

**Question.** What is the state of Yami evolution?

**Answer** As is evidenced by their recent use of stone implements,

-3-

implements, the shape and unglazed nature of their
pottery, and their use of the loom Yami culture may
generally be described as "Late Neolithic". Their
irrigation methods in the cultivation of tare, their
use of iron, and the design and construction of their
boats and houses characterise them however as possessing
an exceptionally advanced stone age culture. Bamboo
is now little used by the Yami but there is evidence
both in their design and in their legends that some
of their ancestors have sprung from a bamboo using
people. In general their cultural stage of development
is very similar to that of many other tribes in this
area, but their social and moral code is much superior
to the majority.

**Question.** Have you derived any advantage from being the first
Europeans to visit Koto-sho?

**Answer** As already noted I am fully appreciative of the
honour we have received in being allowed to be the
first European scientists to visit Koto-sho. As
previously noted I am hopeful that from the data I
have been able to collect I shall later be able to
build up some important research in the Anthropological
field. It must however be emphasised that our work
is in no sense "Complete". The Yami are a fully self
contained society who have clearly existed for many
hundreds of years entirely independent of outside
influence in any form. As such they are deserving of
thorough and intensive study. The investigations
which we have just undertaken cannot be regarded as
anything but superficial; a thorough study would
require years of research. I hope in full confidence
that the Japanese authorities will continue to aid
the cause of science by rendering assistance to
scientists of both of their own and foreign nationality
who wish to make researches into Yami sociology.

**Question.** What do you think is the best way to treat the Yami?

**Answer** I think it would be almost impossible for the Japanese
Administration to improve on their present methods.
Throughout my stay I have been tremendously impressed
by the nature of the present administration which in
its treatment of the natives seems to me to have
achieved an ideal standard unsurpassed by any colonial
power anywhere in the world. In my opinion the
following are the principal points which should be
considered in the administration of primitive native
peoples.

1) The culture and social behaviour of the
native have been created by the native himself
to enable himself to make adjustment with the
particular environment in which he lives.
If these cultural regulations are changed the
native will in all probability be unable to
adjust himself to his environment and for one
reason or another the race will die out.

=== Page 9 === (confidence 1.00)
-4-

This pattern of circumstances has occurred again and again in European colonies in various parts of the world. The European colonists have as a rule sought to impose both their laws and their religion on the primitive society with which they live and the result has been the complete breakdown of the native society concerned. The culture of the colonising power is only superior to that of the native in the sense that it stronger; it is most unlikely that the native can ever easily adapt himself to fit in with the colonists idea of culture. It is very rash therefore ever to think of "Civilising" the native. Clothes will bring disease to a race which has accustomed itself to do without them; changes of food will similarly destroy their health; and more serious still all changes in law and social morality will tend to bring about the complete disruption of the society concerned. I therefore think it wholly admirable that the Japanese authorities in Koto-sho have avoided the temptation to "civilise" the natives and changes their social customs and behaviour.

2) Against all that it must be recognised that whenever a primitive culture is contact with a stronger colonizing culture changes in the social structure are bound to take place whatever the intention of the administration, and the native society must necessarily collapse unless the natives are given means to cope with the changing circumstances of their lives. In Koto-sho the authorities, while carefully avoiding any attack on native custom, are nevertheless teaching the new generation of children to speak the Japanese language and to accustom themselves to Japanese ways of thought. These children therefore while still retaining their belief in the simple world of their forefathers are yet coming to be more and more equipped to deal with the changing conditions of the new world with which they are now coming into contact. The English have a proverb "Make haste slowly". In my opinion it should be the motto of all colonial administrators; and it is one which the Japanese in Koto-sho seem to have appreciated better than any other colonial power of my experience.

In submitting the following comments on my visit to Koto-sho as a member of the Second Stewart Psycho-Ethnographic Expedition, I would like first to express my gratitude to the Government of Taihoku for their courtesy in enabling me to enjoy this privilege. Apart from my work in connection with the Expedition I was immensely impressed by the harmony of the relations between the Yami and the Japanese authorities. A factor that makes this particularly striking is that the Yami are an independent, individualistic people, with a highly stabilised culture. For a method of government that has made possible constructive control without upsetting the traditions of such a people an onlooker must feel the warmest admiration. Another feature that strikes a visitor is the beneficial effects of the medical attention that is being made available for the natives. I left Koto-sho with the feeling that a visit to this southernmost part of the Japanese Empire is profitable in more than a strictly psycho-ethnographic sense.

(The following report by Miss Nancy Grasby appeared in Japanese in the Taiwan Nichi Nichi of December 1, 1937.)

Roughly forty miles east of the southernmost tip of Formosa is the island of Koto-sho. Before Koto-sho became part of the Japanese Empire it was known as Botel Tobago, and there are references to it under this name in old Chinese records. Apart from these references, little or nothing appears to be known about the early history of the island. Thirty-five miles in circumference, with a coastline broken by bays and scarred with fantastic rock formations, it is an island of mountains that rise abruptly from a narrow coastal plain and in places sheer from the sea. Geologically it appears to be volcanic in origin: the soil is for the most part a rich decomposed basalt and the most rugged of the coastal cliffs bear evidence of a marine volcanic deposit that a later series of movements hurled some hundreds of feet above sea level. The beaches of the island are covered with grey, water-worn pebbles and small boulders, among which the sea has washed an infinite variety of broken coral formations; and the rocks near the shore that are exposed when the tide is low are covered with many-coloured coral growths. Between the sea and the terraces of taro that occupy the rich alluvial land at the base of and on the lower slopes of the mountains are belts of wild pineapple whose twisted trunks and ragged dark green leaves are strangely picturesque and throw into stronger relief the flat green of the sea and the brilliant grass green of the hills near the shore. In the deep gullies of the mountains there are tree ferns and ornamental shrubs and edible berries and coconut palms and various kinds of bananas; and in the forest land chains of banyans linked together with a broken wall of aerial roots, the ornamental teal tree with its sprays of glossy scarlet leaves, the valuable wood of which no native will use because of the evil consequences that are supposed to attend contact with the tree, trees with large wax-like white flowers, trees with clusters of mauve flowers, hardwoods, and innumerable other trees from which the wood for houses and boats and household utensils is obtained. The forests of Koto-sho are eerie places, with their tangled vines and grostesque fungi, their stillness and their damp. In the belts near the shore land crabs bearing on their backs a gastropod-like shell that conceals everything except a formidable claw or two by means of which they are able to proceed with surprising rapidity, live in holes under the tree roots; and pale, creamy-green snails cling to the leaves of succulent lily-like plants.

This is the home of the Yami, a dark-skinned, small statured people of mixed racial origin. Some 1700 in number, they live in seven compact villages of straw-thatched houses that are distributed round the island on the gently rising plain between the seashore

=== Page 14 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

seashore and the mountains. The population is kept at a fairly even level by malaria, of which a particularly dangerous form is prevalent, though the natives have developed an immunity which enables them to withstand its attacks without visible inconvenience.

There are many interesting things about the Yami. In the first place they are a complete ethnic group, apparently bearing no relation to the natives of Formosa and possessing a conservative and highly stabilised culture that has probably existed in its present form for many hundreds and possibly thousands of years. Since the island came under Japanese control a handful of police have been stationed on the island, a school has been built, which the children must attend and at which those from the more distant villages live as weekly boarders, and a doctor has been appointed to tackle the malaria problem and give general medical care; but, in their wisdom, the authorities have not interfered with the customs of the people and there is an atmosphere of friendliness and co-operation that must excite the admiration of any visitor. The Yami social system is a democracy that, had I not seen it in the flesh, I would not have believed could exist except in the mind of a Utopian idealist. It is distressing to imagine what might have happened had a people as intelligent, as proudly independent, as peaceful and as happy as the Yami fallen into clumsy hands. There are no laws except custom, and there are a minority of social offences, the reason for conformity to a line of conduct that is acceptable to your neighbours appearing to be the simple one that to act otherwise would not be polite. There is deference for age - the accumulation of experience through the years must entitle the opinion of an old person to more respect than that of his juniors; but, there being no laws there is no need for administrators of the law and no person has authority over another. A dispute sometimes necessitates discussion by all the members of a village, and when this need arises a conference is called, for the duration of which the wisest and strongest person in the village is chosen to preside. His authority ceases, however, when the conference ends. It is noteworthy that when the Japanese suggested the appointment of Yami police, who would receive salaries and certain other privileges not enjoyed by their neighbours, the suggestion was opposed and the matter was dropped.

The marriage system is peculiarly advanced. Marriages are, as a general rule, arranged by the parents when the parties to the marriage are in early childhood. When the children are old enough to marry the parents of the bridegroom present the bride with a necklace, and at any time after the bestowal of this gift the bride may be summoned to a feast at the home of

=== Page 15 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

of the bridegroom, which is the only other formality required for the marriage to be legal. The interesting feature of such a marriage is that though the bride must for the next few months attend her husband's house daily and cook for him and perform other domestic duties, he cannot exercise any other marital rights until she expresses a wish for the marriage to be consummated. She may return every night to the home of her parents and if, after an interval which is considered sufficient to have given the feelings of the couple a fair trial, she is still unwilling to accept her parent's choice, the marriage is dissolved by her returning the necklace and going back to her parent's home to live. The husband has an equal right to repeat a bride who does not please him. After this tribute to parental authority both parties are free to choose a partner.

Another striking feature of the Yami social system is the method by which a rough equality of land ownership is maintained. There is no objection to a man's owning as many taro patches as he can acquire, but since there is no such thing as hired labour his property is limited to the area that he, or rather his wife, for women do the field work, - can manage. This has an intimating side-issue: a man who is unmarried must work his own fields, so that what is virtually a bachelor tax is in operation.

It might be thought that women upon whom the burden of manual work fell would be poor servile creatures; but this does not appear to be so. They are debarred the higher intellectual pursuits of their men folk - they know little or nothing of the complicated Yami theory of the nine layers which make up the universe, or the traditional stories that trace back to a Flood period and explain the involved ritual that accompanies the ceremony of birth and the seasonal ceremonies that punctuate the Yami calendar and occasion the only holidays they know, but in their personal relationship they maintain a sturdy independence. There is strong family affection and in the responsibility of caring for the children the father takes a full share. He is an extremely important person at the birth ceremony - it is he who must prepare the millet for the first meal eaten by his wife after her delivery, bury the bamboo stick, which is the only obstetrical instrument used and which he has in all probability made ready weeks before, at a point under the front saves of the house, and take an active part in the ceremony of baptism and the birth feast - and while his wife works in the fields he adds the duty of minding the young children to the various crafts that occur his time. He is an excellent craftsman, and there is very little rough workmanship in the baskets and "carrying" jackets he weaves from rattan, the hats he makes for himself and his family of coconut or nanana fibre or rattan, the wooden household utensils, and the nets and spears used in fishing.

fishing. Almost the only craft which he does not undertake is the weaving of material for the loin cloths but, except on festive occasions when a jacket of the sea material is worn, are the only garment of a man, and of the oblongs of cloth worn by the women as skirts and capes.

The complicated weave of Yami bark-fibre cloth is inconruous in the light of the simplicity of some of their domestic arrangements and their rudimentary culinary skill. Cooking is done in earthenware pots on an open fireplace and I did not hear of any other method of preparing food except by boiling it. Indeed, the whole diet is extremely simple, - except at festivals when there may be pig or goat or chicken, that family sits down three times a day to biled tubers and fish boiled in a quantity water. Fish soup is served at every meal and is the only drink except water. Alcohol beverages are unknown and there is no substitute for tobacco. Of fruits there are bananas and wild berries, and it is probable that the quantity of seaweed eaten helps to maintain health.

Possibly the description sounds drab. It is certainly the picture of a hardworking people, but it is also the picture, though a very incomplete one, of a happy people. Their dislike of strife is not apathy, or the inertia caused by over-work. Their folk-lore is rich, imaginative and sophisticated. They have an excellent sense of rhythm, which is evidenced in the dances of the young girls - men and married women who have children do not dance. They sing a great deal; and the simplicity of their song themes is reminiscent of Chinese poetry. Here is a sample:

Song about the grass from which Yami hats are made. - "The grass is very beautiful, so beautiful that when I made a hat from it visitors wanted to buy it and take it to Taito. (Taito is used generally by the Yami for any place not on Koto-sho). I sold my hat and now I am very sorry because I will not be able to wear it at the Flying Fish Festival. If I had it I would feel happy and well dressed when I went to catch flying fish, and I would not mind if the waves were rough. Now that I have sold my hat I cannot keep my mind on my work."

The Yami make bowls and pots of clay, some of them graceful and beautiful in design. They also make little figurines of clay. These, we were told, had no religious significance but were purely a fumbling for artistic expression. I would like to have discovered more about their work with clay, but like everything else in the Yami calendar where is a set time for this work and I was not there at the right time. One figurine that I saw, of an ape-like figure in whose immovable clasp a miniature reproduction strained as feeble strength might have been an Epstein interpretation of the conflict between

The expedition during which the above observations were made was conducted by Dr. Kilton R. Stewart, who was formerly a Psychological Research Fellow at the Peking Union Medical College. Other members of the expedition were Mr. E. R. Leach and Mr. T. Trevor. The programme of psycho-ethnographic work arranged by Dr. Stewart included the giving of mental tests and the collection of data that would throw light on mental processes. The mental tests revealed an intelligence quotient on the part of the Yami comparable with that of the American children on whom the tests had been standardised. A report on the expedition, which lasted from and of December until March 19, during which time the festival that is the keystone of Yami culture was held, is in course of preparation.

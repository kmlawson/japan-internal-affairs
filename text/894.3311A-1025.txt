Mr. Summerlin:

At a recent interdepartmental committee meeting which I attended as the representative of the Secretary of State, it was decided to make a recommendation to the President as follows:

"The State Department to consider, in consultation with the Navy Department, withholding from Japanese public vessels permission to visit ports in the Hawaiian Islands, other than Honolulu."

In view of the foregoing would you please be so good as to confer with FE in regard to any communications from the Japanese Embassy which may come to your Division involving the question of visits of Japanese public vessels to Hawaii?

Hugh R. Wilson

DEPARTMENT OF STATE
DIVISION OF FAR EASTERN AFFAIRS

June 8, 1938.

The attached letter from the Navy Department relates to an unpleasant incident which occurred in Honolulu when an employee of the local telephone company was subjected to discourteous treatment at the hands of a Japanese sailor on the Japanese naval tanker Hayatomo when he came to remove a telephone which had been installed in the vessel during its stay at Honolulu. Following the incident, the president of the telephone company informed the Japanese Consul General that unless a suitable apology was received from the commanding officer and unless future applications to furnish telephone service to similar vessels carried satisfactory guarantees against a repetition of the incident the company would decline to furnish telephone service in future to such vessels.

The case would seem to call for no action by the Department.

"Honorable Kosaku Midzusawa
Consul-General of Japan
1742 Nuuanu Avenue,
Honolulu, T. H.

"Dear Mr. Midzusawa:

"On Thursday, May 5th, 1938, at 8:05 A. M., a representative of the Japanese Chamber of Commerce telephoned Mutual Telephone Company and requested that a telephone be installed on board the Japanese naval tanker "Hayatomo", which was docked at U. S. Naval Pier 5A, Honolulu harbor. The telephone was installed, tested out and working at 1:45 P. M. the same day.

"On Saturday, May 7th, 1938, at about 8:00 P. M., some one, apparently on board the J.I.N. 'Hayatomo', called our chief operator

=== Page 4 === (confidence 1.00)
and advised her that the boat was sailing the next day and that we should send somebody for the telephone at 1:30 P.M.

"On Sunday, May 8th, 1938, at about 1:25 P.M., one of our repairmen, Mr. Brian Bedford, a native of Pennsylvania, started up the gang plank of the 'Hayatomo' for the purpose or removing the telephone. At the same time a number of Japanese in civilian clothes were being allowed to board the 'Hayatomo', apparently without question. Our repairman, however, was stopped by an armed guard. He showed the guard his official telephone company badge and asked the assistance of two of the Japanese civilians who were going aboard to explain his mission. This was done very politely. Mr. Bedford also pointed to his telephone company automobile with the telephone company insignia on its side in a further effort to identify himself.

"Notwithstanding, however, the sentry still refused to let Mr. Bedford pass. Mr. Bedford then appealed to a member of the United States Shore Patrol, who accompanied him back to the gang plank. The guard then called a Japanese officer who, with the assistance of Japanese civilians who were still boarding and leaving the ship without question, advised Mr. Bedford that he would not be allowed on board and that 'perhaps the next time the 'Hayatomo' visited Honolulu they would return the telephone'.

"Our repairman then left the gang plank and went to his car with the intention of going to the United States Shore Patrol office for assistance in recovering our property. At this time, a Japanese in civilian clothes left the ship and came over to the company's automobile. He asked our repairman if he wanted the telephone. The answer being 'yes', the Japanese civilian said, 'All right, come with me'. Mr. Bedford accompanied the civilian, who, after considerable discussion in Japanese with the armed guard, succeeded in getting him aboard.

"On removing the telephone, Mr. Bedford started to leave the ship but was stopped by a Japanese sailor who searched him as though he were a criminal. The sailor also snatched a route book (memorandum book carried by repairmen to keep a record of the calls they have to make) from Mr. Bedford's hands, thumbed through it and shook it open before returning it.

"We feel the foregoing facts present a situation requiring that we bring it to your attention. We are confident that you will agree with us that there is no justification or extenuation whatever for the events as related. It is quite possible that the Commanding Officer of the 'Hayatomo' was entirely unaware of the incident. Nevertheless, until a suitable apology is received from the Commanding Officer, we shall decline to furnish telephone service to similar vessels. Upon receipt of such apology, we shall be agreeable to receiving applications for telephone service to Japanese naval vessels

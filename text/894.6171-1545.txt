The title is amended as follows: "The Law for the Cultivation of the Forestry Resources."

In Article 1, the wording "at the time of the Greater East Asia War" and "for the consolidating into a war effort and" are deleted.

In Article 7, "After the delivery of the Bond" is amended to "After the delivery of the Bond other than those cases mentioned in the preceding paragraph", and following paragraph is provided as the Paragraph 1 of this Article.

"If there would be any transfer of ownership, between the issuance of the order stipulated in Paragraph 1 of Article 5 and the fulfilment of the afforestation conduct due to the order, the new owner of the forest shall be authorized to succeed the rights and obligations of the former owner who has received the Bond."

Supplementary Provision

The present law shall come into force as from the day of its promulgation.

FOREWORD

1. This report is one of a series of six Natural Resources Section Reports describing the forest resources of Japan. The areas covered by the others are Kyushu, Shikoku, southern Honshu, central Honshu, and Hokkaido. This form of division was determined by the fact that the Japanese Bureau of Forestry administers the forests of Japan through six district offices covering these areas. These reports are being prepared so that the staff sections of General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and the Military Government teams of the Eight Army in the field will have a source of information as a guide in determining policy regarding forestry in Japan. Included in the report is a broad inventory of the standing timber by ownership and location, production trends in major forest industries, and a brief discussion of the methods used in logging and milling.

2. The statistics on which the report is based were supplied by the prefectural forestry offices and by the Akita and Aomori district offices of the Japanese Bureau of Forestry. They were supplemented by spot checks made during a field trip to the area and with data already available in the files of the Natural Resources Section. Their accuracy is limited by the difficulties encountered in converting Japanese units into American standards and by the fact that Japanese records do not directly present the information desired. In the case of forest industries, such as lumber production and charcoal making, in which a large number of marginal producers are moving in and out of production, the statistics on the number of producers and amounts of production change daily. Trends indicated by the data are substantially correct, and should serve as an index as to the amount and kind of timber in the area and the status of production as compared to previous years.

SUMMARY

1. Of the total land area of the prefectures of Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Akita, and Yamagata, 64 percent is classified as forest land. These forests, situated in the Deciduous Broadleaf or Temperate Zone 1/ and in the Conifer or Cold Zone 1/, contain approximately nine billion cubic feet 2/ of standing timber with 69 percent of this amount in broadleaf species.

2. This area is among the leading lumber producing areas in Japan. Although the five prefectures contain only about 600 sawmills, among them are some of the largest and most modern in the country. These mills produced an average of 432,000,000 board feet 3/ annually in the years 1935 - 1939 and 525,000,000 board feet per annum during 1940 - 1945.

3. Tables, charts, and maps showing volume by ownership, volume by types, production, and the centers of forest industries are included for reference.

RESOURCES

A. General

1. The Akita and Aomori forest districts which include the prefectures of Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Akita, and Yamagata, are among the most heavily forested areas of Japan. Sixty-four percent of the land area of the five prefectures, or slightly more than eight million acres, is in forests (Table 1). This represents about nine percent of the total forest area of Japan, including Hokkaido.

2. Classified as to climatic zones, the forests of northern Honshu below 1,000 meters are in the Deciduous Broadleaf or Temperate Zone 4/, while those above 1,000 meters are in the Conifer or Cold Zone. The principal commercial species found in this area are: (1) Sugi (Cryptomeria Japonica); (2) hiba (Thujopsis dolobrata); (3) karamatsu (Larix leptolepis); (4) akamatsu (Pinus densiflora); (5) buna (Fagus sp); and (6) nara (Quercus crispula or Q glandulifera).

B. Ownership

1. Slightly more than 50 percent of the forest area of northern Honshu is state-owned. The second largest owners are private interests and communities, which own 31 percent and 14 percent respectively. The remaining area belongs to the prefectures, shrines and temples, and the Imperial Household (Table 1).

2. When considered from the point of volume of standing timber, the volume on state-owned land far exceeds that of other owners, more than 80 percent of the total. Twelve percent of the volume is on private forests, five percent on community forests, and the remainder is scattered among the groups mentioned in Table 1.

C. Volume and Growth

1. In the Aomori and Akita forest districts is an estimated stand of slightly over nine billion cubic feet of timber. This includes all species, sizes, and age classes, some of which are immature and others which are suitable for use only in charcoal-making or as firewood. Of this volume, about 69 percent is hardwood species and the remainder is the softwood variety (Table 2).

2. The cut for the fiscal year of 1945 (1 April 1945 to 31 March 1946) was slightly more than 241,000,000 cubic feet. The test estimates place annual growth in this area at about 160,000,000 cubic feet per annum. This indicates that in the fiscal year of 1945 the cut was equal to approximately three percent of the total growing stock and was nearly 50 percent greater than the annual growth. This apparently heavy overcut is not of too great significance owing to the fact that large amounts of the total growing stock of this area is confined to mature natural stands in which little if any growth has taken place.

A. General

1. The administration of forest land in northern Honshu is divided among three agencies: the Bureau of Forestry (Sanrinkyoku), the prefectural forest offices (Rinmuka), and the Imperial Estates Bureau. Of these the Bureau of Forestry, which administers the State forests, is by far the most important. Next in importance is the prefectural forest offices, which have general supervision over private forests, community forests, prefectural forests, and the forests of shrines and temples.

2. Since the Imperial forests in the area are so small, their administration will not be discussed.

2. In Aomori and Akita forest districts, where the large percentage of forests is owned by the State, functioning of the prefectural forestry office is secondary. It is more or less dominated by policies of the district forest office (sirinkyoku) under whose jurisdiction it falls. Once again the need for a unified forest administration is apparent.

FOREST MANAGEMENT

A. Management Plans

1. Forest management, according to a prepared plan, is confined almost exclusively to State forests. Many of the private forest owners speak of planned cutting. The plan, however, exists not on paper but in their minds and consists merely of planting, waiting the required number of years, and clear-cutting the area. The process is then repeated.

2. The current trend is toward having management plans for private forests drawn up by the forest associations with the aid and approval of the prefectural forestry office.

B. Silviculture

1. Four systems of silvercultural treatment are practiced in northern Honshu: (1) clear-cut and plant; (2) coppice; (3) group selection; and (4) individual tree selection. It is difficult to evaluate these systems as to frequency of use.

2. In the case of old age natural forests of Aomori and Akita, the group selection system is the most widely used. This consists of clear-cutting small patches from one tenth of an acre to 1½ acres in the area. Treatment after cutting varies. In some cases, the area is allowed to seed in naturally; in other cases the area is cleared and planted the year following the cut. The cutting cycle is between 20 and 30 years.

3. Hardwoods that are used for fuelwood are managed as coppice. The area is clear-cut and sprouting is relied on for regrowth. The rotation age is between 15 and 30 years.

4. Clear-cutting is the most popular form of silviculture among private owners. The tracts are small, the timber is grown on a short rotation (35-60 years), this form of management requires no large store of technical knowledge, and it is inexpensive in the eyes of the

5. Individual tree selection is discussed frequently by the Japanese foresters, but the only instance observed where it was being used was in the natural hiba (Thujopsis dolobrata) forests of Aomori. Here the cut was 20 percent of the total volume, and trees selected were in most cases aged 130 - 150 years with the breast high diameter being from 56 - 64 centimeters. Selection was based on the desire to obtain the greatest amount of merchantable timber and to leave a many-aged, many-storied stand.

## LOGGING METHODS AND EQUIPMENT

### A. General

1. Although Akita and Aomori forest districts are among the most productive in Japan and the timber harvested is some of the largest on Honshu, hand logging methods are used. The basic woods tools are single-bitted, long-handled axes and one-man crosscut saws (Figure 1). Large-scale mechanical aid is limited to the logging railroads that are used extensively on State forests. These roads are narrow gauge and are permanent installations within the forests. They are well constructed, and maintenance is good. Motive power is supplied by internal combustion charcoal engines and in some cases by wood-burning steam locomotives of conventional design. The empty cars are hauled into the woods by these engines, loaded, and returned to the flat by gravity where the engine is employed once more to move cars into storage yards.

2. In order to move the largest sized logs from the place of felling to the railroad spur, sleds (kimma) are used (Figure 2). These are moved over a skid road that is built by placing round logs about six inches in diameter and four feet in length in a fashion similar to that used in laying railroad ties. The sleepers are lubricated with water and the sleds moved over them by a combination of manpower and gravity.

C. Charcoal

1. Production: The Aita and Aomori forest districts have slightly less than 50,000 charcoal kilns (Table 5); production capacity at this time is about 368,000 metric tons of charcoal per annum. At present the output is about 72 percent of maximum. The average annual production for 1935 - 1939 was slightly more than 418,000 metric tons. From 1940 - 1945, production averaged more than 391,500 metric tons annually (Table 6). The peak production year was 1940 with a gradual decrease to the low of 1945 (Figure 8). Since charcoal is produced in widely scattered places, the accuracy of the figures quoted above is limited, but the trends indicated are correct.

2. Requirements: In spite of the fact that severe winters cause a high domestic demand, all the northern Honshu prefectures are surplus areas and export to the south. As was the case with lumber, this export demand causes the total demand from northern Honshu to be almost unlimited.

3. Manufacture: Charcoal is made in crudely constructed kilns situated in the woods near the source of raw materials (Figure 9).

These kilns are generally operated by one or two families who do all the work involved, from the cutting of the wood to the baling of the charcoal. One of the greatest difficulties in increasing production is the lack of food. Since the kilns are deep in the woods, no land to grow food is available and the workers must depend on an outside supply for their rations. This supply is scanty and erratic. As a result many of the charcoal makers have left the woods to work on farms where the labor is no harder, and rations are more dependable.

## D. Railroad Ties and Mine Props

1. Production: During 1935-1939, production of mine props averaged about 2,000,000 cubic feet yearly, while that of railroad ties was slightly more than 1,300,000 cubic feet. From 1940 to 1945, the average annual production was 3,356,000 cubic feet of mine props and 1,320,000 cubic feet of railroad ties (Tables 7 and 8). Peak production was achieved in 1942 and like all forest industries, the low point was reached in 1945 (Figure 10).

2. Requirements: Exact data are not available as to the needs for mine props and railroad ties within the prefectures being considered. However, because of Japan's obligation to ship mine props to China and railroad ties to Korea, demand exceeds present production.

3. Manufacture: The manufacture of both railroad ties and mine props is relatively simple. In the case of mine props, it is done in the woods and consists of cutting them to the proper sizes and in some cases, peeling the bark. A large portion of the railroad ties is hewed. This also takes place in the woods. The major problems facing the industry are shortage of food for the workers and lack of transportation to move products from the place of manufacture to the consumer.

E. Pulpwood

1. Production: The production of pulpwood in this district averaged 828,000 cubic feet per annum for 1935 - 1939 and about 3,000,000 cubic feet per annum for 1940 - 1945 (Table 9). The peak year was 1942; the lowest production was in 1945 (Figure 11).

2. Requirements: The main consuming agencies of pulpwood in this area are the Tohoku Shinko Rayon Pulp mills located in Akita and Miyagi Prefectures. When working at a maximum rate they consume over 9,000,000 cubic feet of pulpwood annually. This is well over the maximum production of pulpwood in the surrounding area. Now, because of coal and chemical shortages, the mills are running at about 30 percent capacity and at this rate would need slightly less than 3,000,000 cubic feet of pulpwood per annum. This figure is equal to the planned production of pulpwood in the Akita-Aomori Districts during 1946. The amount of pulpwood cut would appear to be sufficient unless an increase is made in coal and chemical allotments to the industry. As far as the wood supply is concerned, the main difficulty is getting sufficient transportation to move wood from the cutting place to the mill.

The Foreign Service of the United States of America
American Consulate
Midden, China
October 21, 1948

CONFIDENTIAL

Subject: Cost of Living at Changchun. Anticipated Duplication of Changchun Economic Situation at Mukden.

To the American Ambassador, Nanking.

The Consul General has the honor to transmit herewith a very interesting and timely memorandum prepared by Vice Consul Allen G. SIERENS on October 19, 1948, and entitled "Cost of Living at Changchun. Anticipated Duplication Economic Situation Changchun at Mukden".

According to the best information available today, Changchun fell to the communists two days ago, after approximately a year and a half of isolation of varying degrees of intensity, and for months prior to its capitulation the death toll among the civil population from exposure, lack of medical attention and starvation had been appalling.

If Mukden weathers the presently gathering storm and the Government troops withdraw to this base and go into garrison here, it may be that they will cast away any opportunity they now have to withdraw from Manchuria. In such case there is strong likelihood that Mukden will become another Changchun to wither and rot. Should such likelihood materialize conditions here will be as bad or worse than those which have held sway at Changchun.

These conditions, if no other, will determine whether this Consulate General should remain open until Mukden falls into Chinese communist hands. Should Mukden become tightly encircled by the communists it will probably lose access not only to food supplies, but, like Changchun, also to coal. It would then become necessary to airlift coal as well as food to Mukden for the needs of the office and the staff. Once the airfields at Mukden come under communist fire or occupation, the only means of supplying the office and staff would be through airdrops, which have proved most wasteful at Changchun.

We should profit from the experiences of Changchun and should Mukden become closely invested by the communists we should then reappraise the advisability of keeping Consulate General Mukden open until the city passes into communist hands. It would seem that, should Mukden remain in Government hands that long, the time for such reappraisal will be the early spring months of 1949. Since it would be very cruel, if not fatal, to the staff to keep the Mukden office open without fuel, and if fuel for the winter of 1949-50 is not obtainable during the spring of 1949 experience shows that there is not much likelihood it will become available during the summer or autumn, such reappraisal can be based on the availability of coal during the coming spring.

The above figures clearly indicate what happens to an urban economy under siege conditions in the Northeast. Changchun is now apparently within days of falling to the Communists. Column 3 prices above are of course of somewhat academic interest only. The price spiral which has produced these figures is recurring at Mukden, and is of course aggravated by the increasing loss of public faith in the National Government and its currency. During the past eight weeks the price of kaoliang, the commodity which sets the economic pace in Manchuria, has increased two and a half times at Mukden while the American dollar on the black market has increased only once, or doubled. This clearly demonstrates that the real cost of living in Mukden is rising rapidly

Other

* This cost is rising not only in Mukden, but in peripheral areas. A field trip to a village about 20 miles from Mukden on October 18, 1948, revealed that prices in that village and villages as much as twice as far from Mukden were as high and sometimes higher than those in Mukden itself, a phenomenon apparently caused by activities of bandits and by military requisitioning.

Other characteristics of the Changchun economy which can be expected to recur at Mukden if the Nationalists elect to make a determined stand in their last Manchurian bastion are as follows:

1. No coal has come into Changchun since October, 1947, and even at that time coal stocks within the city were limited to some 10,000 tons. Output of Nationalist coal mines in the Mukden area, while sufficient to meet minimal power and civilian needs, has not been sufficient to permit the building up of large stockpiles in Mukden except by a few official organs who have provided for heating their offices during the winter. These coal mines are all within easy reach of a Communist effort, and could be cut off from Mukden within a matter of a few days.

2. Production of electricity at Changchun ceased in October, 1947, and for six months previous to that time was sporadic and sufficient only for minimal official needs. The chief sources of power at Mukden are thermo-electric plants at the Penhsi and Fushun coal mines*, points which, as stated above, could now be isolated by Communist armies almost at will. This would leave Mukden completely dependent for electricity on a small local thermo-electric plant operated by the Mukden arsenal for its own requirements, a plant of which the top productive capacity is only 1500 kw, and for the operation of which coal reserves would in any case be extremely limited.

3. There has been no city water supply at Changchun since May of 1947. Mukden water supplies are dependent on three reservoirs which must be filled by electric pumps. For the past months these reservoirs have been kept filled to approximately half capacity only because of power shortages. It is obvious, therefore, that Mukden would be forced to rely on wells in the event of power failure caused by Communist seizure or isolation of Fushun and Penhsi.

In the meantime, coal and electricity costs at Mukden already have commenced rapid rises**, and coal particularly, because it is controlled semi-privately, will reach fantastic costs if Mukden is cut off from its coal supplies.

By the time any close siege of the Mukden area has developed, it can be expected that the black market (e.g. demand) for US dollars will decline rapidly and ultimately disappear almost completely. By that time those able to use dollars will have left the city, communications with other points will have reached a minimum if they exist at all, and the local economy will be oriented entirely toward potential Communist control of Mukden, a control which will be expected to enforce bans on the circulation and possession of US currency and under which gold will be the chief "hard" medium of exchange.

It is thus obvious that in terms of US dollars the present rapid rise in the cost of living at Mukden will increase and ultimately become prohibitive if the Nationalists decide to hold Mukden as long as possible. Events of the next few weeks probably will indicate whether the Nationalists are going to make a stand at Mukden or decide to abandon the city and salvage such troops as they are able to save for continued resistance in China proper. The immediate economic future of Mukden will be vitally affected by this Nationalist decision.

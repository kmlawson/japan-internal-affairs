Letters to The Citizen

Letters to appear in this column must be signed for publication with true signatures of the writers. Preference is given to brief communications but the editor does not undertake to publish all letters received.

Japanese Answer to Archdeacon

Editor, Citizen: Archdeacon F. O. Scott, Canadian war-time padre, is reported to have said at Toronto, on November, 17, 1937, that he was "informed on good authority" that Japanese naval officers in disguise are living "in so-called Japanese fishing villages in British Columbia:"

There are now 40,000 Japanese in Vancouver alone and Japan knows every inch of our Western coast, besides owning enormous timber limits in those regions. Japan also has large vessels engaged in the salmon industry there. They may belong to the class of mystery ships which England used in the war.

I do not know whether the press report is accurate or not. Anyhow, I can assure you that such a statement is utterly groundless and malicious propaganda against Japan. At the same time the report betrays the astonishing ignorance of the inventor of this shocking fabrication. I cannot believe that such a fine gentleman as Archdeacon Scott of Canada is responsible for the propagation of this most ridiculous news.

Allow me to speak frankly for the better friendship between Japan and Canada.

Do you think there is anything that British Columbia has of such importance to make it worth while to be found out by "disguised Japanese naval officers"? Do you think that the Japanese navy must study something in British Columbia by such silly methods?

Anyone who has the least knowledge of the navy, especially of the Imperial Japanese navy, will surely laugh at the idea.

I am sure you are generous enough to understand that when saying this I am not lacking in my high respect and admiration for the well-established Canadian national defence.

There is one more question I should like to ask. Do you believe that "knowing every inch of Canadian Western coast" Japan still has to keep her "disguised naval officers in British Columbia"?

If there is any foundation to such a report, I hope the authorities concerned will at once arrest those threatening, criminal "disguised Japanese naval officers." It is the best short cut. Having such "information on good authority" it is negligence on the part of the Canadian authorities not to arrest them at once.

So far as I know, the total population of Japanese in Canada is about 20,000 and in Vancouver it is about 7,000. The number is decreasing these years, 40,000 in Vancouver only is too ridiculous. The latest authoritative statistics will tell you the exact number.

Although I have very little knowledge about timber business in Canada, I do not think Japanese in British Columbia are allowed to "own too enormous timber limits" there. Anyhow, Japan is now the best customer of the B.C. timber. Ever since the outbreak of the Swo-Japanese conflict we hear repeated complaints of the Canadian (not Japanese) lumber merchants against the depression of lumber exports to Japan from British Columbia. Although it is somewhat amusing in the light of "trade boycott against Japan" that such complaints come from the Canadians and not from the Japanese who are reported to have "enormous limits of timber."

As for the "threat" of the "large Japanese vessels" engaged in the B.C. salmon industry, I think it is enough to ask you just to look at the special fishing regulations for the province of B.C. and the latest statistics of the fishing boats in British Columbia. You will find there that the reality is far different from the "well informed" report — COMMANDER K. TAKEUCHI, Japanese Legation, Ottawa, Nov. 18, 1937.

# Says Department Is Kept Well Informed On Coastal Matters

Hon. Ian Mackenzie Declares Activities of Japanese Being Watched—Urges Private Citizens To Report on Matters of Defence

“THE matter is not a new one to my department. We have an intelligence department and it is well informed upon all matters affecting the safety and well-being of the country,” declared Hon. Ian Mackenzie, Minister of National Defence, when questioned regarding statements concerning activities of the Japanese on the Canadian Pacific Coast yesterday.

“If private citizens have information of importance to the defence of their country, I think it is their duty to furnish it to the Department of National Defence. It will be cordially welcomed,” the minister continued.

“It is the policy of the Canadian Government, and I am sure the desire of the Canadian people, to live at peace with all nations. Recognizing the disturbed condition of the world, the Government has, during the past year, initiated an extensive defence programme, in which the Pacific Coast has a prominent place.”

## TO AID LIBERALS

Mr. Mackenzie is here to take part in the Federal by-election campaign in this city on behalf of R. W. Mayhew, Liberal candidate, and will speak this evening at 8 o’clock at the Royal Victoria Theatre, when Premier T. D. Premier and Hon. John Hart will also be heard.

Speaking earlier in the day in Vancouver, the Federal minister said this year’s estimates provided for 102 new airplanes which the department was having constructed. Most of the planes will be built in Canada, while the rest will be built in England. Included are training ships, service fighting planes, bombers and reconnaissance planes.

Eleven machines to be built in Canada will be constructed by the Boeing Aircraft Company at Vancouver.

During his stay here, the minister will inspect a number of defence projects which are now under fence projects. He is accompanied by his secretary, C. Norman Senior.

# British Columbia Court

## MAY EXCHANGE DATA ON 'SPYING' WITH U.S. IS BELIEF AT CAPITAL

Naval Officers Disguised as Fishermen, Padre Scott Is Told

FAVORS U.S. INFLUX

By R. W. LIPSETT

Ottawa, Nov. 17.—Hon. Ian Mackenzie, minister of national defence, reached Vancouver to-day. Before he returns to Ottawa early in December, it is believed here he will go thoroughly into the causes of the latest wave of apprehension on the Pacific coast arising out of charges of Japanese espionage activities in that area.

While unrest and a certain degree of fear have frequently been manifest over Japanese "spying and plotting" on the western seaboard of Canada the present disturbance is unusually widespread and is held to demand more reassurance than statement that the intelligence service of the defence department is at work.

Mr. Mackenzie, it is said, will spend part of his two weeks at the coast in first hand inquiry not only into the reports of the intelligence service, but into the complaints of those responsible for spreading the general alarm. Reports that the United States is concerned over the activities of Jap "fishermen" in Alaska waters may lead to interchange of confidential information on the general subject between Washington and Ottawa.

The apprehension felt in British Columbia if found he have substantial basis may lead to important changes in defence estimates for next year. As a result of the acquisition of Radio Canada, a key defence position known as "Little Gibraltar," from "Catholic" Columbia, plans for its acquisition were last year prepared and senior defence works have already been established on it. Other plans of coast fortification are also in existence, but the amount voted for the current year was sufficient to permit only very little work being done on Atlantic and Pacific coasts.

The government is aware that some of the Jap fishermen on the B.C. coast were formerly in the Japanese navy but does not regard that as conclusive evidence that their present business is other than fishing. The disposition in Ottawa is not to regard Jap fishing vessels as "mystery ships." Most of them have been thoroughly inspected by competent Canadian officers and have been certified as fishing boats.

Government officials did not believe that the throwing down of immigration barriers would provide a solution of any danger which may threaten Canada. The capacity to absorb immigrants, it was claimed, limits to a degree that would require many decades to reach a point where there was adequate population to defend the country against a first-class power. In the meantime the entry of a heterogeneous mass of people might promote internal dissension and weaken present defensive strength.

## "Many Wild Statements"

Mayor George Miller of Vancouver, told The Star by telephone from Victoria to-day he believed information obtained by Archdeacon F. G. Scott regarding Japanese activities on the west coast was "highly colored."

"Out here we hear those things from time to time," Mayor Miller stated, "but we've never had anything official on it. There are many wild statements because many people are opposed to the Japanese because of their aggressive commercial activities."

Primary purpose of the present trip of Hon. Ian Mackenzie, minister of national defence, to Vancouver, Mr. Miller stated, was to take part in a by-election.

"Although," he added, "Mr. Mackenzie will go into the causes of charges of Japanese espionage activities here."

Mr. Miller didn't place a great deal of credence on Archdeacon Scott's information that many residents of so-called Japanese fishing villages were really Japanese naval officers in disguise.

"When you speak of the Japanese," Mayor Miller stated, "you are dealing with a very aggressive race. They don't pass up anything. It is true they hold tremendous timber limits in British Columbia.

"Out here the people resent the fact the Japanese started in the fishing industry in a small way and through aggressive tactics expanded to a point where they literally controlled the industry. They started in the timber industry in a small way. People resent the manner in which their interests in this industry are growing so rapidly. But I don't think there is anything significant in this—it's just resentment.

"Naturally there would be some reserve officers here," the mayor said. "No doubt there are Japanese who have served in the Japanese navy and are retired. Naturally they would take to the sea. But we've never taken charges they were engaged in spy work very seriously."

## Observant People

There were 40,000 Japanese in Victoria and the Japanese in the west probably knew every inch of Canada's coastline. Mayor Miller agreed with Archdeacon Scott.

"But why wouldn't the Japanese know every inch of the coastline?" They're up and down the coast all the time and they are a most observant people. How much the Japanese government would know about our coastline, I don't know.

In this connection Col. W. W. Foster, chief of police of Vancouver and vice-president of the Dominion Command of the Canadian Legion, told The Star over the telephone that anyone could get detailed information on Canada's western coast by buying an ordinary 50-cent map.

# WHY SPY ON CANADA?

Archdeacon SCOTT having been quoted as saying that Japanese naval officers in disguise were living in British Columbia coastal villages, there engaged in espionage, Minister of Defence IAN MACKENZIE retorts that this isn't news to him. "We have an intelligence service and it is well-informed upon all matters affecting Canada's safety."

This, to a layman, seems odd. If our Department of Defence knows or thinks that Japanese are engaged in espionage activities in Canada, why doesn't it draw the thing to the attention of Japan? Everybody knows that espionage systems exist, but we have never heard of a Government, in possession of information about foreign spies within its territory, failing to do something about them.

But we wonder if there are Japanese spies in Canada. What would they spy upon? This country is doing nothing that isn't an open book. Our public accounts (available to everybody) tell what we are spending on defence. And we have no secret war funds, no secret air or naval or military bases. Practically everything that we have in a war way, or that we are doing in a war way, is in our newspapers.

In the circumstances, why should the Japanese Government pay people to send them information about things of which they must be well aware already? Our coasts? They have been there ever since Japan has been Japan; Tokyo must know as much about Vancouver and Victoria and Esquimalt as Ottawa does.

The unfortunate effect of a spy scare is to cause otherwise sensible people to lose their sense of proportion. Thus a letter comes to THE JOURNAL from an Ottawa man who quotes from Archdeacon SCOTT's statement, goes on to say there may be "some significance" in his own observations--in the Summer of 1933 he saw two Japanese photographing the Montreal waterfront, the Windsor station and the Westmount city hall; in 1934 a Japanese took "at least twenty pictures" of the Chateau Laurier, the Ottawa Union Station and the Rideau Canal, and the following year two Japanese "spent an entire morning" photographing the Parliament Buildings. The most harmless of hobbies in the eye of suspicion becomes an iniquitous campaign by a foreign state.

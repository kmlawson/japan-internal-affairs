It is thought that the Japanese Navy would anticipate the immediate transfer of the main strength of the American Navy to the Philippines and that the Japanese Navy would make every effort during the passage of the American fleet through the Caroline and Marshall Islands to maintain continuous attack by means of submarines with the hope of cutting down the American force. During the recent session of the Diet, Baron Shidehara stated on several occasions that, although Japan had agreed to bring down its tonnage in submarines from the figure previously set as the minimum necessary for defensive purposes, there were several ways in which the deficiency in submarines could be made good. It may, therefore, be of no little interest to our Government to learn that extensive manoeuvres will be held by the air corps in the region through which it is supposed the submarines will operate in time of war.

EXTENSIVE AIR MANOEUVRES IN THE SOUTH SEAS.

Tokyo JIJI SHIMPO.
May 31, 1930.

The Oppama Air Squadron which succeeded in May of last year in making an unprecedented flight of 1200 miles, from Yokosuka to the Island of Saipan in the South Seas, will repeat the flight on June 14th if the weather be favorable. On this occasion six large sea planes will make the effort. This flight is due to the fact that the recent Naval Conference has made it absolutely necessary to develop the air force as a part of the general scheme of defense and consequently the object of this flight is different from that of the flight which took place last year. The object is that the squadron shall take part in manoeuvres to test actual war conditions and will operate in conjunction with the 2nd battle fleet which is now carrying on vigorous manoeuvres in the waters adjacent to Saipan.

There are thirty-six warships now operating in the South Seas and they are all the most effective ships in their respective classes. They include the four 10,000-ton ships, the ASHIGARA, HAGURO, NACHI and MYOKO, and only the most effective of the submarines. They will complete their operations and return north on or about the 18th or 19th of June, the Oppama Air Squadron returning to its base a day or so earlier.

American Embassy, Tokyo.

Dear Sir:

It has been unofficially decided that the Grand Naval Review of 1930 is to be held on October 26, 1930, off Kobe, and that invitations to attend the review will not be extended to foreign warships.

Since, before and after the Review, a congestion of ships is expected in Osaka and Kobe, you are requested to inform your Navy Department and the Commander in Chief of the Asiatic Fleet, to make arrangements so as to keep your warships away from that region.

Very sincerely yours,

M. Koga,
Aide, Navy Department.

The "Temps" of 1" will be published in article as follows:-

"The Japanese Admiralty is busy with the plans for the great naval maneuvers for the next fiscal year. The exercises will be the most important as yet realized in Japan. Six million yen were voted for this purpose and incorporated in the budget for 1935-1936.

The maneuvers will be divided into three parts under the direction of the Commander-in-Chief, Vice Admiral BANKICHI PAKHASHI. The exercises will begin off the coast of Japan and will spread progressively to the Coriles, Kamchotka and Juske. It is the first time that the Japanese Navy will open to us extensively in the Northern Pacific. The maneuvers of 1925 will be the crowning of a defensive program of three years. 192 warships of all categories will take part and 200 aeroplanes."

Maneuvers of the Japanese fleet.

In view of all of the points enumerated above, the recent reports which apparently leaked out, intentionally or otherwise, at Tokyo, Shanghai and Swatow to the effect that seventy-five ships of the Japanese Navy under the command of Admiral Takahashi would hold maneuvers in the very near future near the waters of the Celebes and the Moluccas tended to increase the fear of military aggression.

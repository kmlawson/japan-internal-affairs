SUBJECT: "Rule of the Road" in Japan

This memorandum is in reply to one of November 12, 1948, from Mr. Kelly, but in his absence I am addressing it to you.

Mr. Kelly asked us to find out whether the Army had considered the question of changing traffic in Japan from driving on the left to driving on the right. It appears that there is no file or detailed information on this subject in the Pentagon, but it is known that the question was considered by General MacArthur in 1945. However, since all the streetcars and tracks are equipped for driving on the left, the proposal was discarded as impractical, since it would have been a very large and expensive job to change them.

Question has been raised in Washington as to the possibility of useful action being taken at this time to change the direction of highway traffic in Japan from the present "drive to the left" to driving to the right.

There are few areas in the world in which road vehicles still are driven to the left, and these are principally in the British Commonwealth. According to a compilation prepared in the Department, the only countries in which traffic still moves to the left are as follows:

Europe - Sweden, the United Kingdom and Ireland.

Africa - Ethiopia, Kenya and Uganda, Mozambique, Northern Rhodesia and Southern Rhodesia, and Union of South Africa.

Asia - Burma, India, Japan, Ceylon, and Siam.

Oceania and Australia - Australia, and New Zealand.

The fact that Japan is now occupied by American forces, and that the traffic is reported to be composed predominantly of American-type military vehicles with standard drive, would seem to offer the best possible opportunity to effect a change from the drive-to-the-left practice. Indeed, statements by officers who have served in the occupation forces in Japan indicate that traffic in certain areas at least has moved to the right ever since the occupation began.

The principal argument usually adduced against the change-over (in such countries as the United Kingdom, for example) has been the cost of changing certain fixed installations such as streetcar frogs and switches, overhead wire connections, signs and signals, et cetera.

In international conferences for many years, British representatives have acknowledged that the drive-to-the-right is now followed by the great majority of countries, and that an eventual change-over is inevitable in the others, but have pleaded that they are unable to make the adjustment because of cost. The subject again is expected to be discussed at an international conference which will be held in August 1949 by the United Nations to conclude an international convention on road traffic.

The Department has discussed informally with the Department of the Army the question of changing traffic in Japan, and has been informed that the question was considered by General MacArthur in 1945 but was discarded as impractical. There is no file on the subject in either Department, however. It would be appreciated if a statement could be given the Department covering the extent to which the problem has been considered and whether any useful action could be instituted under the particularly favorable conditions for change-over now existing.

According to files of Adjutant General, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, question of changing traffic flow from left to right-hand side of road originated with Commanding General, Eighth Army, March 16, 1946. Arguments advanced were that between eighty per cent and ninety per cent of all motor car traffic is of American made vehicles suitable for right-hand driving thereby safety would be enhanced. Referred to GHQ, SCAP, Staff Study dated April 15, 1946 was prepared by G-4, GHQ, recommending change-over, with concurrence of economic and scientific section, Chief Engineer and Provost Marshal.

On reference to Japanese Ministry of Transportation estimated cost of change-over was placed at ¥100,000,000 (about ¥14,000,000 at then existing United States Army exchange rate of ¥15 to ¥1,000), representing costs involved in alteration of fixed installations, including, for surface street cars: relocation of principal crossovers, relocation of electric switches and signals, re-equipment of cars and installations; for overbuses: re-equipment of 3,000 buses with new doors. In subsequent examination of Japanese figures, United States Army Chief Engineer reduced estimated cost of conversion to ¥84,000,000 (about ¥11,000,000), including cost of materials, which he estimated would total over three-year period: 1,996 tons of steel, 4,788 tons of cement and 43,424 tins. Chief Engineer concerned with Japanese engineering estimates that work involved would require thirty days before and three years after change-over.

Memorandum from Japanese Government Liaison Office recommended
against change-over, concluding (1) that right- and left-hand flow of
traffic is a matter of custom, and (2) change-over should be deferred
until transportation facilities restored to normal.

Commander-in-Chief disapproved change-over on grounds that "the
difficulties of obtaining acceptance of this action on the part of
the British in (the Allied) Council (for Japan) and (the Far Eastern)
Commission are believed to be insurmountable."

Two points remembered by official of Public Safety Division, G-2,
neither of which are documented in Adjutant General's files, follow:
(1) some Military Government Teams in prefectures of their responsibility
and on their own initiative ordered traffic change-over and
consequently Japanese Government appealed to CHQ for ruling to assure
uniformity in traffic flow; (2) opposition developed to change-over
within CHQ on ground that rules of land warfare do not permit occupying
power to alter local customs unrelated to military security.

Although originally proposed by Commanding General, Eighth Army,
and concurred in by other staff sections already indicated, rejection
of proposed change-over by this headquarters was definitive in character
and letter is now considered here to be "dead issue." Inasmuch as one
of principal objections raised has been that of cost, current emphasis
under united states directive on economic stabilization in Japan and
assessment reluctance to entertain new projects involving substantial
expense without contributing directly to national economy would seem
to militate against likelihood of favorable reconsideration of change-
over plans at this time. Usefulness of any fresh initiative of action
in this regard would probably be largely dependent upon means proposed
for meeting expense involved.

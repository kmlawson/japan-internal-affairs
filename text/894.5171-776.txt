Suspension of Stock Exchange Market:

The Tokyo Stock Exchange failed to open its doors on September 25th, and remained closed over the weekend until September 28th. The exchange had been opened intermittently since Monday, but was swamped with selling orders. Some members of the Exchange were facing bankruptcy as a result of the fall in share prices, and practically all were confronted with serious losses. The situation called for emergency action, and was solved, temporarily at least, by the action of the Nomura Bank of Osaka in loaning Yen 3,400,000 to the brokers in the form of a joint obligation of all members of the Exchange.

The break in share prices was occasioned by the suspension of Gold payments in London, but other factors, such as uneasiness over the critical situation in Manchuria and China, doubtless contributed some influence.

VI. GENERAL ECONOMIC AND FINANCIAL CONDITIONS.

The outstanding event in Japanese business circles during February was the collapse of the Tokyo stock market which began on the 14th. Before the end of the month the value of the securities listed had declined by about a half billion yen. The collapse is said to have been due, first, to the inflation of values which has taken place since the imposition of the gold embargo; secondly, to the announced intention of Japan to secede from the League of Nations; and thirdly, to the increased costs of production in industry. The enormous profits of Japanese industries during recent months were due largely to the fall in the exchange but now that the raw materials bought from abroad when the yen was at par have been used up it is not possible to make the profits which were formerly made.

The wholesale price index in Tokyo dropped 2.9 per cent during the month of February. This was due to a drop in foodstuffs and in all textiles.

The accrued unfavorable trade balance since the beginning of the calendar year was, on March 1, Yen 126,647,000.

There are indications that an iron merger will be successfully accomplished in Japan in the near future. The steel plants owned by the Government will be merged with the privately owned plants, the Government owning a controlling proportion of the stock and supervising the operation of the new concern.

DEPARTMENT OF STATE
DIVISION OF FAR EASTERN AFFAIRS
March 1, 1933.

TOkyo's despatch No. 274 of January 30, 1933, encloses a copy of a letter from the Embassy to Dr. Worden, of Yokohama, with regard to the latter's claim that he is exempt from the payment of income tax on income derived from the rental of perpetual lease property. The Embassy states that this is not an explicit treaty right and that therefore it cannot intervene on his behalf on the ground of the violation of an existing treaty. The Embassy has, in the past, taken the stand that each lease is an individual contract between the lessee and the Japanese authorities and should be treated as is any other private contract. The Embassy is not in a position to take any general stand vis-a-vis the Japanese Government on the question unless Dr. Worden can show that he, as an American citizen, is being discriminated against, which is apparently not the case.

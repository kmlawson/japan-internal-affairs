## Note by the Drug Control Service of the Secretariat

The purpose of this note is to furnish the most essential information in regard to the control of drugs and of Opium smoking and in regard to the drug situation in Formosa.

The information given in the note is based on the legislation in force and on annual reports on the traffic in opium and other dangerous drugs and on prepared opium furnished to the League of Nations by the Japanese Government up to and including 1938. The statistical information is that furnished by the Japanese Government to the Permanent Central Opium Board and certain statistics relating to prepared opium have been taken from the special annual reports on prepared opium.

The most important features of the opium and drug situation in Formosa are:

1. Opium smoking is temporarily authorised and prepared opium sold to registered and rationed consumers by a Government opium monopoly.

2. In the manufacture of prepared opium considerable quantities of raw morphine are obtained as a by-product. Large stocks of raw morphine have thus accumulated but in later years fairly substantial quantities were exported each year to Japan.

3. Formosa is one of the few centres in the world for the cultivation of the Coca Leaf. Coca Leaves are exported mainly to Japan; both crude cocaine and cocaine hydrochloride are manufactured in Formosa.

Through the ratification by Japan, the following three of the four International Drug Conventions apply to Formosa:

(1) The Hague International Opium Convention of 1912;

(2) The Geneva International Opium Convention of 1925;

PART I. CONTROL OF NARCOTIC DRUGS

3. Short Analysis of the Control System.

All narcotic drugs covered by the 1925 and 1931 Conventions except the coca leaf are under control in Formosa. The definition of the term Narcotic Drugs is wide, extending to all derivatives of morphine, ecgonine and their salts. Preparations containing drugs are covered by legislation as required by the Conventions. In regard to preparations containing morphine and acedicone, legislation goes beyond the Conventions, as such preparations are subject to control irrespective of the drug content. New drugs may be added by decision of the Government General. The enumeration of narcotic drugs in the legislation does not include the coca leaf. (See further below under Section 5.)

There is a central authority, namely the Police Bureau of the Government General at Taihoku.

Import and export of opium are prohibited, except import by the Government. International trade in narcotic drugs is governed by the import, certificate and export authorisation system.

The manufacture of drugs is subject to previous authorisation by the Government General, and manufacturers are under obligation to send in quarterly reports. They are subject to inspection. Wholesalers in narcotic drugs are not licensed by the Government General, but firms allowed to sell medicinal and prepared opium in wholesale trade are designated by the Government.

The central authority is the Police Bureau of the Government General at Taihoku, and under its guidance and direction Prefectural Governors and District Chiefs exercise control over opium and narcotic drugs.

5. Production of Raw Materials.

Raw Opium and Indian Hemp are not produced. The cultivation of the opium poppy for the production of opium is prohibited under Article 9 of the Opium Ordinance.

## 6. Manufacture of Drugs.

A person desiring to manufacture drugs is required to obtain permission from the Governor General of Formosa, who for his part, when granting permission, takes into careful consideration the quantity of drugs for local consumption, the quantity required for export and the quantity to be kept in stock in order that the quantity manufactured will not exceed the estimated requirements. The annual reports state that strict control is exercised over the factories. Manufacturers have to submit quarterly reports and are subject to inspection both at regular intervals and on surprise visits. Supplies of raw materials, quantities of drugs manufactured and disposed of are checked and verified on the basis of factory books and quarterly reports.

There is one licensed drug manufacturer in Taiwan, namely: Sin-ei Factory of Taiwan Syoyaku Kabusiki Kaisya (Taiwan Drug Manufacturing Co., Ltd.,) Sin-ei Gai, Sin-ei Gun, Tainan Province. The factory manufactures crude cocaine and cocaine hydrochloride.

As will be explained in detail in Part II (Control of Opium Smoking) prepared opium for sale to registered smokers is manufactured by the Government Opium Monopoly. Prepared opium is manufactured to contain a fixed percentage of morphine (6%) and as a consequence large quantities of crude morphine are obtained as a by-product in the prepared opium factory. Stocks of raw morphine have been accumulating in Formosa. On January 1st, 1935, there were in stock 5,776 kilograms of pure morphine contained in crude morphine. These stocks had been reduced to 1,855 kilograms on December 31st, 1939, through exports to Japan.

The international trade is subject to import certificates and export authorisations. Import certificates and export authorisations are issued by the Police Bureau of the Government General at Taihoku. The export of heroin is prohibited as an administrative measure.

PART II. CONTROL OF OPIUM SMOKING.

16. Origin and History.

In 1895 Formosa came under Japanese administration and it was found that a great number of the Formosan population was addicted to opium smoking. Although absolute prohibition of opium smoking had always been the policy in Japan, experience proved that it would be almost impossible to abolish opium smoking in Formosa at once. In 1896 the Governor General of Formosa declared that only habitual smokers would for medical reasons be permitted to consume opium. Total prohibition was laid down as principle but special licences to smoke were granted to medically certified chronic addicts. An opium monopoly was established and the opening of smoking establishments and the sale and manufacture of smoking paraphernalia was made subject to a licensing system. In September 1900 the preparatory work for the licensing of opium addicts had been completed and 169,064 addicts were given smokers licences. Although the control was based on the principle that no more licences would be given, which would have led to opium smoking disappearing entirely with the death of the last smokers licensed in 1900, it proved impossible to carry out this policy. A great number of secret smokers existed and the registers were opened three times, admitting new smokers to the licence system. This occurred in 1904-05, in 1908, and in 1929-30. The revised Opium Ordinance of 1929 stipulates that smokers' licences could only be given to persons addicted at the time when the Ordinance came into force. In 1938 the number of licensed smokers was 10,884.

17. General System of Control.

The administration of the opium ordinance comes within the competence of the Monopoly Department which imports opium, manufactures prepared opium, extracting morphine so that prepared opium contains uniformly 6% morphine, and distributes prepared opium through licensed wholesale and retail dealers. The manufacture and sale of smoking paraphernalia may be undertaken only by licensed persons. Smokers are licensed and rationed. All smoking establishments were abolished from 1929. All licensed opium smokers are under strict control of police officers who carry out inspection of smokers in their homes at regular intervals. Women are allowed to become licensed smokers under the same conditions as men. There are no legal stipulations as to the minimum age of licensed smokers but licences have been given only to persons above the age of 20.

The purchase and sale of dross is prohibited, and smokers are obliged to destroy the dross under police supervision. The Monopoly does not purchase dross, although there is legal provision for such purchase.

18. Government Opium Monopoly.

The manufacture and sale of prepared and medicinal opium is a Government monopoly, administered by the Government Monopoly Bureau which controls the monopolies in opium, salt, camphor, liquors and tobacco. The Monopoly Bureau comes under the direct control of the Police Bureau of the Governor General and has its head office in Taihoku. The opium factory is also situated in Taihoku.

19. System of Sales.

Prepared opium is sold by the Monopoly Bureau through the intermediary of licensed wholesale and retail dealers. The wholesale dealers are appointed upon application by the governors of the provinces. Only persons of good standing and reputation are appointed. Wholesalers may sell prepared opium only to licensed retail dealers, and in unbroken receptacles. At each purchase the retailers must produce their licence, whether they buy themselves or through employees identified by special police certificates. There is no recent information in regard to the number of wholesale dealers in prepared opium. In 1928 they were 55 in number. The annual report for 1932 states that the number then was 9. Prepared opium is delivered by the Monopoly in two receptacles only, namely 5 gr. and 15 gr. metal tubes. During the period 1935-1938, however, no 15 gr. tubes were manufactured and no sales of this size of tube were recorded for 1936 and onwards.

Wholesalers must sell prepared opium to retailers at a price fixed by the Government and they are allowed a profit of 1.3%. Wholesalers must keep books of their transactions in opium, entering all purchases and sales with the price in each case and the name and address of the retailer who purchased the opium. On the 5th of each month all wholesalers must report to the police the quantities and prices of prepared opium purchased and sold during the preceding month.

All retail sales take place through the licensed retail dealers. Licences are granted by the prefectural governors or by the governors of provinces. Retailers may sell opium for cash only and only to licensed smokers who produce their smokers' licence and their ration book. Retailers are allowed a profit of 10% on the official price and this profit is added to the price when the sales take place. In 1938 the official retail prices of prepared opium were 0.70 yen for a 5 gr. tube and 2.07 yen for a 15 gr. tube. On December 31st, 1938 there were in the whole of Formosa 291 licensed retailers. In 1900 the number was 2,766 and in 1928 447.

20. Control of Individual Smokers, including Rationing.

As mentioned above, opium smoking is permitted only to persons who at the coming into force of the opium ordinance of 1929 were medically certified as being chronic opium smokers. The licensed smokers are practically all native Formosans (descendants of Chinese settlers) and a few emigrant Chinese. The smokers may buy opium only from a licensed retailer and must at each purchase produce the licence and the purchase passbook in which the purchase is entered. All smokers are allowed a daily ration, stated in the licence. The ration is fixed on the basis of a medical examination of the smoker and only three days' rations may be purchased or held in possession at one time. A licensed smoker may not dispose of his prepared opium otherwise than by consuming it himself. There is no recent information as to the rations allowed to various groups of smokers. In September 1929, 14,599 smokers had a daily ration of less than 3.75 grammes (one momme), 8,858 between 3.75 and 7.5 grammes, 1,313 between 7.5 and 11.25 grammes, 335 between 11.25 and 15 grammes, 140 between 15 and 18.75 grammes and 83 more than 18.75 grammes (5 mommes). The number of smokers in 1938 was the following: Formosans 10,788, Chinese 96. The estimated population of Formosans was 5,352,000 and of Chinese 46,000.

## 21. Control of Opium Dross.

Purchase and sale or any other transaction in dross is absolutely prohibited, except when sold to or purchased by the Government. Possession of dross is also forbidden, except when dross which has been accumulated as a result of smoking opium is in the possession of the licensed smoker himself. In practice smokers are obliged to destroy their dross under police supervision. The smokers are told to collect all the dross until the next periodic visit of the police, when the dross is thrown away in the presence of the police. As the licence shows the smoker's daily ration, the police officer can ascertain whether all the dross produced is destroyed by the smokers.

## 22. Import and Export of Raw and Prepared Opium.

Imports and exports of prepared opium and exports of raw opium are absolutely prohibited. The import of raw opium is also prohibited, except imports by the Government.

## 24. Cure of Opium Smokers.

Both opium smokers and drug addicts are subject to compulsory cures. Smoking by non-licensed persons and the abuse of narcotic drugs are punishable offences under the law. The opium ordinance (Article 10) provides that the Governor General may adopt the necessary measures for the purpose of curing the habit of opium smoking and he is also empowered to prescribe suitable measures for the cure of those addicted to manufactured drugs. In practice it appears that only those smokers who are caught smoking illicitly are subjected to compulsory cure. The smokers who have been licensed to smoke after medical examination are considered as chronic smokers, i.e. incurable. When the smokers' registers were opened in 1929-1930, 25,527 secret smokers were discovered. Of these, 5,518 were given smokers' licences, being considered as chronic smokers. 13,854 were obliged to undergo curative treatment and 6,209 were forbidden to continue smoking opium. All licensed smokers were then again examined medically, with the result that 3,884 were required to undergo a cure and 1,253 were forced to abandon smoking altogether. While the smokers were waiting for their cure, they were given temporary smokers' licences.

Medical practitioners who find drug addicts in their practice must report such cases to the Police authorities and those addicts may be subjected to compulsory cure. Smokers and drug addicts are sent for cure to a special institution in Taihoku (Taihoku House of Correction) and to public and private hospitals in the provinces. At least in earlier years some smokers underwent cure in their homes under Police control.

25. Penalties.

Penalties for violation of the Opium Ordinance as a rule are either imprisonment with or without hard labour or a fine. The penalty for illicit smoking is imprisonment with hard labour for a period not exceeding three years, without the option of a fine. The following offences are punishable with imprisonment with hard labour up to five years or with a fine of a maximum of 5,000 Yen: illicit manufacture, import and export of opium, illicit sale, purchase or possession of opium for the purpose of making profit, maintaining an opium smoking establishment, inducing a non-licensed smoker to smoke opium. Illicit transactions in regard to opium smoking instruments are punishable with hard labour up to three years or a fine of up to 3,000 Yen. Other offences carry less severe punishment.

26. Illicit Traffic.

The Japanese authorities state in the annual reports on Formosa that illicit traffic originates from the fact that non-licensed smokers who find it difficult to obtain Monopoly-prepared opium must necessarily purchase smuggled raw or prepared opium, of which the country of origin is presumably China. Before the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese conflict in 1937 contraband opium was usually carried by Chinese and Formosans in junks to the Opium ports of Keelung, Takao, Tansui, Ampei, etc. This traffic disappeared with the outbreak of hostilities. Sometimes opium was smuggled on board steamers by sailors.

## Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs

THE CONTROL OF NATIONAL DRUGS AND THE OPIUM SMOKING
AND THE DRUG INDUSTRY IN FORMED (TAX IN)

**Note by the Drug Control Service of the Secretariat**

The purpose of this note is to furnish the most essential information in regard to the control of drugs and of Opium smoking and in regard to the drug situation in Formosa.

The information given in the note is based on the legislation in force and on annual reports on the traffic in opium and other dangerous drugs and on prepared opium furnished to the League of Nations by the Japanese Government up to and including 1938. The statistical information is that furnished by the Japanese Government to the Permanent Central Opium Board and certain statistics relating to prepared opium have been taken from the special annual reports on prepared opium.

x x x

The most important features of the opium and drug situation in Formosa are:

1) Opium smoking is temporarily authorised and prepared opium sold to registered and rationed consumers by a Government opium monopoly.

2) In the manufacture of prepared opium considerable quantities of raw morphine are obtained as a by-product. Large stocks of raw morphine have thus accumulated but in later years fairly substantial quantities were exported each year to Japan.

3) Formosa is one of the few centres in the world for the cultivation of the Coca Leaf. Coca Leaves are exported mainly to Japan; both crude cocaine and cocaine hydrochloride are manufactured in Formosa.

x x x

Through the ratification by Japan, the following three of the four International Drug Conventions apply to Formosa:

(1) The Hague International Opium Convention of 1912;

(2) The Geneva International Opium Convention of 1925;

(3) Convention for Limiting the Manufacture and Regulating the Distribution of Narcotic Drugs of 1931.

## PART I. CONTROL OF NARCOTIC DRUGS.

### 3. Short Analysis of the Control System.

All narcotic drugs covered by the 1925 and 1931 Conventions except the coca leaf are under control in Formosa. The definition of the term Narcotic Drugs is wide, extending to all derivatives of morphine, egocaine and their salts. Preparations containing drugs are covered by legislation as required by the Conventions. In regard to preparations containing morphine and acedicone, legislation goes beyond the Conventions, as such preparations are subject to control irrespective of the drug content. New drugs may be added by decision of the Government General. The enumeration of narcotic drugs in the legislation does not include the coca leaf. (See further below under Section 5.)

There is a central authority, namely, the Police Bureau of the Government General at Taihoku.

Import and export of opium are prohibited, except import by the Government. International trade in narcotic drugs is governed by the import certificate and export authorisation system.

The manufacture of drugs is subject to previous authorisation by the Government General, and manufacturers are under obligation to send in quarterly reports. They are subject to inspection. Wholesalers in narcotic drugs are not licensed by the Government General, but firms allowed to sell medicinal and prepared opium in wholesale trade are designated by the Government.

The following are by law authorised to use of possess narcotic drugs: Physicians, dentists, veterinarians, pharmacists and persons permitted to use drugs for scientific purposes. Both wholesalers and retailers are under supervision by police officers and experts. Drugs may be dispensed by pharmacists only on prescription.

The central authority is the Police Bureau of the Government General at Taihoku, and under its guidance and direction Prefectural Governors and District Chiefs exercise control over opium and narcotic drugs.

## PART II. CONTROL OF OPIUM SMOKING

### Origin and History.

In 1895 Formosa came under Japanese administration and it was found that a great number of the Formosa population was addicted to opium smoking. Although absolute prohibition of opium smoking had always been the policy in Japan, experience proved that it would be almost impossible to abolish opium smoking in Formosa at once. In 1896 the Governor General of Formosa declared that only habitual smokers would for medical reasons be permitted to consume opium. Total prohibition was laid down as principle but special licences to smoke were granted to medically certified chronic addicts. An opium monopoly was established and the opening of smoking establishments and the sale and manufacture of smoking paraphernalia was made subject to a licensing system. In September 1900 the preparatory work for the licensing of opium addicts had been completed and 169,064 addicts were given smokers licences. Although the control was based on the principle that no more licences would be given, which would have led to opium smoking disappearing entirely with the death of the last smokers licensed in 1900, it proved impossible to carry out this policy. A great number of secret smokers existed and the registers were opened three times, admitting new smokers to the licence system. This occurred in 1904-05, in 1908 and in 1919-20. The revised Opium Ordinance of 1929 stipulates that smokers' licences could only be given to persons addicted at the time when the Ordinance came into force. In 1938 the number of licensed smokers was 10,884.

### 17. General System of Control.

The administration of the opium ordinance comes within the competence of the Monopoly Department which imports opium, manufactures prepared opium, extracting morphine, so that prepared opium contains uniformly 6% morphine, and distributes prepared opium through licensed wholesale and retail dealers. The manufacture and sale of smoking paraphernalia may be undertaken only by licensed persons. Smokers are licensed and rationed. All smoking establishments were abolished from 1929. All licensed opium smokers are under strict control of police officers who carry out inspection of smokers in their homes at regular intervals. Women are allowed to become licensed smokers under the same conditions as men. There are no legal stipulations as to the minimum age of licensed smokers but licences have been given only to persons above the age of 20.

The purchase and sale of dress is prohibited, and smokers are obliged to destroy the dress under police supervision. The Monopoly does not purchase dress, although there is legal provision for such purchase.

### 18. Government Opium Monopoly.

The manufacture and sale of prepared and medicinal opium is a Government monopoly, administered by the Government Monopoly Bureau which controls the monopolies in opium, salt, camphor, liquors and tobacco. The Monopoly Bureau comes under the direct control of the Police Bureau of the Governor General and has its head office in Taihoku. The opium factory is also situated in Taihoku.

- 10 -

## Effect of Sales.

Prepared opium is sold by the Monopoly Bureau through the intermediary of licensed wholesale and retail dealers. The wholesale dealers are appointed upon application by the governors of the provinces. Only persons of good standing and reputation are appointed. Wholesalers may sell prepared opium only to licensed retail dealers, and in unbroken receptacles. Attach purchase the retailers must produce their licence, with the buy themselves or through employees identified by special police certificates. There is no recent information in regard to the number of wholesale dealers in prepared opium. In 1928 they were 55 in number. The annual report for 1931 states that the number then was 9. Prepared opium is delivered by the Monopoly in two receptacles only, namely 5 gr. and 15 gr. metal tubes. During the period 1935-38, however, no 1 gr. tube was manufactured and no sales of this size of tube were recorded for 1936 and onwards.

Wholesalers must sell prepared opium to retailers at a price fixed by the Government and they are allowed a profit of 1.3%. Wholesalers must keep books of their transactions in opium, entering all purchases and sales with the price in each case and the name and address of the retailer who purchased the opium. On the 5th of each month all wholesalers must report to the police the quantities and prices of prepared opium purchased and sold during the preceding month.

All retail sales take place through the licensed retail dealers. Licences are granted by the prefectural governors or by the governors of provinces. Retailers may sell opium for cash only and only to licensed smokers who produce their smokers' licence and their ration book. Retailers are allowed a profit of 10% on the official price and this profit is added to the price when the sales take place. In 1938 the official retail prices of prepared opium were 0.70 yen for a 5 gr. tube and 2.07 Yen for a 15 gr. tube. On December 31st, 1938 there were in the whole of Formosa 291 licensed retailers. In 1900 the number was 2,766 and in 1928 447.

## 20. Control of Individual Smokers, including Rationing.

As mentioned above, opium smoking is permitted only to persons who at the coming into force of the opium ordinance of 1929 were medically certified as being chronic opium smokers. The licensed smokers are practically all native Formosans (descendants of Chinese settlers) and a few emigrant Chinese. The smokers may buy opium only from a licensed retailer and must at each purchase produce the licence and the purchase passbook in which the purchase is entered. All smokers are allowed a daily ration, stated in the licence. The ration is fixed on the basis of a medical examination of the smoker and only three days' rations may be purchased or held in possession at one time. A licensed smoker may not dispose of his prepared opium otherwise than by consuming it himself. There is no recent information as to the rations allowed to various groups of smokers. In September 1929, 14,599 smokers had a daily ration of less than 3.75 grammes (one momme), 8,858 between 3.75 and 7.5 grammes, 1,313 between 7.5 and 11.25 grammes, 335 between 11.25 and 15 grammes, 140 between 15 and 18.75 grammes and 83 more than 18.75 grammes (5 mommes). The number of smokers in 1937 was the following: Formosans 10,738, Chinese 96. The estimated population of Formosans was 5,352,000 and of Chinese 46,000.

## Control of Opium Dross.

Purchase and sale or any other transaction in dross is absolutely prohibited, except when sold to or purchased by the Government. Possession of dross is also forbidden, except when dross which has been accumulated as a result of smoking opium is in the possession of the licensed smoker himself. In practice smokers are obliged to destroy their dross under police supervision. The smokers are told to collect all the dross until the next periodic visit of the police, when the dross is thrown away in the presence of the police. As the licence shows the smoker's daily ration, the police officer can ascertain whether all the dross produced is destroyed by the smokers.

22. **Import and export of Raw and Prepared Opium.**

Imports and exports of prepared opium and exports of raw opium are absolutely prohibited. The import of raw opium is also prohibited, except imports by the Government.

24. **Cure of Opium Smokers.**

Both opium smokers and drug addicts are subject to compulsory cures. Smoking by non-licensed persons and the abuse of narcotic drugs are punishable offences under the law. The opium ordinance (Article 10) provides that the Governor General may adopt the necessary measures for the purpose of curing the habit of opium smoking and he is also empowered to prescribe suitable measures for the cure of those addicted to manufactured drugs. In practice it appears that only those smokers who are caught smoking illicitly are subjected to compulsory cure. The smokers who have been licensed to smoke after medical examination are considered as chronic smokers, i.e. incurable. When the smokers' registers were opened in 1929-30, 25,527 secret smokers were discovered. Of these, 5,518 were given smokers' licenses, being considered as chronic smokers. 13,854 were obliged to undergo curative treatment and 6,209 were forbidden to continue smoking opium. All licensed smokers were then again examined medically, with the result that 3,884 were required to undergo a cure and 1,253 were forced to abandon smoking altogether. While the smokers were waiting for their cure, they were given temporary smokers' licenses.

25. Penalties.

Penalties for violation of the Opium Ordinance as a rule are either imprisonment with or without hard labour or a fine. The penalty for illicit smoking is imprisonment with hard labour for a period not exceeding three years, without the option of a fine. The following offences are punishable with imprisonment with hard labour up to five years or with a fine of a maximum of 1,000 Yen: illicit manufacture, import and export of opium, illicit sale, purchase or possession of opium for the purpose of making profit, maintaining an opium smoking establishment, inducing a non-licensed smoker to smoke opium. Illicit transactions in regard to opium smoking instruments are punishable with hard labour up to three years or a fine of up to 3,000 Yen. Other offences carry less severe punishment.

26. Illicit Traffic.

The Japanese authorities state in the annual reports on Formosa that illicit traffic originates from the fact that non-licensed smokers who find it difficult to obtain Monopoly-prepared opium must necessarily purchase smuggled raw or prepared opium, of which the country of origin is presumably China. Before the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese conflict in 1937 contraband opium was usually carried by Chinese and Formosans in junks to the Opium ports of Keelung, Takao, Tansui, Ampei, etc. This traffic disappeared with the outbreak of hostilities. Sometimes opium was smuggled on board steamers by sailors.

I. INTRODUCTION
Prince Hirobumi Ito, with a group of Japanese states-
men on a mission to the United States in l87l, stated up-
on arrival in San Francisco:
"Today it is the earnest wish of both
our Government and people to strive for the
highest points of civilization enjoyed by
more enlightened countries. Looking to this
end, we have adopted their military, naval,
scientific, and educational institutions,
and knowledge has flowed to us freely in the
wake of foreign commerce...... Japan cannot
claim originality as yet, but it will aim to
exercise practical wisdom by adopting the
advantages, and avoiding the errors, taught
her by the nistory of those enlightened nations
whose experience is her teacher."
At Sacramento, a few days later ne said:
"We come to study your strength, that,
by adopting wisely your better ways, we may
hereafter be stronger ourselves. We shall
require your mechanics to teach our people
many things, and the more our intercourse
increases the more we shall call upon you.
We shall labor to place Japan on an equal
basis, in the future, with those countries
whose modern civilization is now our guide..
Notwithstanding the various customs, manners,
and institutions of the different nations
we are all members of one large human family,
and under control of the same Almighty Being,
and we believe it is our common destiny to
reach a yet nobler civilization than the
world has yet seen.*

II. THE CAUSE OF INVENTIONS
The conquest of the land now comprised in the United
States provided the greatest stimulus to invention in
modern times. The incredibly rapid growth of the popu-
lation and the vast expansion over the territory from
the Atlantic seaboard to the Pacific coast presentea
problems of greatest urgency. There was a constant need
for inventions which woula speed up agriculture, trans-
portation, industry, and whicn woula produce greater
quantities, cover greater territory, and accomplish
more and more per unit of man power.
These conditions and also merican political
theories had an effect upon the minds of the people.
Had Thomas Edison been born in some other country,
where the land was all settled, where tnere were plenty
of people to do the work that had to be done, where
life for the majority of the population nad become as
stereotyped as heredity and class distinctions could
make it, he possibly would not have been stimulated to
do the things he did. In any case, without a need for
the ideas which developed in his mind no one would have
been interested in his work. But if, like Marconi,
Steinmetz and others, he could not repress his creative
mind, it is probable that like the others he would have
come to the United States in order to have freedom with
which to work in a country impatient for his brilliant
ideas.

In Japan, a country where inuividual farms average
only 2.6 acres in size and where hand labor is cneaper
than machine labor, it is not expected that anyone will
invent agricultural labor saving devices. People who
are too poor to afford anything more expensive than a
bicycle are not in a position to invent iuprovements in
automotive vehicles. In a country where nearly everyone
lives in frail houses made of wood, mud, and paper, it
is not expected that new building materials will be de-
veloped. Among a people who use practically no nouse-
hold furniture, no plumbing, only charcoal braziers
for neating and cooking and no labor saving devices, one
can not hope for inventions in regard to the equipment
of homes. In a country too small and too poor to sup-
port commercial air transportation there can be very
little invention in aircraft design. If, in addition
to the situation described above, there is added the
condition of a rigidly stereotyped social system which
for generations has not only discouraged but in some
cases forbidden the mass of the population from original
thought, it is clearly evident that modern invention has
not been favored in Japan.
There is, therefore, an environmental explanation
for the remarkable progress of invention in the United
States and the relative lack of it in Japan. The fol-
lowing Section of this report presents in statistical
form a number of conditions in the United States and

IV. CLASSIFICATION OF JAPANESE INDUSTRY
The spectacular success of Japanese industry in
certain classes of products has aroused so much comment
abroad where little or nothing is known of conditions
in this country, that there has been a marked tendency
toward believing that success in producing textiles,
prepared foods, and other articles of a staple character
in the manufacture of which low labor costs play an in-
portant part, will rapidly be repeated in all classes of
industry. A study of other factors involved does not
bear out such a conclusion. It is believed, therefore,
that a classification of Japanese industry will help to
indicate the direction in which progress has taken place
and is likely to continue.

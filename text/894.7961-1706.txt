SUBJECT: Reopening of Civil Aviation in Japan.

The reopening of limited private trade with Japan on 15 August makes highly desirable the provision of commercial airline transportation by that time. The U.S. Government has just introduced a policy into the FEC, covering civil aviation in Japan and it is clear that FEC will not reach a policy decision by 15 August. In light of this difficulty, General McCoy announced to the FEC on 31 July as follows:

"In preparation for the reopening of private trade with Japan on 15 August 1947, SCAP, pending final action by the FEC on the proposed Civil Aviation Policy toward Japan, as an administrative measure will permit the transportation to, through and from Japan of those persons included in the Inter-Allied Trade Board's quota for private business activities in Japan. The transportation will be furnished in accordance with the provisions of the proposed policy."

894 7961 / 80447

In making the foregoing announcement, neither the State Department nor the War Department intended to restrict airline commercial traffic to businessmen entering Japan under a quota; on the contrary it was reasoned that SCAP's right to exercise administrative discretion would extend to all types of airline commercial traffic, and Part II of War Department's cable to SCAP read as follows:

"Contingent upon your approval Pan American and Northwest will be advised that they may transport persons included in Inter-Allied Board's quota for business activities as well as all other persons having proper authority to enter Japan."

The traffic of commercial airlines is composed of three categories: Passengers, cargo, and mail. From the airlines' viewpoint, all three categories merge and combine to constitute "commercial traffic," even though as "commercial traffic" each category is separable. From the viewpoint of the U.S. Government, it is undesirable to discriminate against cargo and mail in favor of passengers: prior to 15 August SCAP will authorize air mail and transactional communications; Northwest Airlines has already sought (and been thus far refused) permission to carry cargo for Time magazine; if administrative discretion in this case extends to all who are authorized to enter Japan--and we think it does-- it certainly extends to cargo and mail; and it would be difficult to justify permission for the airlines to carry dependents but not, say, U.S. mail. Furthermore, it is believed that no difficulties, traceable to the inclusion of mail and cargo as well as passengers within the ambit of administrative discretion, would be raised at the FEC.

It is therefore proposed to send the following cable to apprise SCAP that in our opinion SCAP may through administrative discretion permit mail and cargo to be carried by commercial airlines to and from Japan.

INTERNATIONAL AIR TRAFFIC IN JAPAN

1. a. Rescission. Circular 11, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, 7 November 1946.

b. Reference. Circular 8, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, 30 August 1946.

2. The directives contained herein become effective this date and shall be brought to the attention of all countries, agencies, and aircrews flying aircraft into Japan.

3. The purpose of this circular is to establish standard procedures to be followed by all aircraft cleared into or out of Japan in accordance with reference 1b above.

4. Owing to the unpredictable character of weather conditions normally encountered over Japan and the extremely rugged nature of the terrain along available approach routes between the Asiatic mainland, the southern islands, and Japan, the safe conduct of flights is a matter of concern to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

5. With the foregoing objects in mind, the following procedures shall apply to all international air traffic cleared into and out of the Tokyo Area, Japan, with the exception of regularly scheduled air traffic along organized air routes as approved by the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

a. Approach Routes and Check Points. To aid in the control of air traffic and to facilitate search for missing aircraft, all flights into Japan shall be conducted as follows:

(1) Any aircraft approaching Japanese territory from the north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan shall conduct their flight so as to pass directly over the town of Ryozu (38°04'N, 138°26'E) on Sado Island at an altitude of 10,000 feet, or at a higher or lower altitude

if weather conditions so require. This point is designated "Check Point Sado." From check point Sado, all flights shall proceed to Haneda Airfield (35°34'N, 139°46'E) via one of the following airway routes (See inclosures 2 and 3):

(a) North Route. Check point Sado to check point Niigata (37°56'N, 139°02'E), thence to check point Koriyama (37°24'N, 140°23'E), thence southward generally along railroad to check point Koga (36°12'N, 139°42'E), thence south to Haneda Airfield.

(b) South Route. Check point Sado to check point Niigata (37°56'N, 139°02'E), thence to check point Nagaoka (37°26'N, 138°50'E), thence generally via railroad to check point Maebashi (36°24'N, 139°04'E), thence to check point Koga (36°12'N, 139°42'E), thence south to Haneda Airfield.

(2) Any aircraft approaching Japanese territory from the west, south, and east of Honshu shall conduct their flight so as to enter Japan by means of established airways as indicated in inclosures 2 and 3, and shall proceed to Haneda Airfield (35°34'N, 139°46'E) as follows:

(a) West. Enter Japan by passing over check point Fukuoka (33°36'N, 130°24'E), thence to check point Osaka (34°40'N, 135°30'E), thence to check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(b) South. Enter Japan by passing over check point Kanoya (31°23'N, 130°52'E), thence to check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(c) East. Enter Japan by passing over check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(3) The above air routes normally encompass the airspace within twenty-five (25) miles over water and ten (10) miles over land, on either side of the rhumb lines connecting the check points listed.

b. Entry Procedure.

(1) Escort aircraft under control of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers may meet inbound flights at any point along the outlined air routes over Japan. Detailed escort instructions may be provided at the time the flight is cleared for entry.

(2) If escort aircraft do not meet inbound flights approaching from north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan, the aircraft cleared for entry shall proceed from Niigata to Haneda Airfield along the northern route as indicated in paragraph 5a(1).

(3) If escort aircraft do meet inbound flights approaching from north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan, the aircraft cleared for entry shall follow the decision of the escort commander as to choice of northern or southern routes from Niigata to Haneda Airfield as indicated in paragraph 5a(1).

(4) Entrance to Japan from the east, south, and west is confined to one established air route over Japan with no alternate so that only the outlined route will be used.

(5) In event of interception by escort aircraft, all instructions given by the escort commander shall be followed implicitly.

(6) Aircraft proceeding along above established air routes shall avoid flying over airfields, remaining at least three miles from them, and shall use caution in avoiding local air traffic which may be in the vicinity of the airfield.

c. Arrival Procedure.

(1) Upon arrival in the Tokyo Area, aircraft shall circle Haneda Airfield within a five-mile radius, flying not lower than 1,500 feet over the city areas and vicinity, and shall request landing instructions from Haneda Tower.

f. Emergency Procedure.

(1) "Haneda Tower" is prepared to transmit and receive in English on 140.58 megacycles (secondary frequency).

(2) Haneda Airfield is equipped with special instrument letdown equipment called "Ground Controlled Approach" (GCA). With this equipment, specially trained operators on the ground transmit to aircraft verbal instructions which, if followed implicitly, will enable the pilot to effect a safe landing under practically any weather conditions. No special equipment is required in the airplane other than radio receiving equipment capable of reception on Control Tower frequencies mentioned above. Under instrument weather conditions, the tower operator will pass control of aircraft to the Ground Controlled Approach operator (voice call "Haneda GCA"), who will verbally direct the pilot of the aircraft to perform the necessary maneuvers to effect a safe landing. In view of this service, incoming flights need not be diverted to other airfields under inclement weather conditions except in case of radio or mechanical failure.

STANDARD LIGHT SIGNALS

1. General. The portable traffic control light is a small, easily handled directive light, which emits an intense, narrow beam of light. The color of the light (white, green, or red) is controlled by the operator through a system of levers and triggers in the two handles. The normal range of the signal in ordinary weather is ten (10) miles in day time and fifteen (15) miles at night. Signals are readily discernible to the pilot of any aircraft visible to the operator. This light shall be used to control the movement of personnel and vehicles on the landing area and the landings and take-offs of any aircraft not equipped with radio.

2. Signals from a portable traffic control light shall mean the following:

a. To Aircraft in Flight.

(1) A green light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED TO LAND."

(2) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "GIVE WAY TO OTHER AIRCRAFT AND CONTINUE CIRCLING."

(3) A series of red flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a recall signal to indicate to the pilot that it is desired that he return and land. (Light signal to indicate clearance to land must also be given when aircraft is in proper position.)

(4) A series of alternating red and green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a general warning signal to advise a pilot to be on the alert for hazardous or unusual conditions.

b. To Aircraft on the Airfield.

(1) When a pilot is taxiing:

(a) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "STOP."

(b) A series of red flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean that a pilot is to taxi back to the hangar line.

(c) A series of green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED TO CONTINUE TAXING."

(2) When a pilot is in position for take-off:

(a) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEAR THE RUNWAY IMMEDIATELY AND WAIT."

(b) A green light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED FOR TAKE-OFF."

(3) General Warning Signal. A series of alternating red and green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a general warning signal to advise a pilot or driver of a vehicle or personnel on the landing area to be on the alert for hazardous or unusual conditions.

(4) Attention is directed to the fact that the warning signal is not a prohibitive signal and may be followed by either a red or green light as circumstances warrant.

Subject: Violations of Air Traffic Regulations by Soviet Aircraft.
DEPARTMENT OF STATE

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to bring to the Department's attention violations of air traffic regulations by a Soviet aircraft on September 22, 1948.

Investigation conducted by the Far East Air Forces revealed that on September 22, 1948 the Soviet aircraft IL-12 No. 2 failed to establish contact with the Tokyo control at Haneda Airport on the assigned radio frequency when the plane reached a point within a 100-mile radius of Tokyo. The Soviet plane also failed to establish radio contact with the escort aircraft.

On arriving at Haneda Airport, the IL-12 No. 2 disregarded parking instructions of the Haneda Airport control and the Haneda Airport ground alert crew.

Moreover, only three and one-half hours advance notice was given prior to the arrival of the IL-12 No. 2 whereas regulations governing flights into Japan require notice twenty-four hours in advance of arrival.

These violations and omissions were brought to the attention of the Soviet Member, Allied Council for Japan, in a memorandum from General Headquarters dated September 26, 1948, in which it was noted that, in the light of previous communications to, and conferences with, the Soviet Member and his authorized representatives, these violations and omissions represent a serious disregard of the essential safety procedures established for all aircraft permitted entry at Haneda Airport.

Pending investigation of the matter, the Soviet aircraft IL-12 No. 2 was detained at Haneda Airport. Exit clearance was authorized effective on receipt by the Soviet Member of a General Headquarters' memorandum addressed to him on September 26, 1948, the necessary investigation into the matter having been completed by the Occupation authorities.

My dear General Eberle:

I am writing to answer your letter of November 15, 1948. With that letter you sent a copy of a letter from Mr. Bixby of Pan American World Airways System, together with a copy of your proposed reply, and asked for our comments on your draft.

We have only one suggestion to make in connection with your answer, and that is to omit the specific reference to a sixty-day period in which policy with respect to Japanese civil aviation will be determined. Since it is not certain that the matter will be settled in that time, you might want to modify the last two sentences of your reply to read somewhat as follows:

"It is hoped that agreement may be reached within the near future. Under the circumstances it is my suggestion that you withhold further action in the matter pending development and final action of the above policy, and that thereafter we again discuss the situation in the light of circumstances which may have developed at that time."

Sincerely yours,

Charles E. Saltzman
Assistant Secretary

Dear Mr. Saltzman:

Inclosed herewith you will find copy of a letter dated 10 November 1948 which I have received from Mr. H. M. Bixby, Vice President, Pan American World Airways System, together with a draft copy of my proposed action. Your comments on the latter are requested.

By way of background, the conversation of 13 August 1948, referred to in Mr. Bixby's letter was held in my office at Mr. Bixby's request, for the purpose of informally discussing the desire of Pan American World Airways System to establish an internal civil aviation service in Japan. The consensus of opinion of those present at this meeting was that the proposal should first be submitted to SCAP for his consideration. This apparently has been done, but SCAP has declined to take the initiative, possibly in view of the policy paper pending in the Far Eastern Commission. This paper (FEC 245/20) was introduced by the U. S. and is now being considered in Committee No. 2. If approved in substantially its present form, it will provide that SCAP may inform the Far Eastern Commission that in his opinion an internal civil aviation service is needed in Japan and thereafter, if FEC fails to take action within 60 days, SCAP may inaugurate such a service.

If FEC should fail to approve a policy on this subject within a reasonable period of time, it may well be that the U.S. will see fit to issue an interim directive or to suggest that SCAP handle the situation by using his administrative power. In any event, I feel that the Department of State has great interest in this question, and before replying to Mr. Bixby, I would appreciate your views.

Sincerely yours,

G. L. EBERLE
Brigadier General, USA
Chief, Civil Affairs Division

Dear General Eberle:

I refer to the conversation Vice President Bond and Assistant Vice President Towers of our corporation had with you and your associates on 13 August on the subject of Pan American's proposal to undertake the establishment and operation of a U. S. flag commercial airline within Japan.

Based upon the opinion you expressed at that meeting, which opinion I may say we shared, we initiated the matter by having Mr. Bond present our proposal to General MacArthur personally in Tokyo. General MacArthur expressed interest, but stated that under existing circumstances he felt the initiative should be taken in Washington rather than by him.

Pan American still is of the opinion that its proposal is sound, and would contribute very materially to the efforts of General MacArthur towards re-establishment of satisfactory economic conditions in Japan. However, we do not desire to press the matter at this time if such action on our part would cause any embarrassment, and therefore would appreciate an expression of your views with regard to our making formal presentation of our proposal to the Department of the Army.

Yours very truly,

H. M. Bixby /sgd/

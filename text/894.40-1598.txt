PUBLICATIONS ANALYSIS, 24 January 1947 No. 91

Welfare

The sociological problems which confront present-day Japan are many and varied. Toward certain of these, however, Japanese magazines seem to be showing an increasingly healthier attitude; the essential problem is singled out and commented upon without hysteria. In particular, repatriation has ceased to be a major topic, but many writers feel that the attitude of the public towards repatriates and returned soldiers is cold and unfeeling and warn against the social repercussions which might ensue.

Much concern is found over the plight of war orphans; and the problem of older children driven into delinquency through the loss of home or family, or through the breakdown of moral responsibility due to economic pressure, is the subject of a considerable volume of articles. Credit is given to existing institutions which are doing their best to cope with the situation, but it is generally believed that the Government must increase funds and facilities for the care of unfortunates if the problem is to be solved.

Homeless Children

Among the remnants cast up by the tide of war and the homeless children of Japan. These waifs include those orphaned during the war-years and an increasing number who are being deserted in infancy due to shortages of food or through simple immorality. A SHUKAN ASAHI editor claims that the latter group, while generally growing in number since the first of this year, is influenced by reductions and increases in the flow of rationed foods. They are deserted "at one to three months of age, by reason of hardship or as the result of immorality. They are almost all in poor health and when brought to the almshouse they are put under the care of nurses. Their only hope of ultimate happiness is adoption. If this does not occur they will remain at the home until they reach the age of eight when they are transferred . . . for primary schooling. Looking at such poor innocents who will never know a parent's love just touch the hardest hear." Novelist Fumiko Hayashi, in BUNSEI SHUNJU, says that "slanioned children move here and there like gypsies, seeking food and shelter. Without sympathy from the people of the community and material aid from the Government, the problem may not be solved."

A FUJIN GAHO roundtable discusses war orphans and, quoting the report of the Department of Welfare made on 25 November 1945, states that their number was 4755. Of these, 983 were in Tokyo, 570 in Osaka, 563 in Hiroshima, and the remainder elsewhere in Japan. Hatsue Koike, of Asahi, reports a visit to a war orphan asylum and says that there are 93 such institutions in Japan, "of which 57 are under direct government management. The Department of Welfare has submitted an estimate of 26,000,000 yen for new asylums, into which it is planned to bring 4,000 gamins." This roundtable group criticizes the Government for not having taken previous steps to alleviate the plight of these children, and scores conditions in existing asylums which are

"poorly accommodated, without tatami or books."

A brighter picture is presented by Kaiko Ifukube in a KURABU report on the Itabashi home for war orphans. This institution is kept by the Fellow Sufferers' Relief Association and the City of Tokyo, and houses thirty children, mostly elementary school pupils. The staff consists of the Director, a Head Nurse, a medical nurse and a governess, all of whom live with the children "who are much attached to their acting parents." Expects the dullness and loneliness characteristic of such children, Ifukube was surprised to find them lively and independent, and attributes this to the home policy of "encouraging the strong points of the children and allowing them to follow their own bent. They devise and make toys and necessities by themselves, and clean their own quarters without direction."

Another optimistic report comes from the pages of KINGU, which commends the war orphanage of Saredio School -- "the only orphanage of foreign management." This writer was much impressed, on the occasion of his visit, by the sight of "the faithful figures of about thirty little boys -- former wanderers with wild and sad hearts -- kneeling in prayer for the repose of the souls of lost parents and families, and in thanks for their own present happiness." He quotes the supervisor, Father Tasinari, who stated that "at the beginning many boys escaped, but they have now become settled and no further attempts are made. The most important thing in getting them settled is to establish connection between boys and leaders through common constructive work. We shall continue to take an interest in these boys as long as they like; and the clever ones may continue their schooling on higher levels."

Juvenile delinquency is an increasing social problem, and ASAHI GRAPH -- in an article on the Seto Boys' Home -- attributes it to "increasing uneasiness of social conditions following the defeat, and economic hardships." This writer states that the majority of the boys in this institution were guilty of theft, and that "two-thirds of them have social diseases." He approves of the Home, writing that "these boys are not treated as prisoners. Parents who storm into the institution go away with gratitude after seeing the lives of their children who fell into error." Another such home is the Shin Nippon Gakuin, according to Shizue Masugi in SHUKAN ASAHI. "Nowadays the number of delinquent boys is greatly increased by hunger, and this guardian home accordingly places its main emphasis on food and work rather than on spiritual cross-examination." This home works on architectural enterprises, and "many of the young man are able to earn money to send home to their parents. They are creating a new organization of society through hard work, and they are taught that through such means they can enjoy a happy life without resort to crime."

## Repatriates and Demobilees

The efficient operation of the repatriation program has put an end to the past volume of articles on this subject -- with the exception of occasional mention of those Japanese who remain isolated in Manchuria. A SHIN NIPPON writer eulogizes "the kindness of the United States," and hopes that shipping may be made available for the remaining unfortunate abroad. He urges a sincere welcome for repatriates, and scores the "deplorable moral attitude of the people" towards wounded and demobilized soldiers: "In deserted street corners one may see men in white clothes (wounded soldiers) to whom people pay little attention. Among brazen-faced passers-by are commanders who deserted their Special Attack Unit forces at the fighting front."

A well-illustrated ASAHI GRAPH article deals with conditions at the Sagamigahara National Hospital, where wounded amputee veterans are received. This writer reports that the hospital -- once the best in Japan -- "lost its function with the termination

of the war, and thousands of patients are now at a loss what to do. Very few artificial limbs are being manufactured now, and the price of an artificial leg assembly runs from four to five thousand yen." According to the author, one patient who lost an arm and a leg killed himself in despair over the cost of treatment "and the worry of feeding his wife and children at home." He further reports that the patients are keeping vegetable gardens to cover the shortage of foodstuffs, and are making clog-thongs in order to earn money for food -- while the Government "allows two and a half yen daily as a ration expense for each patient." He concludes: "Are these men victims of a malign fate that they must suffer harder under the cruel tortures of war while the rest of the world forgets?"

JITSUGYO NO NIPPON has made a survey of fourteen representative places of employment -- banks, industrial concerns, government offices and factories -- and states that the treatment of demobilized soldiers is generally good. "Most of these places of employment are accepting demobilees with kindness, even at the sacrifice of many positions which have hitherto been filled by others." The Government's new "Law of Protection of Livelihood" is praised by TOYO KEIZAI SHIMPO as being of great benefit to veterans, as well as to "all those who need protection owing to poverty caused by war calamities, repatriation, or those whose husbands or parents are dead or missing through the war." Going into a detailed description of the provisions of this law, this author commends its fairness. "The purpose (of this law) is to protect those who need assistance, without preferential treatment. Anyone may enjoy its privileges regardless of age or special qualification. However, protection may not be extended to those who have no will to work while retaining the capacity, nor to a family which has a member legally due to support it, save in urgent cases."

In a letter to the editor of GENRON, Haruhiko Takada, unemployed demobilized soldier, addresses himself to laborers, workers and farming people, urging them "to support a trustworthy government, recognizing that what establishes such a government is the strong unity of the people." Ascribing the present moral degradation to the present Government, Takada writes: "Morality has lost its hold on the people. The propertied classes can manage to change their frozen deposits to new yen, and to exchange goods for food -- but the common people have no money with which to buy and no property to exchange, and can only wait without hope. It seems as if the Government tacitly permits the strong to prey upon the weak. In such a state people will commit burglary, robbery, and murder in order to live. Far from reconstructing a peaceful Japan, such a condition will invite the destruction of our nation." He sees hope, however, "if we have a government -- with a strong political power stemming from the people -- which will share our destitution and trouble and unite our labor for increased production and reconstruction. The bright future of our nation will then be assured."

## The Black Market

With little else awaiting them, many demobilized men have drifted into black market pursuits. This is clearly shown by the volume of magazine articles on the subject, which either explain or excuse this phenomenon with a minimum of censure. Shinnosuke Abe, in SANDEI MAINICHI, relates a conversation with a sturdy young man on a train. After demobilization this youth became a laborer; he did twice the work of an ordinary man, but could not exist on the staple ration. Abe writes: "He could not live as a laborer and so changed his occupation to the black market, though against his will. We cannot but justify his actions, for if this livelihood is taken from him he may be driven to commit burglary or murder in order to exist."

Market-operator Seicho Kondo writes a piece for NIPPON SHUHO on the future of "open-air markets." After the end of the war, "many open-air markets began to appear and these carried out the mission of restoring the lost commerce of Japan. Most of the dealers are non-professional people such as repatriates from abroad, demobilized men, survivors of families killed in the war, and those who lost home or occupation by fire or bombings." Kondo refers to the "confused fighting of people and policemen in the Shibuya market," after which representative markets all over the city were closed. "It is clear that poor men such as repatriates and demobilees cannot pay high premiums and rentals demanded by new markets. The result would be that the new markets which are being built would be occupied only by rich merchants. The future of open-air markets has many pessimistic aspects; it will not, however, take the course of natural disappearance."

Another strong defender of the system is Rokuro Aruga, a member of the Metropolitan Commercial Industrial and Economic Council. Writing in JITSUGYO NO NIPPON, Aruga admits that the open-air markets have both merits and demerits. "They are not as prosperous as before, yet they will never perish and it would accordingly be better to protect them and amend their faults." He suggests that vendors be organized so that they may buy more cheaply; and also that if allowed to occupy the same place day by day they would be better able to take responsibility for what they sold. "When all daily necessities, not to speak of food, had vanished, these open-air markets appeared from nowhere to meet the demands of a hungry public. They gave means of living to war veterans, to those who suffered war damages and to other unemployed; they were forerunners of commercial resurrection and promoted the revival of other retail shops."

The Solution to conditions which drive so many repatriates and demobilees to black marketeering may lie in governmental measures for the relief of these men, in the opinion of Shinnosuke Abe writing in another issue of SANDEI MAINICHI, Abe, however, deplores such action as that taken by "the students relief movement for repatriates, in which about 300,000 students were forced to stand on the streets yelling for a relief fund, ending in failure. Judging from an individual standpoint it is a pathetic deed, morally, to allow them to engage in such a movement. * * * Democracy is a claim of man's free will, not to be confused with a war. Its responsibilities are part of the duties of every cabinet -- the present one is no exception. It is essential for them to produce a government which will be a credit to themselves, and to the people of Japan."

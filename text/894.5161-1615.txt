SUBJECT: Effect of Discontinuance of Payment of Interest on War Bonds.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to this Mission's despatch No. 50 of January 23, 1948 regarding observations made by Mr. KATAYAMA Tetsu, the Prime Minister, and to enclose copy of a memorandum of conversation between Mr. MATSUMOTO Shigeo, secretary to the Governor of the Bank of Japan, and one of the officers of this Mission. The Prime Minister's remark concerning the effect on banks of discontinuance of payment of interest on war bonds prompted pursuance of the question with Mr. Matsumoto.

Summary. Since, according to Mr. Matsumoto, seventy percent of war bonds are in the hands of banking institutions, discontinuance of interest on war bonds would result in a loss to them of five billion yen a year. If the banks should make up the loss by increasing the rates of interest which they charge, this would interfere with the government campaign for savings. To try to do so by raising interest rates on loans to industry would place an additional burden on industry. The only alternative would be to lower or discontinue interest on blocked accounts, but this would be inadequate. The burden of interest payments on war bonds is less now, because of inflation. In 1945, it was twenty-four percent of the budget, but in 1947 it was three and five tenths percent. Emphasis placed by leftist members of the Social Democratic Party on discontinuance is believed by Mr. Matsumoto to be an endeavor to place a further burden on financial institutions so that possibilities for nationalization may be increased.

Mr. YONEKUBO Mitsusuke, Minister of Labor, recently told an officer of this Mission that some attention is being given by the Government to a proposal to reduce the interest rate on the bonds rather than discontinue it entirely. He said that a rate of one percent had been suggested

MEMORANDUM OF CONVERSATION

SUBJECT: Effect of Discontinuance of Payment of Interest on War Bonds.

PARTICIPANTS: MATSUMOTO, Shigeo, Secretary to the Governor of the Bank of Japan. W. Henry LAWRENCE, Jr., Second Secretary of this Mission.

PLACES: Dinner given by Mr. ICHIMADA Heisato, Governor of the Bank of Japan at his official residence, January 20, 1948 and conference at Office of the United States Political Adviser, January 22, 1948.

Mr. Matsumoto stated that War Bonds amount to about 20,000,000,000 yen which at an interest rate of 3.5% means payment of 7,000,000,000 yen a year. He estimated that 70 percent of these war bonds are in banking institutions and ten to fifteen percent in the Bank of Japan. If interest on these bonds were discontinued as advocated by the leftists of the Social Democratic Party, it would mean that banking institutions of Japan would lose about 5,000,000,000 yen a year. Since the banks cannot absorb this loss, it would be necessary to either reduce interest rates on deposits or raise interest rates on loans.

Under the present circumstances Mr. Matsumoto stated either method would cause extreme difficulties. If interest were reduced on deposits, it would seriously interfere with the present campaign being conducted to increase savings as one means of fighting inflation. These so-called "free" deposits amount to about 220,000,000,000 yen. Mr. Matsumoto pointed out that it would also be difficult to raise interest rates on loans because it would place an additional burden upon industry which is now struggling to increase production.

The only alternative is to reduce or cancel interest on blocked accounts. These accounts consist of about 100,000,000,000 yen so that interest would amount to 2,000,000,000 yen a year. These blocked accounts, to which Mr. Matsumoto referred, do not come from the war, but are loans or payments by the government to industries having low priorities. Even if the banks discontinued interest on blocked accounts they would still have to find an additional 3,000,000,000 yen. Probably the only place they can get this amount is from the government. Mr. Matsumoto pointed out that the importance of interest on war bonds was quite different now in relation to the general budget than in the last three years. In 1945 the general budget amounted to about 29,000,000,000 yen, in 1946, 100,000,000,000 yen, but in 1947, 200,000,000,000. He emphasized that it was obvious therefore that the burden of payment of interest was less now than in 1945 and hence that the case for discontinuance of interest on war bonds was much stronger three years ago than now.

Mr. Matsumoto felt that the emphasis placed by the leftists of the Social Democratic Party on the importance of discontinuance of interest on war bonds stemmed from a desire to bring further pressures upon the banking system, hoping thereby to have increasing reason for nationalization of banks.

The following question and answer relative to the resumption of interest payments on Japanese bonds appeared in HANDARD, (House of Commons) February 3, 1948:

"Mr. Keeling asked the Chancellor of the Exchequer whether he will support American efforts to get the service of Japanese bonds resumed; and what information he has about Japanese plans for resuming the service.

"Sir S. Crippa: I am not acquainted with the efforts to which the hon. Member refers, but the rights of the bondholders will be borne in mind in the discussions leading up to the Peace Treaty. As regards the second part of the question, Press reports suggest that the Japanese Government is properly anxious to resume the service of its external debt; but the balance of payments position of Japan is such that it is not likely to be possible in the near future.

"Mr. Keeling: May I thank the Chancellor for this modification of the unhelpful attitude of his predecessor to British holders of these bonds, who, I hope he will agree, are just as patriotic and just as deserving of his support as any other British creditors of a foreign government?"

SUBJECT: Report covering points requested in War Department Radio W 88343, 18 May 1946, relating to Japanese penicillin production.

1. No pre-existing plants or facilities were available for production of penicillin by either surface or tank methods. Consequently mass production is not possible for sometime to come (See para. 5). About 20 small scale surface process plants are operating presently with total output a negligible fraction of Japan and export requirements. Further expansion of surface process is discouraged due to its inherent inefficiencies and resulting disparity in consumption of raw materials as compared to tank process. Current surface plants are mainly hand process and could be established on small scale quickly.

2. Main emphasis currently is on development of tank process. This involves 3 stages:

a. Laboratory, currently under investigation.

b. Pilot plant, currently under active development. Within 3 to 6 months about 10 pilot plants will have been completed and operating. Materials for pilot plant construction are readily available by adapting and altering equipment from other plants, which are idle or partially bombed out. Certain smaller penicillin manufacturers are readily getting their pilot plants fabricated by engineering construction companies who apparently are eager for jobs of this kind and who have facilities for design and heavy construction. Larger penicillin firms are manufacturing on the premises of huge plants now inoperative for a variety of reasons. These organizations have skilled engineering and mechanical staffs, machine shops, etc. and much spare equipment, piping, etc. which make pilot plant construction an easy job.

c. Full plant scale. Detailed consideration of this phase has not yet been given due to intense preoccupation with pilot. Inspection of many large heavy industrial plants of every variety indicates the existence of a huge reservoir in Japan of chemical engineering apparatus and materials including tanks, piping, fitting, valves, pumps, sharples supercentrifuges, steam power plants, electric generators, air compressors, brine cooling units, etc., all easily adaptable to penicillin plants. Almost all these plants are shut down entirely due to lack of raw materials or are partially bombed out. Not only are such items available within many of plants of prospective penicillin manufacturers, but seemingly unlimited amounts of them could be rehabilitated from plants totally out of the penicillin program. To provide special pieces of apparatus or for heavy rehabilitation or alteration work there appears to be sufficient engineering construction facilities available in Japan. Certain few types

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to forward five copies of a report prepared by the Hypothec Bank of Japan on the subject of the Vitamin Oil Industry in Japan.

The enclosed report contains several statistical inconsistencies; it will be noted, for instance, that two separate and irreconcileable sets of figures are given for 1947 production goals on page 58 under the heading "Production Program and Actual Output". It is felt, however, in spite of these discrepancies, that the report may be of interest to the Departments of State and Commerce.

Japan's production of vitamin oils, according to the enclosed report, reached a peak of 450,800 kilograms in 1940, declining thereafter. By 1947 the industry had begun to revive, production being four times that of 1946 and nearly two-thirds that of 1940. The goal for 1948 was set at 320,124 kilograms; the Hypothec Bank has informed this office that actual production reached 427,400 kilograms, a total second only to that for 1940.

Production was somewhat erratic in 1947, with goals for Vitamin-A oil being considerably exceeded, but with Vitamin-D oil, and, in the last quarter, Processing Oil, lagging behind.

Large-scale export of vitamin oils began only in 1939, according to the enclosed report. Pre-war exports were shipped predominantly to the United States. No figures are given for total post-war exports, but it is noted that exports to the United States totalled 63,605 kilograms in 1947, a marked increase over the figure for 1946.

## Vitamin Oil Industry in Japan
INFORMATION ON EXPORT INDUSTRIES—No. 8

### I. Introductory Remarks.

The vitamin-oil industry in Japan is rather of recent origin. It may be traced to the manufacture of cod-liver oil commenced on a small scale in 1888 at Rebun Island off Hokkaido. Fourteen years later, it developed into the cod-liver oil industry in Hokkaido and Korea. Afterwards, several species of fish other than cod came to be noticed for their vitamin contents. This, together with improvement in the manufacturing technique, resulted in the production of liver-oils of denser quality, or vitamin-oils. The recent great strides made by this young industry are largely due to the favorable geographical condition of this country, viz. the ease with which fish-livers can be obtained in abundance, and to the increased public demand for the products as a result of the spread among the people of the knowledge of the nutritive value of vitamins.

With the restoration of peace, the industry emerged from its wartime stagnancy, as an important factor in the economic reconstruction of Japan. Its output is steadily increasing, the exports in 1947 amounting to 68,600 kgs. (¥78,000,000).

The industry has a very fair prospect of export, provided that the supply of its chief raw material, or fish-livers, will be fully secured with the recovery of our fisheries.

## (5) Manufacturing Process.

The methods of obtaining oils are generally standardized, the pressure method being adopted for livers rich in oil contents and the alkali method for livers containing less oil.

Though the details may vary with the factories, the processes most generally followed are as follows:—

The same process is used for both vitamin A and vitamin D.

(a) The pressure method.—The livers are first washed in fresh water, then sorted in salt water. Those coming up to the surface of the water are placed in digesters containing water, wherein the livers are heated by pressure steam. Then, they are left still in a paste-like condition.

Oil gradually exudes, whilst greaves sink. The crude liver oil thus obtained is cooled down to a temperature below 0°C and filtered to free it from wax substance.

(b) The alkali method.—The livers are cut up into small fragment, and heated for 40 minutes in a 2% solution of caustic soda.

In the case of livers containing much vitamin (tuna, for example) 10% of processing oil is added after they have been heated. Then, by the action of alkali solution, the albuminous matter in the livers become soluble. The crude oil comes up to the surface, partly forming emulsion. If left still, the oil separates itself into three layers, from the above, in the order of oils, emulsion and waste fluids. The oil is separated from the other two mixtures by employing super centrifugal separators.

## (6) Technical Level.

Because of its dependence for raw material on fish, the industry is limited to some particular areas. Even in U.S.A., one of the few countries where the industry is conducted to any appreciable extent, production is below the domestic demand. It is said that the Japanese products generally contain vitamins as much as several times those of other countries. This is because the fish livers acquired in Japan contain much vitamins. Future improvement in the technique will lead to the production of vitamin oils of higher international unit.

As regards the volume of output, there is still room for expansion. As an illustration of this, we may take the case of cod. Cod-livers afford oil at an average yield of 35%, which, however, may be raised to a still higher figure by technical improvement.

Those plants which lie remotely from the fishing grounds find it difficult to obtain oils at the above-mentioned average yield as the result of the use of frozen fish. In the Hokkaido plants, where the method employed does not show any progress beyond a primitive stage, the yield is in the neighbourhood of 30 per cent.

What is required of the industry, therefore, is to remove the technical defects and thereby make itself capable of meeting the demand from overseas.

### IV. Demand &amp; Supply Condition of Raw Materials.

The principal materials of the industry are livers, caustic soda and coal, of which livers are most important. Indeed, the prospect of the industry is largely dependent on whether livers can be supplied in adequate amount. Link system is adopted for the rationing of these materials, which, however, fall short of actual requirements.

#### (1) Livers.

Japan had been, in pre-war days, such a fishing country that she accounted for 40 per cent of the world’s total catch. But the loss of the northern territorial waters in consequence of her defeat in the war led to a sharp decline in the amount of livers collected, which went down in 1946 to 683,543 kgs. or 12 per cent of the 1940 figure of 5,734,000 kgs. The kinds of fish which are employed to obtain vitamin A and D oils, are fatbass, large and small tunas, swordfish, shark, bonito, mackerel, amberjack, whale, flatfish, yellow fin tuna, and young tuna. The Vitamin Oil Industry Co., Ltd. is the sole purchaser of these fishes and livers which are then allocated by the company to the makers.

The company is now in shortage of funds and unable to purchase livers up to its requirements. Though the makers are endeavoring to fill up the gap by means of direct purchase from fishing companies, the money difficulty also hinders them from acquiring supplies as much as they need. This keeps them down at an operation of 30 to 40 per cent.

#### (2) Caustic soda, coal, processing oil, etc.

Next, let us consider the problem of secondary materials such as caustic soda, coal, processing oil, etc. On the average, 700 kgs. of coal, 60 kgs. of caustic soda and 181.5 kgs. of processing oil required for producing 180 kgs.

## V. Demand and Supply Condition of Product.

Vitamin oils are in great demand in overseas countries, especially in the United States, and the trade circles place future possible exports at some ¥100 million in a year.

The products of low international unit are sold in a variety of forms to domestic consumers and the demand for them is more than the industry can meet. From the present situation of this country, great importance is attached to the promotion of export, with more than 70 per cent of production being shipped out to overseas countries.

## VIII. Trade Organization.

The Vitamin Oil Industry Co. is the unifying institution for the enterprises in the industry, engaged in the procurement and distribution of raw materials and products.

It is thought that the company may be absorbed into the Edible Oil Public Corporation to be organized shortly. The aim of the Vitamin Oil Trade Council is the promotion of friendly relations and joint technical research, while Vitamin-A.D. Association, which is organized by makers of drugs from vitamin oils, has a membership of more than 50.

## IX. Conclusion.

With the progress of scientific researches into vitamins, the products have come in more and more demand both at home and abroad, and the opinions of the trade circles are that the American demand will enable Japan to ship out up to the value of ¥100 million per year.

That Japan is a sea-girt country places her in an advantageous position for obtaining livers, and she is in self-sufficiency in respect of secondary materials. Moreover, since the products fetch a good price in the market, we may expect the industry to play no small part in making Japan’s trade balance favourable.

However, a number of problems confront the industry for solution, such as the poor showing in procuring livers owing to straitened money situation and the consequent lowering in the rate of operation, the modernizing of plant and equipment. At any rate, the depression in liver procurement, among others, is the greatest bottleneck to an increased production. A bright prospect can be guaranteed provided only this problem will be solved.

## X. Appendix.

### (1) Liver oil.

#### (a) Cod-liver oil.

This is obtained from the livers of codfish or pollack. The Japanese products are by far superior in quality to those of others countries, containing five or six times as much vitamin A.

The codfish affords oil of better quality than the pollack. The former is caught in the seas about Hokkaido and Saghalien, while the latter is found mainly in the north Japan Sea coasts. Among the foreign countries, the Norwegian and the Scottish coasts are noted.

The vitamin-A content of cod-liver oils varies with the year and with the place where the fish was acquired, but the most striking variation is produced by seasons. To be more particular, the cod or pollack caught during the period from November to February next year afford the least vitamin A whereas the after March catch has a far greater content. This is because the spawning season falls to the months of January and February.

The dilated livers immediately before the spawning afford oil in larger quantity, but of poorer quality, while denser oil is obtained,

(b) Vitamin-oil.

The vitamin or the denser liver-oil generally contains more than 30,000 international units of vitamin. It is intended chiefly for export, being in little demand in the country on account of its high cost.

Though the differentiation is made between the vitamin-A oil and the vitamin-D oil this does not mean that these two products contain exclusively vitamin-A or D. They are differently so called according which of the two vitamins they contain in larger quantity.

In these two tables, the facts which require our attention are as follows:

1. Of the aggregate bank loans of ¥250 billion at the end of June 1948, 71.4% was accounted for by loans to the fields of industries, power and transportation. This points to the fact that Japan seeks to rehabilitate her economy on the basis of mining and manufacturing industries.

2. The amount of loans outstanding in July 1948 shows an increase of ¥142.6 billion (or 133%) over the end of September of the year preceding. When revalued in terms of price indices, this still represents a ¥92.8 billion increase.

3. The power field, i.e., coal and electricity etc., is in the lead in respect of the increase of loans, with transportation business second.

Of the industrial loans which amount to ¥121.2 billion, or 48.5% of the total, 60% is accounted for by textiles, machine and tools, and chemicals. On the other hand, reduction is witnessed in other branches of industry, i.e., building etc., commerce and other miscellaneous trades, indicating the severity of money difficulties under which these businesses find themselves.

The increasing volume of loans to the field of power and transportation, in contrast to the depressing situation in general industries, tells the strengthened enforcement of the priority system in production and credit in favour of the key industries.

It appears that the promotion of export industries is a problem whose solution is left to the future.

INTRODUCTION
The closing quotation for raw silk on the Yokohama
Silk Exchange in December 1939 was Yen 2330 per bale.
One year before the cl osine cuotation was Yen B45. In
terms of American dollars, raw silk increased during
the year from 1.86 to $4.31 per pound.*
This rapid and almost unprecedented inerease has
had profound and aisturbing effects on two American
industries; silk hosiery and silk textiles. Further-
more, it has meant that the American consumer of silk
stock ings has found it necessary to curtail purchases,
or to expand her expenditures for this product.
The rise in si lk prices occurred because domestic
factors in Japan, for the first time, became momentarily
more important' than did marketing conditions abroad.
Economic trends in Japan will continue to play a part
in future price movements. The purpose of this report
is to outline and evaluate the various aspects of those
economic forces which are most profoundly influencing
Japan's silk industry. Such a study involves the
relationship of raw si lk to:
1 - the economic position of the
Japanese farmer,
2 - general inflationary price trends
in Japan,
3 - the increased use of silk domestically,
and
4 - the competitive power of the raw silk
industry in relation to rayon and
Nylon.

The extreme deflation in raw silk prices during
the years following 1929 has had marked effects on
the position occupied by sericulture in Japan's
agricultural economy. There follows en outline of the
more important changes which occurred between l929 and

A. Mulberry Acreage
In l929, Japan's mulberry acreage amounted to
626,000 cho, or approximately 1,533,700 acres. In
1930, this acreage increased to 714,000 cho.
Beginning in 193l, acreage decreased each year until
in 1938 it totaled only 549,000 cho, or approximately
1,345,00 acres. The decrease between 1929 and 1938
was 12.3 percent.
Mulberry acreage in 1929 accounted for 10.6 parcent of
the total area in Japan under cultivation. By 1938
this peroentage had fallen to 9.0.
The downward trend in mulberry acreage was contrary
to the general movement in Japanese agrioulture. Total
area under cultivation rose from 5,897,434 cho in 1939,
to 6,078,285 cho in 1938, an increase of 3.1 percent.

The years 1930 - 1936 constituted a periou or

As is indicated in Table XI, annual raw silk ex-

however, at a tremendous sacrifice in price depreciation.
Of the factors responsible for the fall in prices, mention
has already been made of the failure to make adequate
downward adjustments in mulberry acreage, cocoon production,
and raw silk output, in the period following the peak years
of 1927, 1928 and 1929. Other factors which forced a con-
tinued deflation of raw silk prices will be outlined in
later sections of the report.
The years 1936, 1937 and 1938, were relatively
stable in the si lk industry and it was believed that
annual exports could be held at a level of 475,000 -
500,000 bal es; provided a proper price ratio between
silk and rayon was maintained.
In 1930 and 1931, the United States took more than
95 peroent of the raw silk exported from Japan. This
proportion declined until a low point of 80.3 peroent
was reached in 1937. During this period, there had
been a marked expansion in exports to areas other than

The export stability which had been maintained

Exports to countries other than the United States

decrease was due to smaller shipments to France, and
most of the balance represented the curtailment of ship-
ments abroad of government silk.

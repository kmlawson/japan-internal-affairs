The war time petroleum refining capacity of Japan was approximately
110,000 barrels of 42 U.S. gallons per day of wh1ch some 15,000 to 20,000
barrels per day was located in areas of indigenous production and was em-
ployed exclusively for processing indigenous crude. Comparatively little
loss in the refining capacity used on indigenous crude was sustained dur-
ing the war with the result that the existing capacity in the producing
areas is still roughly double that recuired to process the maximum indigen-
ous crude estimated to be produced in the foreseeable future. The refin-
ing capacity built to process 1mported crude (between 90,000 and 95,000
barrels per day) suffered heavily from bombing but even so, approximately
40% (say 38,000 barrels per day) will be useable after some repairs have
been made. Allowing for only 50% use of the refining capacity located in
the indigenous areas and assuming full use o the above mentioned 38,000
barrel per dey capacity designed for processing imported crude, it is es-
timated that Japan will have an annual potential output of approximately
15,000,000 barrels of refined petroleum products.
Estimated consumption of petroleum products in Japan has been plac-
ed et 8,000,000 barrels in 1947, r1sing perhaps to15,000,000 barrels by 1952.
It is therefore obvious that without drastic reduction in the refin-
ing capacity there will not be room for the sale of imported products for
some considerable time.
The Standard-Vacuum O1l Com any had an investment in Japan, exclusive
of Manchuria, Korea and Formosa, of approximately $6,000,O00, devoted en-
tirely to distributi anj selling Imported products. (Rising Sun, a sub-
sidiary of Shell (British), occupied a similar position.) Standard-
Vacuum's properties suffered heavy damage as a result of bombing and the
problem of rehabilitation when repossessed requires early planning. The
degree to which the properties will be rehabilitated will depend upon the
character of Standard-Vacuum's post-war trade in Japan. If heavy inroads
on Japan's refining capacity are made for reparations, Standard-Vacuum will
be justified in making plans for serving a trade in imported products as it
did prewar. If only modest reduction in refining capacity results under
Reparations Policies, Standard-Vacuum will have no alternative course of
action exeept to acquire its own refining capacity in Japan, and to rehab-
ilitate such of its properties as will be useful. Viewing refining cape-
city as war-making potential, it would be logical to expect Japan's re-
fining capacity to be reduced to that necessary to process indigenous
erude. However, 1t appears from Press end other reports, that some
economists who have made studies of the subject, find arguments for pre-
serving some or all of the refinins capacity used for processing imported
crude.
Doubtless U.S. Government's Reparations Policy will be determimed
after due consideration has been iven to all factors involved, e.g.
war-makins potential, national economy of Japan and internationsl rela-
tions affecting the defense of U. S. A., as well as American trade relations.

I. THE PROBLEM.
1. General Order No. 6, dated 2 October 1945,

policies relating to organizations, financial structures,
and subsidies affecting mining products, including
petroleum.
2. In pursuit of this mission, the following
problem is presented:
a. A large unified organization is
technologically essential to maintain petroleum production
in Japan, by co-ordinating exploitation of present produc-
ing fields and conducting intensive exploration for new
reserves.
b. The existing large organization, Imperial
Oil Co, controls 95 percent of petroleum production, but
its mixed government and private ownership is an unde-
sirable feature for an organization having a virtual
monopoly of one of the nation's vital resources.
c. The problem lies in establishment of a
large organization, within the limits of policy relating
to monopolies and fair trade, which can effectively main-
tain and possibly increase production of crude oil. A
bill is now being drafted by the  inistry of Commerce and
Industry for presentation to the Diet to establish such an
organization in the form of a Government Commission. The
main provisions of this Bill are outlined in Inclosure 1
(Tab A).
II. FACTS BEARING ON THE PROBLEM.
1. Imperial Cil Co was established by the Japanese
Government in 1941 in an attempt to halt the decline in pro-
duction by co-ordinating activities within the producing
industry. The structure and history of Imperial Oil Co and
its relation to the general situation of the producing
industry are outlined in Inclosure 2 (Tab B).
2. Indigenous production has gained considerably
in importance in relation to consumption since the end of
hostilities; indigenous production now provides about 25
percent of consumed petroleum products. Maintenance of
production is essential, and increase is desirable, in
order to reduce expenditure of dollar credits on imported

4. The only means by which current production can
be maintained, and possibly increased, is by undertaking
intensive exploration, followed closely by drilling and
rapid exploitation. Such a program has been/operation for
the past year and, despite considerable handicaps inherent
in the existin organization, some successes have been ob-
tained.
5. Japan's oil resources will certainly be in-
creased by application of modern methods of exploration.
Japan has the necessary potential to develop her oil
resources efficiently, if a suitable organization is per-
mitted.
6. Cil exploration involves a huge financial
gamble which can be undertaken only by an organization
with large financial resources, and a large pool of equip-
ment and technical personnel, welded into an integrated
organization which can co-ordinate and follow up the many
phases between initial exploration survey and final pro-
duction of oil.
III. DISCUSSION.
1. Since the structure of Imperial Oil Co con-
flicts with existin policy concerning monopolistic enter-
prises, various proposals involving partition, either
geographically or functionally, were seriously studied.
Any form of partition would, however, result in weakening
of the overall effort and affect the national interest
adversely.
2. Vithdrawal of government interest in the
existin half-government, half-private organization would
leave a large private enterprise, reduced in capital, with
a virtual monopoly on production areas and leases on almost
all potential areas in Japan, with no guarantee that the
exploration program vital to the national interest would be
carried out. Political, social, financial, and economic
factors militate against the possibility of achieving the
sweeping reforms which would be necessary in establishing
an efficient private body from the existing organization.

Dear Mr. Clayton:
For more than five years Japanese have had possession
of storage and distribution properties owned by the Standard-Vacuum
Oil Company in Japan. During the greater part of this period Standard-
Vacuum properties have been used by the Petroleum Distribution Control
Company, a Japanese oil monopoly. This situation continues today and,
it is believed, but neither denied nor confirmed, that a charter is
about to be granted by the Japanese Government which will continue
this monopoly in Japanese hands. Standard-Vacuum considers the con-
tinued exclusion from its properties and its trade (1) highly detri-
mental to its pre-war investments in Japan amounting to about
$6,000,000, and its pre-war position as a distributor of finished
petroleum products throughout Japan (2) directly contrary to the an-
nounced policy of our government as to safeguarding of American invest-
ments and trade abroad, and (3) likely to lead to eventual nationaliza-
tion of the petroleum industry.
Standard-Vacuum is acquainted with and appreciates the
reasons voiced by the U.s. authorities against the return of trade re-
presentatives to Japan. However, there is a marked difference between,
and it seems ineouitable to treat alike, the interests of firms, simply
importers and/or exporters and firms having large pre-war investments
in Japan for land, buildings, equipment, distributing and technical or-
ganization. Standard-Vacuum believes they are entirely justified in
asking that consideration be given to this difference and that firms
with large material interests in Japan should be privileged to re-
possess and safeguard such interests. This should not create diffi-
cult nor insurmountable obstacles since the number of firms, American
or other foreign nationals, having like interests in Japan is not large.
The following approach to this problem seems feasible
and is offered for the consideration of the authorities concerned:
Mr. H. W. Daniels, the Company's General Manager for
Japan and now Chairman of the Petroleum Advisory Group, and three other
senior experienced executives have been in Tokyo since shortly after oc-
cupation, entirely at Company expense, attached to the Supreme Commander
Allied Powers as Civilian Oil Advisers. It is suggested that Mr.
Daniels or Mr. R. C. Ludlum (also P.A.G.) be authorized to act for
Standard-Vacuum and, in conjunction with and under approval of local
authorities, to make and carry out plans (a) for the protection, preser-
vation and the necessary reconstruction of the Company's properties
and facilities; (b) for the same privileges of supervision and manage-
ment over its pre-war distributing organization and participation in
the civilian petroleum consumption as are now permitted to Japanese

ed 90,000 barrels of heavy
black fuel oil purchased in

Navy contract.

dustrially. Some of it may be
refined into lubricating oil and
gasoline.
Informed sources explained
the use of Japanese tankers
will make more American
tankers available for United
States use on oil hauls.
One United States Navy of-
ficer will be aboard each Japa-
nese ship for the long voyage
through the Indian Ocean and
Arabian sea to Bahrein.
Some Japanese tankers to be
used on this operation were
used last winter in whaling
operations in the Antarctic.
The entire project is under
the direction of United States
Navy authorities here.

any margin for profits. At present the various oil

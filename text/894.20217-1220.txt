The Department has received information from various sources that individuals of the Japanese race have in recent months been engaged in what appear to be topographical surveys along the Pacific and Caribbean coasts of certain Latin American countries. Still our navy's one vital short cut: the Panama Canal.

READING TIME • 9 MINUTES 35 SECONDS

"If one Roosevelt could build the Panama Canal, why cannot another Roosevelt build the Nicaragua Canal?" This remark, reported by a former President of Nicaragua, may well embody one of Franklin D. Roosevelt's most cherished ambitions.

In 1916 we paid Nicaragua $3,000,000 for the privilege of building the canal. Before we can do so, it will be necessary to make another deal with Nicaragua and pay her more money. In the meantime no one else can build a canal, if we don't. The Navy endorsed the Nicaragua Canal. Congress is not averse. But it is believed in Washington that abandonment of the project may be one of the conditions laid down by Tokyo before it will discuss limitation of armaments with the United States. The almost complete cessation of any talk about the Nicaragua Canal, the recent ominous silence of Congress, lend color to this conjecture.

But the Japanese war lords are realists. They do not entirely rely on diplomacy. Those familiar with Nicaraguan affairs claim that Japan is conspiring with the present Dictator of Nicaragua, General Somoza, to sabotage the canal if possible and in any event to spread over the entire region a net of espionage.

About 1895 Japan tried to secure from Nicaragua a concession to build and dominate the canal. At that time sinister negotiations were going on between Nicaragua, Mexico, and Japan. The relations with Mexico and Japan have never been sufficiently scrutinized. They may be of grave importance in case of a war between the United States and Japan. The close relations existing between our neighbor republics and Japan did not escape European observation. That fact explains in part the proposal in the notorious Zimmermann note for an alliance between Mexico, Germany, and Japan, which was to restore to Mexico her "lost provinces." Texas, California, etc. Fortunately, Japanese attempts to acquire the Nicaragua Canal more than four decades ago were frustrated by vigilant American interests. After that, until a short time ago, Japan ignored Nicaragua.

But in the last few years Nicaragua has received a great deal of attention from Tokyo. Within the present year Japan has sent an expert to show Nicaraguan planters how to prepare their cotton for export to her. The expert announced that Japan would buy the entire crop. The purchase price that was agreed upon nets

![img-1.jpeg](img-1.jpeg)

a profit of nearly 100 per cent to Nicaraguan planters. Not content with gobbling up all the cotton, the Japanese buy cotton seed, rubber, hides, scrap iron, deerskins, dyewoods, etc., in Nicaragua. Recently Kemichi Toniti, an emissary of the Japanese Ministry of Commerce and Industry, made a mysterious visit to Nicaragua. For the first time in history, Japan found it advisable to send a Minister Plenipotentiary and Envoy Extraordinary, Saichiro Koshida, to Managua, the capital of Nicaragua. His arrival, in spite of its significance, received no notice in the United States. In Nicaragua he was welcomed with public rejoicings and feasts lasting seven days.

Those familiar with events behind the scenes recall that three years ago Rodolfo Espinosa, Vice-President of Nicaragua, received a mysterious invitation to visit Japan. He asked the President, Dr. Juan B. Sacasa, to entrust him with an official mission. The President, stancily American in sympathy, refused. The Vice-President made the trip nevertheless and was received in private audience by the Emperor of Japan, who also pinned on his breast a high Japanese decoration.

I have it on indisputable authority that one such fleet landed, not in the regular harbor, nor provided with proper landing papers and passports, but in a remote region important only in connection with the projected canal. This fleet's Japanese apologized when the then President of Nicaragua, who was friendly to the United States, protested. Incidentally, not very long ago a Japanese "fishing boat" belonging to a similar fleet was found in Californian waters. In the boat were instruments and records which had nothing to do with fishing, unless it was fishing for naval secrets.

General Anastasio Somoza, Dictator of Nicaragua, who ejected the legally elected President after the United States withdrew its marines, is on exceedingly cordial terms with the Japanese. He also seems to be exceedingly well supplied with funds. Some people suspect that, while pretending friendliness to the United States, he is playing the game of Japan.

Neither Japan nor Nicaragua can in the long run prevent us from building the canal, but they can sabotage the work and delay it. Moreover, Japanese spies can familiarize themselves with every secret of the canal's construction. Japanese colonists, settling along the route, would be in a position to do severe damage in case of war.

By some curious oversight, the United States has made no agreement with Costa Rica, Honduras, and Salvador, although the proposed canal traverses their territory. It is not believed that it will be difficult to come to terms with these small Central American states, unless Japanese money begins to roll in as it does in Nicaragua. Our treaty with Nicaragua does not expire until the year 2015, and is renewable at our option. Between now and then many things may happen, including a world war. Our very existence may depend upon this canal.

The Panama Canal is a miracle of engineering, but it is extremely vulnerable to the elements and to enemy attacks. As long as it is our only short cut between the two oceans, our naval power can be cut in two by a slight earthquake, a mere shiver of the earth, or by an enemy bomb. The naval strategy of the United States is based upon quick access from the Atlantic to the Pacific. If such access becomes doubtful, it will be necessary for us to build another navy, equal in size to our present fleet, and hold it in the Pacific.

The Panama Canal is approximately fifty miles long; the Nicaragua Canal will be three times as long, but it is 400 miles nearer to San Francisco. It will save, therefore, 800 miles to any ship going and returning through its mighty locks. It is less likely to be affected by earthquakes. Moreover, it will be necessary to dig only seventeen miles; the rest of the Nicaragua Canal will go through natural waterways. The most important of these is Lake Nicaragua, large enough to afford anchorage to all the navies of the world, assembled. Its sweet water will wash the barnacles off ships from the salt seas. It will be easy, moreover, to provision our ships in this region, for the land is rich not only in fruit but in cattle, in which Panama is notoriously lacking.

Both Representative Carl Vinson of Georgia, chairman of the Naval Affairs Committee of the House, and Senator Walsh of Massachusetts, chairman of the Naval Affairs Committee of the Senate, consider a canal through Nicaragua and Costa Rica essential to our national defense. They hold that the new waterway will facilitate our commercial relations with Latin America.

The Nicaragua Canal was first discussed in 1848. Nearly twenty years ago a commission reported that the Nicaragua route "had greater advantages and fewer difficulties" than any other. Uncle Sam listened, and fell asleep again. Now the total expense of building the canal will be $772,000,000. It will take all of eight to ten years to complete the job. Fifty million dollars will be required for defense and twenty-five million for the acquisition of rights, franchises, and lands. These last-mentioned figures are from the estimate of Lieutenant Colonel I. Sultan, who headed an expedition of two hundred American engineers to Nicaragua.

The canal will be approximately 172.8 miles in length. The route recommended by Lieutenant Colonel Sultan starts at Brito on the Pacific and follows the Rio Grande to the low divide between the basins of the Rio Grande and Las Lajas. From there it goes down the valley into Lake Nicaragua. Thence it leads to the region of the Conchuda Hills. After following the San Juan River, it proceeds through the valley of the Danta and the San Francisco River and the valley of the Limpio. The final stretch leads down the Valley of Desado to the Caribbean Sea north of Greytown.

Lieutenant Colonel Sultan affirms that the Nicaragua Canal will provide a shorter route from port to port for about two thirds of the vessels which now go through the Panama Canal. A hostile power, he reasons, would find it difficult to capture or destroy both the canals. Aerial reinforcements from the United States could reach Nicaragua more quickly than Panama. The facilities of two canals will give flexibility and greatly add security to our naval operations. Sultan concedes that the defense of the two canals requires stronger forces and is exposed to certain inherent weaknesses when compared to defense of but one. However, the United States will be able to provide ample defensive forces at both canals without unduly jeopardizing its major military objective.

THE distances between the canal termini—290 miles by sea and air from Colón to Greytown on the Atlantic, and 750 miles by sea and 470 by air from Panama to Brito on the Pacific—are not actually great. The defensive air forces, therefore, will support each other, and so can the naval defensive forces. "The difficulty of defending two canals is not," Lieutenant Colonel Sultan insists, "double that of protecting one, nor are any obstacles insuperable."

President Taft, keenly interested in the project, negotiated a treaty with Nicaragua. Much to the surprise of the Nicaraguans, President Wilson—spurred by Colonel House—resumed and successfully concluded the negotiations. The Bryan-Chamorro Treaty of 1916 gave us a vested interest in the canal. Then the World War crashed upon us and the scheme was once more forgotten.

A startling look at the evidence of intrigue in Nicaragua—and what it may mean to the U.S.A.

When an engineer became President it was revived. On his South American trip, undertaken before his inauguration, Hoover asked for a secret conference with President Adolfo Diaz of Nicaragua, and discussed the problem of the canal in detail. It was his intention to set the wheels for its construction in motion at once. But the depression swept this aside, along with other plans of his.

Meanwhile Nicaragua herself passed through much turmoil. When the American marines were withdrawn, all armed forces were united in a National Guard. At the suggestion of the American Minister, all Presidential candidates pledged themselves to keep the guard non-political. However, General Somoza expelled the duly elected candidate of the Liberal Party, Sacasa. Then, holding the country under his thumb, he made himself President.

Behind the screen of Somoza's dictatorship the strange things that have been mentioned are going on. The currency of the country has dropped to a low level, but the President himself is said to be suffering no hardship. His army is well paid. Japanese influence and Japanese intrigue grow apace in Nicaragua. The pro-Japanese Somoza is said to have the backing of only ten per cent of the population; his regime rests mainly on the bayonets of the National Guard. Yet it is Somoza with whom the United States must negotiate a new treaty before the Nicaragua Canal can be actually built!

# Banano y algodón

El Dr. Raúl Gurdán, ex Ministro de Hacienda de Costa Rica, quien se halla en Managua, declaró a un distinguido amigo nuestro, que la siembra de banano en las costas del Pacífico es el *desideratum* de Nicaragua y Costa Rica.

Nuestro apreciado interlocutor agrega que el Banco Nacional puede patrocinar el siguiente plan: Que se siembre banano en abril y después de la limpia de julio, se siembre algodón en agosto en el mismo campo. Esto no es un ensayo o experimento, pues ya se ha hecho esto con buen resultado en Chinandega.

Es sabido que el banano paga los gastos todos con el producto de la primera cosecha y de consiguiente quedará la utilidad plena del algodón al agricultor.

Para esta finalidad es necesario que el Banco se decida ya a dar las habilitaciones para que el agricultor esté en aptitud de preparar la tierra y sembrar el banano en el próximo mes de abril.

En Costa Rica ya se está sembrando en el Pacífico. Lo único que debe preveer el Banco es que los nuevos bananales queden en una zona donde pueda ser recogido por los vapores de la United Fruit que pasen de Costa Rica a California.

He aquí, pues, un medio expedito y seguro para procurar oro, y salir de esta crisis.

Jamaica exporta al año más de 20 millones de dólares en banano, en tierras de condición inferior de las de Chinandega.

Para justificar esta afirmación basta saber que plantaciones de banano en Chinandega de más de cuarenta años de edad producen exuberantes cosechas, pues las tierras riquísimas son inagotables.

En la Costa del Pacífico no hay ciclones. En la Costa del Pacífico la capa de tierra vegetal es diez veces más profunda que la del Atlántico.

Por allá hay pestes y plagas que en el Pacífico no se conocen.

Tan solo es necesario que haya en el país quien haga las habilitaciones.

- Translation -

BANANAS AND COTTON

Dr. Raul Gurdian, formerly Minister of Hacienda of Costa Rica, who is now in Managua, told a distinguished friend of ours that banana-planting on the Pacific coasts is the need of Nicaragua and Costa Rica.

Our worthy speaker adds that the National Bank can stand behind the following plan: Let bananas be planted in April and after the July cleanings cotton may be sowed on the same field. This is not a trial or an experiment, for it has successfully been done in Chinandega.

It is known that banana planting pays its costs with the first crop and consequently the full value of the cotton is net profit to the planter.

For this purpose, it is necessary for the Bank now to decide to furnish funds so that the planter may be able to prepare his soil and to plant bananas this coming April.

In Costa Rica, planting on the Pacific Coast has already begun. The only thing the Bank must do is to see that the new banana plantations be in a zone where they may be picked up by United Fruit steamers on their way from Costa Rica to California.

In this there is an expeditious and safe way of getting gold to get us out of this crisis.

Jamaica exports every year more than $20,000,000 worth of bananas from soil inferior in quality to that of Chinandega.

To justify this statement it is sufficient to know that banana plantations in Chinandega more than forty years old produce exuberant crops - for the richness of the earth there is inexhaustible.

On the Pacific Coast there are no cyclones. On the Pacific Coast the stratum of vegetable soil is two times as deep as that of the Atlantic.

On the Atlantic side there are insect pests and plagues unknown to the Pacific.

All we need is someone to furnish the cash.

I am just in receipt of information to the effect that Fernando H. Rothe of San Jose, Manager of the "Compania Comisica-ista de Bienes Raices", has been or is engaged in negotiations with Japanese now in Costa Rica, covering the sale to the latter of a tract of land of approximately 150,000 hectares in Nicaragua, in the vicinity of the San Juan River. Rothe is stated to have an option on this tract. It is stated to have a great quantity of hardwood on it and that it is admirably suited to the raising of cotton and/or bananas.

I believe it hardly necessary to point out our interest in any such transaction of a large area in the vicinity of the projected Nicaraguan Canal. Although the difficulties of obtaining further information on negotiations are fully appreciated, any further detail you may be able to gather on the subject will be greatly appreciated.

In connection with the foregoing, I might state that I consider Rothe quite a "slick" individual and on the other hand, he is quite a live-wire. Incidentally, he is the one who first got the Creevy brothers interested in Costa Rican manganese. I believe it would be best that he, himself, be not approached on the subject.

Thanking you for any further information it may be possible for you to gather on this important subject, I am, with best wishes,

Sincerely yours,

IN THE ABSENCE OF THE M.A.

Alex A. Cohen,
In charge of office.

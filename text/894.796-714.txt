Due to the economio depression of Japan as a whole
it is not likely that there will be any considerable in-

loaded to full capacity. The Japan Transport Company is

near future. The inaccessibility of the aviation terminals
1s a big factor in limiting the demand for planes as the
inconvenience caused by the difficulty of reaching the

INFORMATION CONCERNING TOKYO DISTRICT
Area, Climate and Topography.—The Tokyo consular district comprises all of Japan from the region adjacent to Tokyo north-
ward, and its area is 79,693 square miles. In the southern portion of the district the climate approximates that of Maryland,
while that of Hokkaido, in the North, resembles the climate of Maine. The region adjacent to Tokyo is an extensive alluvial
plain of great fertility, and there are a number of other coastal plains and river valleys in the district, all intensively cultivated,
chiefly by hand labor. The major portion of the District, however, is covered with mountains, many of which are volcanic.
Population, Race, Language, and Standards of Living.—According to the census of 1925, the district then had a population of
22,002,914, which is remarkably homogeneous since, with the exception of about 5,000 foreigners, chiefly concentrated in the city
of Tokyo, all the people are of the Japanese race. Japanese is the universal language, but English has become the language of
foreign intercourse, and is taught in all the higher schools. The people are very progressive, and during recent years have
adopted occidental customs and the use of foreign-style goods to an ever increasing extent. However, the standard of living is
still much below that of America, the per capita wealth in Japan being estimated at about $862, at par of exchange, or approxi-
mately one quarter of that in the United States.
Leading Occupations and Industriea.—The principal occupations and industries of the district are: agriculture, in which, in
conjunction with its allied secondary occupations, such as sericulture, about half the population is employed; manufacturing,
which includes factory industries producing iron and steel goods, electrical materials, woolen and cotton fabrics, chemicals, paper,
leather, and food products, and also household industries which produce many of the most characteristic manufactures of Japan,
such as special kinds of silk and cotton fabrics, Japanese paper, lacquered ware, hemp, braid, toys and wooden ware; fishing.
Hokkaido being one of the world's famous fishing grounds; and mining, especially coal and copper.
Principal Imports and Exports.—The foreign trade of Japan in 1929 totalled $2,016,565,000, an increase of $78,319,100 over
1928. Imports exceeded exports by $31,241,000, comparing with $104, 327,010 in 1928. Of the total, the share of the United
States amounted to 36 percent or $729,193,000, the United States supplying 29½ percent or $304,136,000 worth of Japan's imports
and taking 421½ percent or $425,057,000 worth of Japan's exports comparing with 2812 percent and 42 percent respectively in 1928.
Imports from the United States consisted of: raw cotton, $128,506,000; lumber, $31.302,000; machinery and parts, $17,619;000;
iron (other than pig-iron, $594,000), $17,275,000; automobiles and parts, $14,437,000; dynamos and transformers, kerosene, gasoline,
wheat, rice, hides and skins, leather, aluminium, lead, copper, zinc, and numerous other commodities in lesser quantities. Leading
exports from Japan to the United States comprised: raw silk, $351,251,000; silk tissues, $6,837,000; chinaware, $6,743,000; canned
foods, $6,707,000; tea $3,778,000; hats and caps, plaits for hat-making, toys, brushes, buttons, marine products, vegetable oils,
camphor, menthol crystals, and agar-agar.
General Customs Policy and Regulations.—The Japanese import tariff is a combination of specific and ad valorem duties,
designed to foster domestic production insofar as feasible. Most raw materials needed in manufacturing, such as raw cotton,
wool, some varieties of lumber, ores, and the like, are on the free list. The United States in common with many other nations,
enjoys the benefits of conventional tariff agreements with Japan which concede under certain conditions lower duties on a
limited number of articles than are prescribed in the tariff law.
Postal Regulations and Ratea—Japan is a member of the Universal Postal Union, and has a Parcel Post Agreement with the
United States. Letter postage to Japan is five cents for the first ounce and three cents for each additional ounce. Periodicals
pay one cent for each two ounces. Commercial papers pay five cents for the first ten ounces and one cent for each additional
two ounces. Samples pay two cents for the first four ounces and one cent for each additional two ounces. Parcel post packages
are subject to an additional charge of ten sen (five cents) for customs examination.
Transportation Conditions and Connectiona.—Several American steamship lines maintain rapid and regular services between
Japanese ports and both the Atlantic and Pacific ports of the United States. Yokohama is the port through which pass most of
the exports and imports of the Tokyo District. Transportation between Yokohama and Tokyo, a distance of 18 miles, is effected
by lighters towed up the bay, by rail, and by truck. In Tokyo, the political and financial capital of the country, are located the
head offices of many of the principal industrial and commercial firms of Japan, and a network of railways and numerous steamship
lines via Yokohama connect the city with all points of the Empire.
Character of Packing Desirabla—Merchandise for Japan should be packed to withstand handling, possibly lighterage, and
torage in a climate sometimes exceptionally humid.
Usual Terms of Credit.—American exporters usually find it more satisfactory to quote c.i.f. Yokohama rather than f.o.b. at an
American city or port. It is usual for the foreign exporters to require an advance of perhaps 25 per cent with order, irrevocable
letter of credit placed with the exporter's bank, and full payment against shipping documents. Japanese purchasers usually
plan to secure 90 days credit, but this credit is ordinarily granted by the bank in Japan which finances the transaction. There
are various banks in Japan with connections in the United States through which business may be transacted, among which
may be mentioned the National City Bank of New York, with branches in Tokyo, Yokohama, Kobe and Osaka.

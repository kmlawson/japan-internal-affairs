Article 1. In this ordinance the following drugs shall be termed narcotics:

Clause 1. Morphine, diacetyl morphine, cocaine, and their salts.

Clause 2. Crude cocaine, and ecgonine.

Clause 3. All manufactured drugs containing 0.2% or more morphine or 0.1% cocaine, whether or not fixed by pharmacopoeia (including so-called anti-opium remedies.

Clause 4. All manufactured drugs containing diacetylmorphine.

Clause 5. Galenical preparations (extract and tincture) of Indian Hemp.

Clause 6. Other drugs designated by the Governor General as narcotics.

Article 2. In this ordinance the profession of medicine includes physicians, dentists, and veterinary surgeons. The profession of drugs covers druggists, prescriptionists, and manufacturing chemists (including patent medicine manufacturers). "For use as doctor's medicine" is understood to be when doctors use for diagnosis or when used according to a doctor's prescription. Manufactured drugs for the above-mentioned medical profession and when they are used for compounding medicine shall be considered as for medical use.

Article 3. Narcotics cannot be used except by the medical profession or for scientific purposes.

Article 4. Narcotics cannot be manufactured, imported from foreign countries, imported from Japan

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that the Taiwan (Formosa) Government-General is said to be contemplating increasing the number of permits issued to opium smokers in that island.

In February 1896, the Taiwan Government issued restrictive regulations known as the Taiwan Opium Ordinance whereby only confirmed addicts were allowed permits for using opium, after undergoing strict examination. It was confidently expected that these regulations would result in the gradual elimination of opium addiction, as the government expected to be able to control the importation and consequent indulgence in the drug. In September

1900

=== Page 24 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

1900, the number of licensed opium smokers reached 169,064, which figure might be regarded as something near the actual state of opium addiction among the natives at that time. During the years 1904 and 1905, 30,000 additional licenses were issued, and in 1908 there was another addition of 15,849 licensed smokers.

Since then there have been very few licenses issued, and at the end of 1928, the total number of registered opium smokers was reduced to about 27,000, according to official reports. It was recognized, however, that although the official reports showed a steady and gratifying decrease in the number of registered addicts, these records did not take into account the unlicensed and illicit smokers, whose number was feared to be on the increase.

These unregistered addicts constituted a problem that the Taiwan Government-General could choose either to ignore and to refuse to recognize, or else to deal with by extending official cognizance, and placing the addicts under official supervision by issuing further permits to them.

On December 17, 1929, the Government-General of Formosa, apparently without the sanction of the Imperial Government, began to accept applications for new licenses to use opium, apparently deciding that an honest effort to determine the actual users of opium and to place them under official supervision was preferable to evading the issue. There were more than 30,000 applicants for the new licenses by the end of December 1929, but when 10,000 new licenses were ready for issuance, the Imperial Government stepped in and called a halt. (According to some reports 10,000 licenses had already been issued when the Imperial Government interfered.)

In the meantime a wave of opposition to the new measure had been growing. Considerable attention was given to it by the newspapers of Tokyo, the Tokyo ASAHI condemning it as a reactionary move. Several organizations of native Formosans presented petitions to the Taiwan government to rescind the order or to obtain further authority from the Imperial Government, criticising the new measure as one calculated to encourage opium consumption merely for the purpose of raising further revenue for the local government, which they condemned for laxity in enforcing the existing regulations, declaring that they wanted total abolition in place of the futile method popularly called "gradual abolishment". Some of this criticism may, no doubt, be attributed to the perennial discontent of agitators against Japanese rule, but it is rather an edifying spectacle to see Formosan natives of Chinese extraction criticising the local Japanese government for non-enforcement of prohibitory legislation!

According to an article in the Tokyo ASAHI, February 5, 1930, the question of Formosan opium regulation was brought up at a meeting of the Cabinet in Tokyo on February 4th by Mr. Matsuda, Minister of Overseas Affairs. The Government decided to summon to Tokyo Mr. Ishizuka, Governor General of Formosa, who was wintering at a hot spring resort near Tokyo, and to issue rigid instructions, the details of which are said to have been as follows, to him regarding the regulation of opium in Taiwan:

That the Government General of Formosa which has, since December 17, 1929, been receiving applications for permits to use opiates, shall postpone indefinitely the period for granting such permits in view of the fact that the date for such permission is not yet officially fixed.

2)

2) That Mr. Munai, Chief of the Second Section of the Bureau of Productive Enterprises of the Department of Overseas Affairs, who was leaving Tokyo on February 16th for Formosa on a mission to welcome the League of Nations commission of three investigators scheduled to arrive in Formosa on February 19th, should, in cooperation with Mr. Inoue, a specialist of the Department of Foreign Affairs and who also was under instruction to proceed to Formosa, investigate the actual condition of opium using in Formosa and report thereon.

3) That, if it is deemed absolutely necessary as a result of the investigations to issue further permits to opium addicts, such extensions of permits be done under rigid supervision.

4) That the proceeds from the issuance of such permits amounting to about Yen 700,000, be applied solely to the treatment and prevention of opium addiction, with a view toward complete abolition.

It is interesting to speculate on what influence the arrival of the League of Nations commission in Taiwan had upon the Japanese Government in causing it to take a hand in the internal affairs of the colony. Recent criticism by Sir Malcolm Delevigne and Sir John Campbell in the Opium Committee at Geneva of Japan's complacent attitude toward the export of narcotics to China and India seems to have made the Japanese Government somewhat sensitive toward further criticism of her control over narcotics

narcotics. Obviously, a move to increase the number of registered addicts - a move which has no precedent for nearly twenty years - would be interpreted as a confession, on the part of the authorities, of failure to curb drug addiction in Taiwan, and would consequently strengthen the contention of those critics who have attacked Japan's control of production and exportation of opiates. Such a confession would be particularly mortifying inasmuch as Japan has rather plumbed herself on her handling of the opium problem in Formosa. The ASAII voices this sensitiveness in stating "In view of the fact that the rigid measures adopted by the Home Government have delicate relations with the administrative policies of the Formosan Government and with international matters, the development of the situation is being watched with keen interest."

I learn from members of the British Embassy that the British Consul at Tamsui reported that, before the arrival of the Opium Commission, there was a considerable flurry within the Taiwan Opium Monopoly but that he had not been able to discover what was being done.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that the matter of the control of opium addiction in Formosa continues to occupy much attention both in official circles and in the vernacular press. It is obvious that the Japanese Government, which for the past few years has given the impression that the problem of narcotic addiction in Formosa is one well on the road to solution under the operation of existing regulations, has been stirred by reports

=== Page 30 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

reports of wide-spread addiction and evasion of measures designed to control the importation of opium. A publication issued in February 1930 entitled "The Opium System in Formosa" by the Formosa Government General admits that "the number of opium addicts who had contracted the harmful habit through secret smoking has never been on the lane".

As reported in my despatch No. 61 of March 11, 1930, the Formosa Government had recognized the growth of illicit smoking and in 1929 had made a start toward effective control by taking a census of addicted persons with the object of issuing further permits, when the matter was taken up by the Central Government. In my despatch No. 45 of February 24, 1930, I reported the revised ordinance of the Taiwan Government regulating narcotics. In March the Government issued a new ordinance regulating the transportation of opium on Japanese ships as reported in my despatch No. 72 of March 22, 1930. The Japanese Government is now considering ways and means of further regulating traffic and addiction of opium. A number of conferences between officials of the various departments of the Government concerned have been held. Thus far no public announcements have been made, but the press reports that at a joint meeting of officials of the departments concerned, a statement of policy with regard to opium in Formosa was drawn up for the approval of the Cabinet as follows later. It is interesting to note that this statement is practically identical with a memorandum handed by the Governor General of Formosa to the visiting delegates of the Opium Committee of the League of Nations several weeks previously, on February 28th, which memorandum was obtained by the Embassy from the Vice Minister of Overseas Affairs with the statement that it was "confidential".

as to their residence and bona fides, and that
punishments be imposed for frauds.

It is further reported in the press that the Department
of Overseas Affairs has drawn up, as the result of investigations
of the conditions of opium addiction in Formosa, the
following program:

1) That official permission shall be given to about
5,000 addicts out of a total of 25,000 applicants
for opium permits.

2) That upward of 20,000 persons who are addicted to a lesser degree and who are included in the total number of applicants in the foregoing item shall be given medical treatment and cured under a three year program after the 1930-1931 fiscal year.

3) That the sum of Yen 400,000 shall be included in a supplementary budget for the 1930-1931 fiscal year as expenditure for medical treatment and that with this sum the Saisei-in Hospital at Taihoku shall be enlarged, while new relief stations shall be inaugurated in eleven public hospitals in Taihoku, Taihoku, Tainan and other districts of Formosa, where those suffering from opium addiction may be admitted for treatment.

4) That seven watch stations shall be established on the western coast and other points of Formosa to prevent the smuggling of opium; that two officials be newly appointed for the control of opium using; that about 100 special police be newly appointed for the supervision of those who are suffering from addiction and who are not taken to relief stations for treatment and also for the control of illicit use of opium; and that the sum of Yen 100,000 shall be included in a supplementary budget for the 1930-1931 fiscal year to finance the above project.

5) That an opium committee shall be established in the Government General of Formosa for the control of opium matters.

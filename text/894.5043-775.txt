The attached report on a recent meeting of Japan Seamen's Union, from McClintock at Kobe, is a very good report, but I think it would suffice if you read the following summary:

The importance of the meeting lies in the fact that the Union adopted a resolution to the effect that,

"We declare that the rapid advance of Japanese trade abroad is due to low wages and long hours of work, the sacrifice of the workers for the sake of industrial progress of the country."

The Union is the most important labor union in Japan, having a membership equivalent to one-fourth of all the labor union membership of the country.

The Union itself and its membership are imbued more strongly with western conceptions of the rights of labor than are industrial workers. This difference is due primarily to the fact that the

paternalistic

paternalistic relations which existed between employer and employee in industrial enterprises in feudal times still, to a large measure, govern the rights and obligations of employer and employee toward each other. This is not the case, however, with the seamen. The Japanese merchant marine came into existence only after the downfall of the Tokugawa Shogunate in 1868, during whose administration of three centuries the construction of ocean-going vessels was forbidden. Those employed in the merchant marine have started with a fresh slate and have endeavored to improve their conditions of employment along western lines, that is, by group bargaining rather than along paternalistic lines.

I suggest that you glance through the list of the objectives of the Union, as set forth on pages 9 and 10.

The deliberations of the thirteenth annual meeting of the Japan Seamen's Union (Nihon Kaiin Kumiai) are of interest not only because this organization is the largest and most highly integrated labor union in Japan, but also because they throw light upon certain significant trends affecting organized labor in Japan at the present time. At a time when official delegations from Japan are attesting that labor conditions here are of the best, it is somewhat startling to read the resolution of 98,000 men to the effect that "We declare that the rapid advance of Japanese trade abroad is due to low wages and long hours of work, the sacrifice of the workers for the sake of the industrial progress of the country." There is perhaps no other union in Japan which would have dared to make so blunt a denial of the thesis of the Japanese Government. It becomes interesting, therefore, to ascertain what manner of organization is the Nihon Kaiin Kumiai.

The Japan Seamen's Union comprises in its membership practically one fourth of all union members in the Empire. In the middle of 1932 there were 906 labor unions in Japan of a total membership of 376,000 (1), while in the same year the Seamen's Union had 96,000 members. Today the Union has 98,612, an increase of 17,000 men in the past six years. During 1933 the membership increased by 2,445, at a time when the number of union members for all of Japan was diminishing. In the first six months of 1933 membership for all unions declined by 9,218 (2). When this fact is

is made clear the steady increase in numbers of the Seamen's Union becomes the more remarkable.

About 44% of the 225,000 qualified mariners of Japan are enrolled in the Seamen's Union; the highest percentage of organized workers in any industry in Japan. It is also significant to observe that 42.8% of all union members in Japan, as of July, 1932, were connected with seafaring or shipbuilding. Whereas in industry unionism has made but imperceptible progress, in marine transport and the phases thereof it has made its greatest advance. One reason for this, in the opinion of the writer, may be that the artisans of Japan have behind them centuries of experience of the feudal system and the craft guild, in which the master worker stood in a patriarchal relation to his men. There never was a Japanese merchant marine really worthy of the name until the opening of the Meiji era, while during the three centuries of the Tokugawa Shogunate all international traffic by sea was forbidden to Japanese ships under pain of death for the crews manning them. The result was that the seamen of Japan did not have behind them the tradition of feudal patriarchy, although they had ingrained the instinct of all Japanese to organize for the common good. Therefore, when the Japanese merchant marine at last developed and Japanese seamen came to observe the conditions affecting their fellows under other flags, they organized on modern lines as a labor union.

The Japan Seamen's Union, the Officers' Association and the Dockers' Unions belong to the right center of Japanese organized labor and are among the principal members of the Japan Labor Union Conference, which occupies middle ground between the extreme right and left wings of labor opinion. **Functions of the Japan Seamen's Union.**

Having located the position of the Japan Seamen's Union

Union with reference to other unions in the Empire and seen by comparison the front rank position it occupies in organized labor, it is pertinent to examine the functions of the Union and the ends to which its very considerable strength is used.

The Marine Union Association.
---
One of the most important functions of the Seamen's Union is in connection with the Kaiji Kyodo Kai or Marine Union Association, composed of the Union, the Officers' Association and the Japan Shipowners' Association. The Kaiji Kyodo Kai is concerned with the prevention and arbitration of disputes arising between shipowners and officers and crews, with the study of problems affecting conditions of marine employment, the conduct of a seamen's employment exchange and the maintenance of seamen's homes and institutes. It has been highly effective in maintaining standard rates of wages in the merchant marine.
(4)

Disputes.
---
There were no strikes in 1933 on the part of the Seamen's Union. Disputes involving the Union numbered 245, an increase of 35 cases as compared with 1932. The Union ascribed the augmented number of disputes to the increased activity of the Japanese merchant marine since the second half of 1932 and to the asserted fact that the Japan Shipowners' Association had been lax in controlling its own members with reference to maintaining proper conditions of maritime labor.

## Seamen's Unemployment Relief.

Since April, 1930 the Japan Seamen's Union has spent almost half a million yen in providing work for unemployed seamen. Aided by a Government subsidy of ¥552,708, the Union disbursed ¥1,055,402 during the period, providing a total of 887,431 days of work to unemployed seamen. (2)

The unemployment relief work of the Union was originally intended to be evolved as unemployment insurance, with the result that the plan of providing work was abandoned in March, 1934 and a program of unemployment insurance was inaugurated on April 1. The present scheme will be financed by the Union and the Government on the basis of two yen from the public funds to one advanced by the Union. In 1934 the Union proposes to spend ¥60,000 for the insurance fund, which, with a contribution of ¥120,000 from the Government, will make a total of ¥180,000 for the relief of unemployed mariners. (2)

The system of unemployment insurance thus calls for no payments by the immediate beneficiaries, other than such contributions as the recipients of relief may have at one time made to the Union. Seamen who have been on the beach for three months may thereafter draw a benefit of ¥12 per month up to a maximum of three months: in other words, for the first quarter of the year without work they receive nothing, but for the second quarter of unemployment they are given ¥36. Should they remain without employment still longer, they may draw a benefit of ¥9 a month for a further period

The Seamen's Union and the Japanese Merchant Marine.

The Japan Seamen's Union has been active in supporting measures for the benefit of the merchant marine and in most cases its aims have been identical with those of the steamship operators, although for different reasons of self-interest. Of such a category was the agitation in 1933 on the part of the Union against an increase in the price of steam coal which was threatened by the Japanese coal trust. Another instance of cooperation with the shipowners has been the work of the Union in favor of the Ship Improvement Law of October, 1932, providing for the scrapping of 400,000 gross tons of obsolete ships and the subsidized construction of 200,000 gross tons of modern vessels. In this respect the Union was interested on two counts. In the first place, the statistics on disaster to Japanese merchant ships reveal a striking incidence of accident in vessels of more than 25 years of age. Secondly, the Ship Improvement Law resulted in the building of new vessels in which the accommodation for the crews was as excellent as in any merchantmen of any other country. The law thus protected Japanese seamen by reducing

**Relations With Other Organized Labor.**

As the principal union in Japan, the Seamen's
Union has a powerful voice in the deliberations of
all organized labor in the Empire. It seems generally
able to secure representation on the various Japanese
delegations to the International Labor Conferences
at Geneva, as well as to other labor gatherings of an
international character. The Union claims that its
delegate to the Joint Maritime Commission meeting
held at Paris in December, 1933 was largely instru-
mental in securing action by the Commission to urge
the International Labor Office at Genova to hold a

Finances for 1933.

The report of the Treasurer of the Japan Seamen's Union for 1933 revealed that the organization was in a very good financial condition. Total income for the year was ¥431,328, an increase of approximately ¥55,000 over that for 1932. Total expenditures were ¥399,168, or about ¥60,000 less than during the preceding year. Reserves amounted to ¥232,917, not counting a special mutual relief fund of ¥76,082. (2) The Seamen's Union is the richest of any labor organization in Japan.

Platform.

The Thirteenth Annual Meeting of the Japan Seamen's Union ended by the adoption of a many-planked platform. Under the general topic of improvement of conditions for seamen may be listed the following objects for which the Union would work:

1. The establishment of an eight hour day at sea.
2. Creation of a system whereby wages should be increased every six months until the "rise in the standard of living of seamen" has been accommodated.

"The Union aims at the improvement of labour conditions and the advancement of the social position of seamen, and admits that with this object in view the Union will sometimes oppose employers and at other times co-operate with them. This is the reason why it considers collective agreements a powerful means of labour movement and industrial cooperation an effective emergency measure. Sincere efforts of working classes, however, have not been rewarded with success. In their efforts to prolong their life capitalists have used the time of emergency as an excuse to mislead the nation. They are endeavouring on the one hand to create an economic block uniting Japan and Manchuria and on the other hand to promote the advance of Japanese trade abroad taking advantage of low wages and long hours of work. The Saito Cabinet has been in existence for over two years, during which period the Diet has been in session several times. Yet no labour legislation or social political measures have been submitted. This shows how utterly the Government has neglected working classes, the very moving forces of the industrial progress of the country. Moreover the Government and employers are defending their position that Japanese labour conditions are not inferior and are demanding workers to join them in this on the ground that the nation should be united against an attack from abroad. We declare that the rapid advance of Japanese trade abroad is due to low wages and long hours of work, the sacrifice of workers for the sake of the industrial progress of the country. The Government and employers should be aware of this fact and change their selfish attitude. We have already sacrificed enough for the sake of the promotion of industrial co-operation. The time has come to put forward our humble demand -- the enactment of a trade union law, establishment of an eight hour day on board ship, restoration or acquisition of bonuses or allowances, establishment of a minimum manning scale and the realisation of other items on the agenda adopted at this meeting.

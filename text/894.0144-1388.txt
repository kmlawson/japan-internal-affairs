THE KURILE ISLANDS
(Chishima)

Postwar territorial adjustments in the Far East will involve the question of the possible detachment of the Kurile Islands from the Japanese Empire. The islands are of considerable strategic importance and of some commercial value to Japan.

I. AREA AND POPULATION

The Kuriles are a chain of 47 sparsely inhabited, rugged, volcanic islands extending for about 690 miles in a northeasterly direction between Hokkaido and Kamchatka. With an aggregate coastline of approximately 1,500 miles in length, they contain an area of 3,944 square miles. They have a population of 17,550, all Japanese. Approximately 90 percent of the inhabitants are located on the three southernmost islands of Kunashiri, Shikotan and Etorofu.

II. HISTORY OF JAPANESE ACQUISITION

Japanese expeditions visited the southernmost islands in 1798 and 1801 claiming them as Japanese territory. During the nineteenth century the Russians, having occupied Kamchatka, entered the northern islands in search of furbearing animals. As they worked southward they infringed upon Japanese claims.

In 1875 Japanese title to the Kuriles was cleared in a convention by which Russia agreed to withdraw entirely from the Kuriles in return for Japanese withdrawal from southern Sakhalin. Russian settlers on the islands subsequently returned to Kamchatka.

### III. POLITICAL ADMINISTRATION

The Kuriles are considered to be a part of Japan proper. For administrative purposes they are included with the northern portion of Hokkaido to form the Nemuro branch of the Hokkaido administration, with its central office at Nemuro, in Hokkaido. Organized local government exists in the three southern islands only, where it is similar to the administrative arrangements in other rural regions of Japan. Agents of the Department of Agriculture and Forestry are stationed on the principal islands for the enforcement of restrictions upon seal hunting.

In 1936 there were 15 elementary schools and five higher elementary schools, with a total of 40 teachers, all located in the three southern islands.

### IV. RESOURCES AND INDUSTRY

There are extensive conifer forests on the southern islands and sufficient timber is cut for local needs. Sulfur deposits are worked also on the southern islands. Gold and silver deposits mined by the Russians are probably exhausted. A survey in 1938 indicated the presence of iron ore in paving quantities on Kunashiri, the southernmost island; at most, production is insignificant.

Fishing is by far the principal, and in many of the islands the only, industry; the value of the 1938 catch was estimated at 32 million yen. About 60 percent of the catch is canned; most of the balance is iced or otherwise processed for shipment to Japan or abroad. Over 30 canning and processing plants are located on the islands. The fishing industry attracts 20,000 to 30,000 outside laborers in the summer months. In recent years the Kurile grounds, especially those in the northern region, have been more thoroughly fished by the Japanese as a result of increasing restrictions on Japanese fishing in Russian waters around Kamchatka. quantities of edible sea weed are collected for shipment. The Japanese government maintains four fish hatcheries in the islands. Some whaling, with a value in 1936 of 890,000 yen, is carried on around the southern islands.

Fur trapping is no longer of great importance, although the Department of Agriculture and Forestry is known to maintain fox farms on the islands. Japan renounced the treaty on October 23, 1940, and it lapsed a year later.

Agriculture is restricted to some small-scale truck farming and cattle raising in the southern islands for local consumption.

V. COMMUNICATIONS

There are no railroads, commercial aviation, or municipal utilities, and only a few primitive roads. On the southern islands of Kunashiri and Etorofu, however, most of the coastal settlements are connected by telephone, telegraph and postal service. A submarine cable connects these islands with Hokkaido. The fishing bases and fleets are equipped with radio communication and several radio stations are operated by the government. Steamship service between the principal settlements is maintained from spring until autumn.

VI. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

The Kuriles are of considerable strategic importance both because of their location between Japan proper and Kamchatka, which makes them part of the land bridge between Asia and America and because of their proximity to the Aleutians, and their position on the great circle route in the northern Pacific. Along with the main islands of Japan, they screen the maritime provinces of Siberia from the open Pacific. Although there are few good harbors, and meteorological conditions render aerial and surface navigation difficult, the islands, as bases for land forces, seaplanes, and submarines, extend considerably the range of Japanese action to the north and east and conversely strengthen the protective screen of northern Japan.

The Japanese are known to have developed minor naval bases and anchorages in the Kuriles. Bases, provided with piers, water tanks, fuel storage and so forth, are located at Shikotan and Etorofu in the south, at Shimushiri in the central section, and at Paramushiro (three bases) and Shimushu in the north. There are also anchorages at Shikotan, Etorofu and Paramushiro. An airfield is believed to have been developed at Shimushu, possibly with underground hangars and fuel storage, and there are possibly two additional fields on the nearby island of Paramushiro. Undeveloped surface and seaplane anchorages are found throughout the archipelago. Although there are numerous areas on the three southern islands and on Paramushiro suitable for land plane bases, construction of such bases would require much grading and drainage. Information concerning fortifications in the Kuriles is lacking but it is probable that the Japanese have fortified several of the harbors and other strategic locations.

VII. POSTWAR DISPOSITION

A. General Considerations

Postwar adjustment of Japanese territory will involve consideration of the possible detachment of the Kurile Islands from the Empire.

Their final disposition may well be affected significantly by the future course of Russo-Japanese relations. According to a provision of the non-aggression pact of April 1941, the two parties agreed to "respect each other's territorial integrity and to uphold the territories of the other". In the event of war between Russia and Japan, however, Russia might be expected to claim some or all of the islands. On the other hand, an offensive by the United States against Japan from the north might result in the occupation of the strategically important islands by American forces.

Japan has a valid historical claim to the islands which was recognized by Russia in the convention of 1876. Their population is entirely Japanese. Although the islands are not vital to the Empire, they are of some

commercial importance, particularly for their fishing grounds. For reasons of security, however, permanent detachment of the islands from Japan may be contemplated.

B. Alternative Solutions

1. Transfer to Russia.--If the Kurile Islands should be transferred to Soviet possession, Russia would then dominate the Sea of Okhotsk and the northern approaches to Japan. This new situation would increase the security of Siberia against the threat of Japanese attack, but would inevitably arouse among the Japanese a feeling of insecurity which would be a further cause of resentment and friction between the two countries. The loss of valuable fishing grounds would also present serious difficulties to the Japanese, although provision might be made for treaty recognition of Japanese fishing privileges. The total resident population being only 17,550, repatriation of Japanese nationals would not be a serious matter.

2. International Administration.--General security might be most effectively promoted by placing the Kuriles under the jurisdiction of an international body. The administration might be set up in accordance with the arrangements to be made for other strategically important islands in the Pacific. Since the principal purpose of international administration would be to prevent Japan from employing the islands for military use, such administration might be limited to supervision and inspection, and it might be possible to utilize Japanese personnel for normal administrative purposes. Provision might be made also for the continuation of Japanese fishing and other commercial enterprise in the islands.

SOUTHERN SAKHALIN:
JAPANESE KARAFUTO*

The postwar settlement with Japan will presumably involve the question of the future status of Japanese Karafuto, or Southern Sakhalin. This territory has been integrated into the Japanese Empire administratively and economically. Considerations of international or regional security may, however, necessitate its transfer to Russia or the establishment of certain restrictions upon the exercise of Japanese sovereignty.

I. DESCRIPTIVE DATA

The Japanese territory of Karafuto (Sakhalin) consists of the portion of the island of the same name south of the 50th parallel, and contains an area of 13,900 square miles. It is located in the Sea of Okhotsk, north of the island of Hokkaido and roughly parallel to the coast of Maritime Siberia.

According to the census of October 1, 1940, Japanese Karafuto had a total population of 414,891, of which 99.41 percent were Japanese.** There are nine cities with a population exceeding 10,000; two of these, Toyohara and Shirutoru, have between 30,000 and 40,000 inhabitants.

The Japanese claim to the entire island was not secure, for a Russo-Japanese convention of 1867 included a clause suggesting the exchange of the southern part of Sakhalin by Japan for full title to the Kuriles, a title which rested on a strong basis of exploration and inhabitation. A few years later Japan offered to purchase Sakhalin. Finally, in a convention of 1875 Japan agreed to withdraw altogether from Sakhalin in return for Russian withdrawal from the Kuriles.

2. Japanese Acquisition of Southern Sakhalin

At the Portsmouth (N. H.) conference of 1905 Japan demanded the cession of the entire island of Sakhalin, a proposal which was at first rejected flatly by the Russian government. Subsequently Japan accepted the southern part of the island up to the fiftieth parallel in lieu of an indemnity, and the cession was accordingly embodied in the Treaty of Portsmouth (September 5, 1905).

III. POLITICAL ADMINISTRATION

1. Government and Law

The administration of Karafuto was formerly under the direct control of the Japanese Minister of Overseas Affairs. The powers of the governor, which extend over mining, forestry, taxation, railways and postal service, are wider than those of a governor in the prefectures of Japan proper. For administrative purposes the Karafuto government, which is located at Toyohara, is divided into four main offices: Secretariat, Interior Bureau, Forestry Bureau, and Police Bureau.

The Japan Year Book (1939-40) states that more home laws of Japan were applied in Karafuto than in other Japanese dependencies. Japanese laws concerning the judicial system, such as civil law, criminal law, the laws of civil and criminal procedures, and the law of the constitution of the courts of justice, were applied fully in Karafuto.

2. Fiscal Data

Revenue in 1937 amounted to 57,004,000 yen, of which 35,000,000 yen were derived from taxes, the balance from various government undertakings. Revenues, which increased steadily after Japanese acquisition of the territory, doubled between 1931 and 1937.

Expenditures in 1937 for administration, education, public works, and so forth, amounted to 35,805,000 yen in 1937, an increase of 1.5 times over 1931.

3. Education

In 1937 there were in Karafuto 253 elementary schools, three middle schools, six high schools and one colonization school, with a total enrollment of approximately 60,000.

IV. ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

1. Minerals and Forests

In 1937 production of coal amounted to 2.5 million metric tons. Although the Japanese have made serious attempts to secure petroleum from Karafuto, it appears that none is produced in the southern portion of the island. Some gold, copper and iron are found.

Karafuto is thickly covered with primeval forests, estimated to total 2.9 million hectares, of which 833,000 hectares are reserved for future growth and 79,000 for experimental work by several of the imperial universities. Timber production in 1937 amounted to 212,000 cubic meters.

## 2. Industry

Industrial development includes nine pulp and paper factories, with a production in 1937 of 216,000 metric tons of pulp and 201,000 metric tons of paper; one rayon plant; numerous fishing and canning enterprises, with an output valued between 10 and 20 million yen per year; over forty breweries; three hydro-electric plants; and several starch and butter factories. Total industrial output in 1937 was valued at 187 million yen.

## 3. Agriculture

In 1937 agricultural production on 10,811 farms amounted to 5 million yen. Only 34,888 hectares of an estimated 331,317 hectares of arable land are under cultivation. Farm output includes grains, vegetables, potatoes and animal products.

## 4. Railways and Communications

The state railway which totals 343 kilometers, consists of the main trunk line between Otomari and Motodamari on the east coast, the west coast line between Honto and Tomarioru, and the trans-island connecting link. There are also three local railways. Karafuto is connected with Hokkaido by government-operated ferry service and by cable. A government radio station is maintained at Toyohara.

## 5. Trade

Overseas trade is carried on almost exclusively with Japan proper. Karafuto ships to the home country pulp, paper, marine fertilizer, fish products and edible sea weed. Imports include rice, cotton goods, oils, beer, sake, vegetables, fruits, salt, tobacco and mineral products. Two ports, Otomari and Maoka, are open to foreign shipping. V. JAPANESE CONCESSIONS IN NORTHERN (RUSSIAN) SAKHALIN

1. Acquisition of Rights

Following the Nikolaevsk massacre of February 1920, an incident of the Siberian intervention, Japanese troops occupied northern Sakhalin and remained in occupation after Japan withdrew from Siberia in 1922. In January 1925 Japan and Russia signed a convention at Peking by which, among other matters treated, Japan agreed to withdraw from northern Sakhalin by May 15 of that year, at which time the area would be restored to Russia in full sovereignty. In an attached protocol Russia agreed, on a royalty basis, to grant to Japanese concerns recommended by their government concessions to exploit 50 percent in area of each of certain specified oil fields, and the right to bid on an equal basis with other foreigners for the remaining concessions. Japanese concerns also were granted the right to prospect for oil in an area of 43 square miles along the east coast, and to exploit any findings on the same 50 percent basis. The Japanese were also permitted to continue the operation of certain coal mines which they had utilized during the military occupation. By subsequent agreement the Japanese concessions were fixed at a 45 year term, expiring in 1970, and the exploration rights at ten years. The latter were extended in 1936 to December 1941.

2. Increasing Soviet Interest in Northern Sakhalin

Between 1926 and 1936 the Japanese managed to obtain the bulk of the oil output of northern Sakhalin by means of their own production and by purchasing the output of Soviet wells. By 1936, however, the situation was changing rapidly, because of the growing industrialization of the Siberian Far East. As Russian oil requirements in the area increased, production was expanded. In 1928 the Soviet Sakhaneft Trust began operations in Sakhalin and by 1934 its production had increased from 26,000 metric tons

VI. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Karafuto's location near to the Siberian mainland gives it considerable strategic importance, both offensively and defensively. Prior to the outbreak of the Pacific war, the Japanese, according to available information, had developed minor military and naval installations in the territory. Civil airfields at Nairo, Ochiai, Toyhara and Otomari were also used by the army and navy. Naval bases, from which operated patrol vessels of the High North Fleet, were located at Nishi, Notoro, Otomari, Maoka and Nairo. Coast artillery was mounted at Nishi, Notoro, on the southern tip of the island, covering La Pérouse Strait, through which vessels pass between the North Pacific and Vladivostok and other ports on the Sea of Japan.

VII. POSTWAR DISPOSITION OF KARAFUTO

1. General Considerations

Postwar adjustment of Japanese territory will involve consideration of the possible detachment of Japanese Karafuto from the Empire. Russia, possessor of this area prior to 1905, is the most natural nation in a position to acquire the territory.

The final disposition of Karafuto will depend in large measure on the future course of Russo-Japanese relations. According to a provision of the non-aggression pact of April 1941, the two parties agreed to "respect each other's territorial integrity and to uphold the territories of the other". In the event of war between Russia and Japan, however, Russia would presumably claim the recovery of the territory.

possible area which might well be left to Japan, since it
presents possibilities of further, although limited,
development. For Russia, which possesses vast undeveloped
areas in Eastern Siberia, Karafuto would be of little
added material value.

Security factors may be of greater significance, as
Karafuto forms a link in the chain of Japanese territory
which commands the sea and air approaches to the Maritime
Province of Russia.

2. Alternative Solutions

a. Transfer to Russia.--This solution would be
justified for reasons of security and on grounds of prior
possession. In the hands of Russia, southern Karafuto
would provide an advanced offensive-defensive base against
Japan. In the hands of a weakened Japan, it would be no
more than a defensive outpost. Nor does it add significantly
to Japan's war potential. If, however, Russia should
enter the war against Japan and should suffer serious losses,
some of them inflicted from Karafuto, it would probably
make a strong bid for possession of the territory. Its
transfer to Russia, coupled with the disarmament of Japan
which will follow the war, would inevitably produce a deep
sense of insecurity in Japan, and thus would be a cause of
resentment and friction between Japan and Russia.

b. Unconditional Retention by Japan.--This solution
would be based upon the recognition of the administrative
and economic integration of the territory into the
Japanese Empire since 1905.

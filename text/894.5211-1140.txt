Sir:

I have the honor to report that the British Ambassador is initiating a negotiation to wind up perpetual lease holdings by British subjects in Japan. He told me that he had approached the Japanese authorities and the leaseholders with a proposal which was sympathetically received on both sides. In outline the proposal is that the leaseholders calculate the amount of annual taxation to which they would be liable if their property
was

=== Page 3 === (confidence 1.00)
- 2 -

was held in fee simple. The difference between that sum and the amount which is paid as perpetual lease ground rent would be capitalized on a twenty-year or other basis to be agreed upon, for which the Japanese Government would give them five per cent. bonds. The property owners would then be given title deeds in fee simple, and pay the same taxes as Japanese subjects.

In speaking of this project to me, the British Ambassador stated that he realized the difference in the attitude of the United States and British Governments, and while he did not wish to discuss the legal aspects of the case, he would, he said, like to have my views. I told him that I was not in a position to comment but that I should be glad to inform the Department. Since this conversation I have been approached by an American who asked whether there would be any objection to the Americans joining in with the British in this matter. I stated that I was in no position to speak for the Department but was reporting the matter to Washington.

The British have organized a leaseholders' committee which is compiling the data necessary for presenting a concrete proposal to the Japanese authorities. This is done, of course, at the expense of the leaseholders. Both the British Ambassador and the British leaseholders' committee have indicated that they would be glad of American participation. I can see no objection to American perpetual leaseholders participating in the work of the British holders as individuals. In view, however, of our

- 3 -

our Government's position in the past that this property question is not a matter of public concern to the United States, I shall take no concerted action with the British without instructions.

The project, if successful, would put an end to a matter which has caused much friction and I think Americans would be well advised to cooperate. I think also that the Embassy might help informally. I should be glad to have the Department's instructions.

Respectfully yours,

Edwin L. Neville

Mr. Ward:

Mr. Turner:

Japanese law provides, in the case of settlements of estates of foreigners, that the laws of the country of the deceased shall apply.

There is considerable variance in the practice of Japanese courts in the administration of estates of deceased foreigners, differences in practice varying with circumstances. The courts almost invariably exercise jurisdiction whenever the deceased has possessed real property in Japan. In certain places where there are large colonies of foreigners, such as Yokohama, the courts are disposed to prove the wills of deceased foreigners, but in the provinces the courts usually decline to have anything to do with the estates of foreigners who have died, whether or not a will has been left; provided, however, that no real property situated in Japan is involved.

I believe that you will find in the files the case of the estate of Irene Mann, an American who died in Nara, a provincial city, in about 1916 or '17. If reports are available you will find that the local court was most reluctant to accept jurisdiction, even though Miss Mann had owned real property in Japan.

The Department is unable to make an officially authoritative statement regarding the probate laws of Japan, but it appears from unofficial information in the

-2-

the Department's possession, which is believed to be substantially reliable, that in the settlement of estates of foreigners the laws of the country of the decedent apply. The following excerpts taken from unofficial digests of the laws of Japan are quoted for your information.

**Administration of estates.** "The heir usually assumes his duties and acts as executor or administrator, but executors can be appointed by will. By Japanese law the property goes to the heirs directly, and the executor, if there is one, is a mere manager and does not become the owner of the property. In the case of foreigners, succession to property at death is determined according to the law of the country of the decedent.

"To obtain probate no bond is necessary at present, and the proceedings are confined to proving the execution of the will. Wills are not 'recorded' in the court as in America and England, and ancillary probate of foreign wills is not required. A copy of a will proved abroad will be recognized in Japan, if properly certified by the competent authorities or consul.

"There is no process by which an executor can obtain a formal discharge from the court upon the completion of his duties or upon his resignation.

"In case the heir is unknown, the property of the succession is constituted into a juridical entity and placed under the charge of a manager specially appointed by the court; and in the entire absence of an heir, the estate reverts to the State after certain public notifications have been given and a certain period of time has expired without any person claiming the property."

## Descent and Succession
"The family law of Japan and the law of succession as set forth in Books IV and V of the Civil Code differ from the other parts of the Code in being mainly of native origin. They are founded upon and adapted to the peculiar conditions of society in Japan. In accordance with the general principles of international law which obtain among European nations, the Government of Japan has, for the most part, exempted foreigners from the operation of its peculiar family law and left them to be governed by the laws of their own nationalities, which, however, are of course to be administered and applied by the Japanese courts, except as otherwise provided by treaty. If a person belongs to a country, the law of which is different according to the locality, he is governed by the law of the locality to which he belongs. Thus an American citizen remains, as to family and succession matters, under the law of his particular home State.

"Succession to property at death is determined according to the law of the country of the decedent. This applies to the question as to who is to receive the property. The appointment and the powers of an executor would be determined by Japanese law, unless otherwise provided by treaty."

## Wills
"As regards foreigners, the existence and the effect of a will are governed by the law of the nationality of the testator. As to the form and manner of making a will, the law of the place where it is made may be followed. Apparently, therefore, so far as the form is concerned, a foreigner may make his will in the manner prescribed by his own law or by the law of Japan; but care must be exercised in regard to form when disposing of property situated abroad. The law is in a rather uncertain state, and it would be well to comply with both the foreign and Japanese requirements as regards form." (The excerpts above quoted are taken from a Digest of the Laws of Japan

=== Page 15 === (confidence 1.00)

Japan prepared in 1921 by J. E. de Becker, Esquire of Yokohama and Tokyo, and published in Hubbell's "Synopses of Laws, 1922").

**Taxation-Succession Duties.** "A succession duty is payable by an estate situated in Japan, wherever the succession takes place, whether in or out of Japan, and whether the party inheriting is a Japanese or a foreigner, including any property given away within one year prior to the succession. The successor must within three months of knowledge of the succession, (six months if he is out of Japan) file a statement of the estate." (The above-quoted excerpt was taken from a Digest of the Laws of Japan prepared by Dr. Rokuichiro Masujima and published in Martindale-Hubbell Law Directory, Volume II, 1932).

So far as the Department is aware, Japanese inheritance or succession taxes are not applicable to the property of foreigners located outside of Japan.

It appears from information in the Department's possession that while Japanese courts usually assume jurisdiction in the settlement of estates of foreigners in areas frequented by foreigners especially when the estate includes real property, courts in the provinces where few foreigners reside sometimes decline to assume jurisdiction in the settlement of the estates of foreigners when such estates do not include real property among their assets.

Article I of the Japanese Alien Land Law, which became effective on November 10, 1925, contains the following provision:

"Article I.

"Article I. The possession of rights in land within the Empire by aliens or alien juridical persons belonging to nations which prescribe, or impose conditions or limitations upon, the possession of rights in land by Japanese subjects or juridical persons, may similarly be prohibited, or identical or similar conditions or limitations may be imposed thereon, by Imperial decree."

So far as the Department is aware, the provisions of the quoted article have not been invoked against American citizens domiciled in the States which impose restrictions upon the possession of land by Japanese subjects, and the Department is not aware of any other provision of Japanese law or regulation which would prevent American citizens disposing by will of their real estate in Japan.

Article I of the Treaty of Commerce and Navigation concluded between the United States and Japan on November 22, 1894, contains the following provision relating to taxation:

"They [citizens or subjects of each of the High Contracting Parties] shall not be compelled, under any pretext whatsoever, to pay any charges or taxes other or higher than those that are, or may be paid by native citizens or subjects, or citizens or subjects of the most favored nation."

No other provision in any treaty between the United States and Japan appears to have any relation to the questions submitted in your letter.

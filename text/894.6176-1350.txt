CARBON BLACK SUPPLY POSITION IN THE JAPANESE RUBBER INDUSTRY

**Tires.**
Carbon black is used in the manufacture of motor tyres, printing inks and moulded plastics such as gramophone records.

**Requirements.**
In Japan as elsewhere, the demand for tyre manufacture is the most important use, representing about 75-80% of requirements. In recent years, total Japanese requirements have been about 5,000 tons per annum the proportion used for tyre manufacture being about 4,000 tons per annum. A special quality of 10.5 is required for this purpose of which the U.S.A. and Germany are at present the sole producers.

**Available Supplies.**
(a) **Domestic Production.**
In Formosa carbon black is produced from natural gas and in Japan proper there is some production from acetylene and coal tar distillates. It is generally agreed that the Japanese have not so far succeeded in producing a sufficiently high quality of carbon black for tyre manufacture, although suitable enough for other purposes. Total domestic production before the outbreak of war was from 2,000 to 3,000 tons per annum with a potential capacity of 5,000 to 6,000 tons per annum. (See Appendix I).

(b) **Imports.**
The special carbon black for tyre manufacture was imported solely from the U.S.A. and during the last ten years imports have varied between 2,000 and 5,000 tons per annum except that in 1940 they were at the abnormal rate of nearly 8,000 tons per annum. These imports ceased in the early part of 1941. (See Appendix II).

(c) **Present Stocks.**
In view of the difficulty of computing the actual amount consumed of the carbon black delivered to Japanese rubber manufacturers, no reliable estimates of present stocks are available. However, an analysis of the position so far as we know it, (see Appendix III), suggests that at the commencement of 1942 stocks of carbon black for tyre manufacture were between nil and 3,000 tons or up to 8 months supply.

If certain economies had been carried out since 1937, i.e. 20% dilution of imported with domestic black, the maximum stock might have been further increased to 7,000 tons or 21 months' supply. A reliable trade source however suggests that at the beginning of 1942 stocks may have been between 3 and 4 months supply only.

**Conclusions.**
It is clear that, while Japan has ample supplies of raw rubber her manufacture of motor tyres is likely to be handicapped by the lack of the special carbon black which is needed. The alternatives open to her are as follows:-

(a) **To use alternative materials.**
Japan could use either zinc oxide, china clay or the inferior grades of domestic black. If this were done the tyres would wear out about twice as fast. The increased quantity of rubber needed to maintain military vehicles could easily be provided but it is very doubtful whether the necessary increase in manufacturing capacity could be provided in a reasonable time. The more frequent necessity for replacement might also be a disadvantage from the operational viewpoint.

(b) **To obtain imports from Germany.**
German carbon black is at least equal in quality to the best American blacks. However it is doubtful whether Germany has any exportable surplus, but there is evidence that Japan wishes to exchange rubber or tyres for carbon black. In view of the great German need for natural rubber it seems very probable that this scheme may be attempted. There are however other materials which the Germans can barter with the Japanese.

(c) **To improve the quality of domestic production.**
This line of attack is undoubtedly being actively pursued with results difficult to foresee. Should Japan be successful in this direction she would have little difficulty in meeting requirements. In the meantime it is possible that present domestic production may be used to some extent in diluting any remaining stocks of American black.

JAPANESE DOMESTIC PRODUCTION OF CARBON BLACK.

In 1931 Japan started to produce carbon black in Formosa from the natural gas available in the island. This product has since been steadily developed but it is not yet known. The type of manufacture of carbon black has been able to emulate American and other countries. The natural gas although they appear to have installed a high degree of delivery and have endeavoured strenuously over the past ten years. The American imported product.

The Japanese Department of Commerce are encouraging the production of black from acetylene; also from coal tar distillates using a German process. Production from acetylene would not give a product very suitable for type manufacture and experiments with the German process are subject in their infancy.

A list of Japanese carbon black producers is attached from which it can be deduced that the total capacity in Formosa is about 4,000 tons and in Japan proper about 1,800 tons at present. Formosan production, however, has been much lower than reported capacity as shown below.

# APPENDIX III.

## ANALYSIS OF STOCK POSITION.

As regards tyre manufacture the stock position, the relationship between imports and consumption. The lack of any relation regarding the latter make stock estimates open to a lower margin. It is possible however to estimate an upper and lower limit between which stocks probably lie.

The years 1930-1940 show a more or less regular increase in imports including a 75% increase in 1940 over the average rate in the five previous years. Tyre manufacture undoubtedly increased between 1930 and 1940 although to what extent is not known. Certain calculations have given the following estimates of tyre production in the years 1934 to 1937.

|   | 1934 | 1935 | 1936 | 1937  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|   | (tons)  |   |   |   |
|  Production of Tyres, (Motor Vehicle and Aircraft) | 16,000 | 17,500 | 18,500 | 27,000  |
|  Equivalent Consumption of Carbon Black | 3,200 | 3,500 | 3,700 | 5,400  |
|  Imports of Carbon Black from U.S.A. | 1,550 | 3,570 | 4,880 | 5,300  |

During this period therefore it seems that imports were entirely consumed with the exception of about 500 tons. In the succeeding years 1938 to 1940 16,740 tons of black were imported.

For the calculation of the minimum stock figure it can be assumed that tyre production remained at the 1937 level in which case at the end of 1940 stocks would only have increased by another 540 tons. Minimum stock would therefore be about 1,000 tons plus any stock accumulated in the years 1930-1933. During these years imports were 11,640 tons or about 2,900 tons per year which does not indicate any likely addition of stock from this period. Minimum stocks therefore remain at 1,000 tons at the beginning of 1941.

To calculate maximum stocks it is assumed that the 1937 level of tyre production is too high and that the level in the years 1937 - 1940 was about 4,000 tons. This would give additions to stock during the years 1934 - 1940 of 5,600 tons.

These calculations give stocks at the beginning of 1941 as 1,000 tons minimum to 5,600 tons maximum. A reliable trade source estimates stocks of carbon black for tyre manufacture at this time to be a year's supply or about 4,000 tons.

During 1941 itself only 1,100 tons of carbon black were imported so that on a consumption level of 4,000 tons stock decreased by 2,900 tons.

At the commencement of 1942 we therefore have a minimum estimated stock of nil with a maximum stock of 2,700 tons or about 8 months' supply. The corresponding trade estimate is 1,100 tons or 3 - 4 months' supply.

There is however another feature which must not be lost sight of which is that total supplies of black during recent years (i.e. imports plus domestic production) have probably shown a surplus over requirements. It has therefore been possible that Japan has, for tyre manufacture, been diluting her imported black with the domestic quality. If this has been done since say 1937 to the extent of 20% there would be an economy of some 4,000 tons of high quality black which could have been placed to stock secretly.

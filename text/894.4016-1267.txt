Dear Mr. Hornbeck:

Our mutual friend, Mr. Earl Thacker of Honolulu, has told me of your marriage and I want to join with other of your friends in wishing you every happiness.

Mr. Thacker also advised me that you might be interested in some of the problems that are coming up on the Pacific Coast and if I may take the liberty I would like to write you in regard to the important problem of a new appointee as Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization in this area. I realize that this appointment comes within the province of the Secretary of Labor but in consideration of our relations with the Orient it is very important that the individual appointed be one who is sympathetic—ally familiar with the interests and problems facing the groups that are most often dealt with in this area.

A 894.4016

It would be my hope that the appointee would, therefore, be one not only interested in but familiar with the Orient and Oriental peoples. The late Mr. E. C. Cahill, Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization in San Francisco, was very helpful in this matter. We found him sympathetic and cooperative with the problems in hand. If we could have a man of a similar stamp appointed it would be very advantageous not only to the Pacific Coast but to the country as well.

Another matter that has disturbed me considerably of late is the question of the attitude of the Government in Tokyo as regards the Jewish problem. There have been reports that some of the Jewish professors in universities at Tokyo have been dismissed, due principally, according to the newspapers, to pressure from Germany. I have asked the Japanese here concerning this matter and they state that their advices from Japan are to the effect that no such action has been taken, or is contemplated, as regards the Japanese government. If you could give me any further light on this question I would appreciate it very much.

Mr. Welles
Mr. Wallace M. Alexander, of Alexandria, Baldwin, Limited, San Francisco and Honolulu, is a prominent West Coast business man and a definitely public-spirited citizen. From him I recently received a letter under date December 20, a paragraph of which might perhaps be of interest to the Department of Labor. The paragraph reads as follows:

"...if I may take the liberty I would like to write you in regard to the important problem of a new appointee as Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization in this area. I realize that this appointment comes within the province of the Secretary of Labor but in consideration of our relations with the Orient it is very important that the individual appointed be one who is sympathetically familiar with the interests and problems facing the groups that are most often dealt with in this area. It would be my hope that the appointee would, therefore, be one not only interested in but familiar with the Orient and Oriental peoples. The late Mr. E. C. Cahill, Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization in San Francisco, was very helpful in this matter. We found him sympathetic and cooperative with the problems in hand. If we could have a man of a similar stamp appointed it would be very advantageous not only to the Pacific Coast but to the country as well."

Stanley K. Hornbeck

Dear Mr. Alexander:

It is a pleasure to me to have your interesting letter of December 20, and for it and for your good wishes of the Season to me and Mrs. Hornbeck I thank you.

With regard to the suggestion which you make in reference to the importance of the office of Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization in San Francisco, I am very glad to have your views and I wonder whether you have addressed the Secretary of Labor on the subject.

With regard to the question of the attitude of the Japanese Government on the Jewish problem, we are informed that the press in Japan carried a report that the Japanese Foreign Office spokesman declared on November 25 that the recently concluded German-Japanese cultural cooperation agreement has no connection with the treatment of Jewish residents and that there will be no discrimination against German teachers employed in Japan who are not in sympathy with the Nazi regime in Germany.

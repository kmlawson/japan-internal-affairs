EASTERN SIBERIA (1).
Winter conditions in Eastern Siberia are most
severe and last from the middle October to mid
April.
By the end of October all harbours
north of Vladivostok are completely frozen over
and from Ootober to end of November there are
heavy falls of snow; there are snow falls again
in February and March. The weather in December
and January is beautifully clear, calm and
intensly cold.
By end of December the ice is
several feet thick as much as four miles out to
sea, and the Gulf of Tartary is full of drift ice.
Notwithstanding these drawhacks there are several
harbours and bays used by the Russians during the
period of open water for seaplane bases and as
stations for exceedingly fast heavily armed motor
boats similar to the British E boats, these boats
all carry torpedoes.
I have no definite knowledge of what happens to
the seaplenes and the motor hoats during the winter
months and I assume they will all return to
Vladivostok.

De Castries Bay.

mouth of the Amur River.

PEASANT INTO SAMURAI
1. THE ANCIENT SPIRIT:
SUMMARY: Under the caste system of feudal JAPAN, from AD 1200 to 1867,
peasants were not allowed to have weapons in their possession.
All fight ing was done by the warrior class, the SAWURAI, who alone had the
privilege and distinction of being armed. Their characteristic weapons
were the long and short swords, held to be 'the soul of the SAWURAI'. The
warriors could use their swords on the peasant cattle without effective
legal restraint. Even today, status as members of the peasant-caste is
entered on the police records held for every JAPANESE.
Conscription and training on the EUROPEAN model were introduced in 1872.
Five years later, SAMURAI, fighting in the old way with the old weapons,
went down before the superior firepower and tactics of the despised peasant
conscripts.
After this battle JAPANESE leaders visualised a conscript Army, equipped
with modern arms but permeated with the ancient warrior spirit, as a weapon
for world conquest. But they failed to carry the lesson of that battle to
its logical conclusion, namely, that the final answer lay, not with spirit,
but with superior firepower. From that time, 'the Way of the Warrior'
(BUSHIDO) (*1) became the privilege of every JAPANESE, not by virtue of
caste, but by being born JAPANESE. This nation-wide caste promotion can
only be compared to the transformation of all GERMANS into the Master Race.
The JAPANESE caste promotion was far greater, as the JAPANESE peasant had
always occupied a more lowly status than any GERMAN peasant or worker.
The custom which obtained among the SAMURAI of wearing two swords was
abolished by an edict issued on the 28th March 1876, and taking effect from
1 January 1877. The edict was obeyed by this strangely docile people with-
out a blow being struck, and the curio-shops displayed heaps of swords which,
a few months before, the owners would less willingly have parted with than
with life itself. Shortly afterwards a second edict appeared, rescinding
the first and leaving any one at liberty to wear what sword he pleased. But
as the privilege of a class distinction was thus obliterated, none cared
to take advantage of the permission, and two-sworded JAPANESE warrior
was for a time extinct.
Symbolic of the merging of the ancient spirit with modern weapons is the
fact that the JAPANESE soldier wears his bayonet on leave. He is taught
that this represents the sword of the SAMURAI and thus mmrks his promotion
to the warrior caste.

2. THE MODERN VERSION:
SUmMARY: A synthetic military code, purporting to be the expression of the
virtues by which JAPAN's feudal warriors lived and died, was in-
vented. This code is BUSHIDO, the 'Way of the Warrior'. (See Appendix 'A').
Although JAPAN's totalitarianism is ancient and instinctive, BUSHIDO is
almost as recent, and was concocted with equal deliberation and for sub-
stantielly the same purposes, as the NAZI creed. Like that creed, and with
greater success, it appeals to the past for justification. In ancestor
worshipping JAPAN, above all, this is essential, for the past is the rep-
ository of all virtues.
As a concept BUSHIDO, Like KODO (See ATIS Research Report No 76, Part II)
is typically vague. Ideally, it involves:
(a) Blind loyalty to superiors.
(b) Unsleeping revenge, whenever required, disregarding every conflicting
human tie or obligation until it is satisfied.
(c) Absolute disregard of death in carrying out duty.
(d) The spirit of continuous attack, climaxed by annihilating victory in
hand to hand combat.
(e) No surrender and no prisoners.
(f) Conviction that the end justifies the means.
BUSHIDO is training in fanaticism. It seeks to inculcate a superior spirit-
ual energy in the soldiers and people of JAPAN so that she can, if necessary.
wear down and outlast materially stronger but less determined adversaries
until they accept some compromise which will permit JAPAN to digest part of
her conquests in peace.

1. VICTORY AT A SINGLE STHOKE:
SUMARY: The sudden all-out Pacific attacks of Dec 41 were in the JAPANESE
tradition of victory at a single overwhelming stroke. This pre-

nature of the JAPANESE; it appears to be associated with the skill, the sud-

and a sport in JAPAN's warrior tradition. Even should success in the Pacific
be incomplete, however, the leaders calculated that victory would only be
postponed, or result in a victorious compromise. Contemptuous of the staying
power, the intemal unity and discipline, the sacrificial capacity of the West-

the militarists counted heavily upon their belief that the willingness of the
JAPANESE soldier to die wes greater than that of any adversary.

humen ties, even family obligations, were overridden by the duty of revenge.

1. The savagery of the JAPAnESE soldier springs from the traditional non-
humanity of the warrior of feudal times.
2. Until conscription in 1872, the JAPANESE peasantry was neither allowed
to bear arms nor to think. Constant and detailed indoctrination has been
necessary to infuse the ancient warrior spirit into the bovine peasant mater-
ia1.
3. BUSHIDO is a modern invention, devised to whip up JAPANESE fighting fan-
aticism and to instil a sense of racial superiority. In this, it has had
considerable success.
4. The product of JAPANESE indoctrination is the warrior rather than the
soldier: fanatical, often suicidal, brave rather than well disciplined. The
language used in their speeches and orders, often melodramatic to Western
ears, is not mere rhetoric, as shown most recently by the resistance and mass
suicides on SAIPAN.
5. In Dec 41 JAPAN's leaders hoped for quick, decisive victory. Failing
this, they believed that the JAPANESE attitude to war, their self-sacrifice
and the greater willingness to die of the JAPANESE soldier would wear down
the ALLIED will to victory.
6. The JAPANESE have stressed the doctrine of the permanent offensive. Even
defense is conceived as a form of attack.
7. The JAPANESE soldier has been indoctrinated with contempt for his enemies,
and an exaggerated evaluation of his own prowess, especially in hand-to-hand
fighting and attack. This distortion, inspiring when things were going well
should accelerate collapse of morale when disproved by battle experience.
8. The JAPANESE soldier is taught that the material superiority of the ALLIBS

9. The policy of death before capture or surrender is still adhered to by

10. In the JAPANESE tradition, revenge has been enforced by the compulsion of
caste and public opinion rather than regarded as a private obligation. Revenge
continues to be a powerful motive force.
a. The SAMURAI caste was forced to yield to the impact of Western culture

About 1o00 AD a form of drama arose 1n JAPAN which has remained extremely

ties and impulses.
In JAPANESE drama and fiction, as a broad distinction, it may be said that
the SAMURAI followed the path of duty, while the merchants and "common people"
allowed their human sympathies to dictate their course of action.
The situation of the eldest son of TOKUGAWA, Iyeyasu, offers an historical
example. When quite young he was married to the daughter of HIDEYOSHI, and
adopted by an allied clan. When TOkUGAWA, Iyeyasu was plotting the destruction
of the HIDEYOSHI clan, his sense of obligation made the young man take arms
against his father. The JAPANESE respected him for not letting his natural
affections override his duty.

Observance of Unit Holidays
Col Harold DOUD, Signal Corps, served as a company officer attached to a
JAPANESE infantry regiment in 1934-35. The following passage on observance

I was now getting used to garrison life and looked forward to our first
holiday, scheduled for 21 Aug. This was the anniversary of the storming of
BANRYU Hill during the siege of PORT ARTHUR; here the regiment had disting-
uished itself by an assault against withering fire with the bayonet.
When the looked-for day finally arrived I soon discovered that our conception
of a holiday as a day of rest was not shared by the JAPANESE Army. The fest-
1vities started with a 3 AM reveille. After breakfast, the men filed into the
orderly room, a section at a time, and bowed low before the pictures or their
comrades who had been killed in action.
Following this ceremony, the 2 Company marched to a shrine of the regiment's
War dead on MUKOYAMA (YONDER MOUNTAIN). Here, after fitting obeisance, a
bayonet tournament was held in commemoration of the bayonet assault that
had carried BANRYU Hill. The tournament ended shortly after daylight. Another
bow to the shrine and back we marched to barracks. There the supply sergeant
produced SAkk and a species of dried flounder, which we downed with a will.
Then, after a few minutes' rest the regular day's work began. The other com-
panies celebrated the holiday in similar fashion. And so I learned that if
you start your holiday observance early enough 1t will not interfere with a
drill schedule. To the JAPANESE soldier a holiday is a day for additional
sacrifice and effort in the service of his Emperor.

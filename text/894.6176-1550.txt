Messrs. Grant and Johnson came in to explore the questions raised by the letter of August 23, 1945 from Mr. F. W. Utz, Executive Vice President of the Rubber Development Corporation, to Mr. Clayton.

Authority

Messrs. Grant and Johnson were asked the status of RDC's authority to enter into new commitments for public purchase. Mr. Johnson stated that Section 5 (d) of the RFC Act, plus the fact that neither the President nor Congress had acted to terminate the national emergency, plus a momentarily expected directive from WPB which would be valid under existing legislation and executive orders until December 31, 1945, appeared to give RDC ample authority to 'purchase, acquire, sell and otherwise deal in strategic and critical materials' (e.g. rubber, as so declared by the President). He also stated that the Reconversion Act, implemented by a momentarily expected declaration from the Director of OWMR that the action would be to the interest of this Government, would give RDC ample authority to fulfil existing commitments.

Price

1. The cost of producing GR-8 general purpose synthetic rubber is now almost down to 18-1/2¢ per pound and serious consideration is being given to removing the "war-order" premium of 17-1/2¢. Such removal would make GR-8 available in the export market at 18-1/2¢, instead of at 36¢.

2. Natural rubber prices immediately preceding the war were in the 16¢-22¢ range, a price generally considered ample even under the clouds of war.

3. Wartime prices, such as Ceylon's 32.36¢, were designed as incentives for high-level production for war purposes. Their continuance is not necessary.

4. A higher opening price, followed by declines would be harmful to consumers and rubber product dealers who would be caught with high-cost inventory.

5. The producing countries themselves should want a price low enough to reestablish natural in competition with synthetic.

6. Although plantation rehabilitation costs may be large, most companies have sizeable reserves set aside for the purpose. The price paid for natural should enable Rubber Reserve to resell at its established 22-1/2¢ ceiling, and thus avoid inflationary pressures, without subsidizing rehabilitation costs.

7. The British and Dutch need foreign exchange and could obtain only limited quantities for a limited period elsewhere if they refused to meet a reasonable (20¢) price from by far the largest single consuming country.

8. CRMB allocations are not self-executing, but only recommendatory subject to implementation by the national agencies of the participating Governments. Therefore agreement on price would be a necessary prerequisite to air-tight allocation in the event of a show-down.

## Procedure on Pricing

Mr. Grant stated he had already been approached informally by the Dutch with "feelers" on pricing and anticipated early and strong pressure from both British and Dutch. Mr. Kennedy suggested the possibility of receiving rubber "on consignment" for several months under CRMB allocation, with price to be settled retroactively to the termination of mutual aid, and after an interim period in which the Rubber Study Group could hold a meeting and explore fully the principles of pricing.

of pricing. It was made absolutely clear that the Study Group could not itself negotiate prices but its discussions would provide a valuable opportunity to explore the question and to lay the groundwork for the principles the United States would desire. Subsequently the price negotiations would take place between the U. S. and the British, Dutch, French, and Thais in either a series of bilateral meetings or multilateral talks. It appeared that the Department would lead these negotiations, with RDC in attendance, rather than have RDC conduct the negotiations as suggested in Mr. Utz's letter.

Mr. Kennedy stated he was leaving on Sunday, August 26, for London and would informally propose that price negotiations be delayed pending an early meeting of the Study Group in late September or early October. Messrs. Grant and Johnson concurred provided Mr. Kennedy could get the British and Dutch to agree not to press RDC on the question of price before the date for negotiations.

## Far Eastern Information

Mr. Johnson reviewed developments. At a meeting called by Mr. Snyder of OWMR on August 18 to consider the establishment of a Policy Committee on Rubber, Messrs. Snyder, Nathan, Krug, Wilson and others had proposed greatly enlarging the U.S. rubber representation in Malaya and in the N.E.I. beyond three people in each area. Mr. Wilson agreed to submit a list of proposed additional names to RDC. After consulting with Mr. A. L. Viles, President of the Rubber Manufacturers' Association, Mr. Wilson had suggested that Messrs. Bicknell of U.S. Rubber Company, Hotchkiss of the Cambridge Rubber Company, Blandin of Goodyear, Stevens of Goodrich and Roberts of Firestone proceed immediately to the Far East as an industry committee under the "auspices" (i.e. assistance on transportation) of RDC to survey conditions and report to the industry, and to RDC for the information of all Government departments.

Mr. Johnson pointed out that when RDC had been seeking men for the liaison work with the British and Dutch, the industry had not volunteered, and in some cases had even refused to make available, men of this caliber. Mr. Kennedy stated the Department could not support the sending of an industry mission of this kind, particularly into Malaya in the face of the Malayan Rubber Estate Owners Company arrangement for pooling personnel and resources, even though the information they might obtain would probably be extremely valuable to the U.S. in the proposed Rubber Study Group meeting and in price negotiations. The alternative was discussed of having the same men proceed as Government officials and as a subcommittee of the Department's Rubber Advisory Panel. Mr. Kennedy was far more favorable to this possibility although he said he would prefer being given an opportunity to explore the matter further in the Department before suggesting it to the men involved.

# GR-S Export Price

Mr. Johnson, in his capacity as a representative of RFC's Office of Rubber Reserve, asked whether the Department could give Rubber Reserve a letter suggesting elimination of the 17-1/2¢ "surcharge" on GR-S exports. Mr. Kennedy said he would look into the matter but felt careful consideration would have to be given to the timing of all these interrelated pricing matters and he would prefer not to take action toward sending such a letter until after his return from London. Messrs. Grant and Johnson were agreeable but said they felt that it would be much better to reduce the GR-S price before opening natural rubber price negotiations and not merely to use it as a trading weapon in any negotiations.

## Note on the second quarter requirements of Spain

The Secretary has received a letter dated March 7 from the Commercial Counselor of the Spanish Embassy, as follows:

"I wish to refer to your letter of February the 6th in which you kindly informed me of the recommendations made by the Combined Rubber Committee on Spanish quotas of rubber. I have today received a cable from my Government giving me in a succinct manner the information requested by you in said letter. I am advised that a full report on Spanish needs follows by mail; as soon as I receive it I shall be very glad to give you the information it contains.

In the meantime I am informed:

1. The Spanish Syndicate is actively trying to procure from the firm Pirelli 250 tons of natural rubber corresponding to the quota granted to us for the first quarter of 1946.

2. Spanish needs of rubber can be calculated at the present moment at 6,000 tons of natural rubber and 6,000 tons of synthetic rubber per year.

3. Spain did not import any natural rubber during the first semester of 1945 and earnestly requests that this be taken into consideration by the Combined Rubber Committee so that the amount which had been stipulated in 1945 of 250 tons per quarter, and which was not imported due to lack of supply, be now granted.

4. Spain's present stocks of natural rubber amount to 250 tons, and its present stocks of synthetic rubber, to 150 tons.

5. Spain's requirements of reclaimed rubber are as follows: 5,000 tons 1st grade, 30,000 tons 2nd grade. These requirements naturally vary in proportion to the imports of natural and synthetic rubber permitted.

6. No definite agreement regarding quantity has been reached with Portugal concerning imports from their Colonies of natural rubber. Nevertheless, whatever rubber is imported in Spain from the Portuguese Colonies is to be manufactured in Spain in the form of tires and returned to Portugal as manufactured products.

I hope that this information will be of use to you in establishing the allocations for Spain during the 2nd quarter of this year."

Further to the UNRRA request circulated in Document CRC-17,
the following request from UNRRA has been received by the Secretary:

"As the rubber requirements for Byelo and Ukraine
Russia have been clarified, we are submitting to
the Rubber Committee requirements for 300 metric
tons of natural latex rubber.

This letter is a supplement to our letter dated 26
February 1946, in which we submitted to the Rubber
Committee UNRRA's requirements for the second quarter
of 1946.

We are aware that natural latex rubber is in extreme
short supply the world over. The latex will be used
in the manufacture of medical and surgical products
for which we understand crude rubber is unsatisfactory.

The preferred source of supply is the U.K. or the
United States. Subsequently, we will submit no crude
rubber requirements for Byelo and Ukraine Russia for
1946. Instead we request 100 metric tons latex rubber
for Byelo Russia and 200 metric tons for Ukraine Russia."

Staff Comment. Advice recently received from the British authorities
indicates that there will be only about 65 long tons of liquid latex
(dry rubber content) available for allocation from U.K. sources in the

Subject: Inspection Arrangements for rubber shipped to the United States from Japan

As you may know, the Combined Rubber Committee on March 12, 1946, allocated to the United States 10,000 long tons of natural rubber found in Japan and reported by SCAP to be surplus (at that date) to Japanese domestic requirements. This allocation was agreed by the British and Dutch members of the Combined Rubber Committee, with the stipulation that (a) the price of such rubber be equated with the price being paid for rubber from other Far Eastern sources, and (b) an investigation be made to ascertain the source of such rubber in order to determine, as far as possible, the original ownership. With these provisions, the U.S. Member agreed, and this quantity of rubber was in due course shipped to the United States. Some two-thirds have already been examined here by British and Dutch inspectors and by U.S. inspectors appointed by the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, and we have received a report from our inspector regarding the proportion of rubber identifiable as having been produced in British Far Eastern territories.

On completion of the examination of the balance of the 10,000 tons shipped here from Japan, H.M. Government will present to the appropriate authorities a claim for the value of that quantity for which a claim for restitution would appear to be eligible under the terms of the policy which is finally adopted by the Far Eastern Commission with respect to the restitution of property looted by the Japanese. Inasmuch as the inspection of this rubber has not yet been completed, it appears desirable that it be examined not only with the purpose of establishing the country of origin but also with a view to determining the date of production of the rubber, to the extent that this is possible, in case the

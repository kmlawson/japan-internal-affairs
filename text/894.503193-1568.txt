SUBJECT: Present Situation of Japanese Plant and Remaining Japanese Technicians in Manchuria.

THE HONORABLE

THE SECRETARY OF STATE,

WASHINGTON, D. C.

Sir:

I have the honor to enclose as of possible interest a copy (in English translation) of an item published in several consecutive issues of the Japanese language newspaper TOHOKU DOHO (NORTHEAST LEADER, Changchun), on the general subject of the present state of the original Japanese investment in the industrial and economic plant of Manchuria, and the relationship of the remaining Japanese technicians to the exploitation of that plant. This item gives the summary of a speech delivered on the subject by Mr. Tatsunosuke TAKASAKI, ex-President of the Manchuria Heavy Industry Development Company, and currently Councillor of the Economic Commission and Resources Commission of the Northeast Headquarters.

It will be observed that Mr. Takasaki makes note of the circumstance that the Chinese economic plants in Manchuria have gone awry. This, he said, was due to the several circumstances that 1) the Soviet removals and destruction of industrial installations and damages due to Chinese mob action were unusually large, 2) the budget has miscarried due to inflation, and 3) there has been a decline in transport capacity as a result of the current civil strife. Mr. Takasaki gave a total evaluation of the Manchurian industrial plant at US $12,000,000,000, with the amount remaining after Soviet removals still valued at US $10,000,000,000. He indicated, however, that "further investigations reveal that the decline of operating capacity caused by the looting of mobs, in addition to Soviet removals, was beyond expectation, mostly showing a decline of 60%, in some cases 100% of operating costs." Mr. Takasaki estimated that an average of 10% of value of the original Japanese investment remained. He stated that where the expenditures for personal services required in reconstruction and administration of the installation remaining (after the Soviet removals) might have been estimated at US $2,500,000,000, under present circumstances expenditures for personal services may be estimated to require only US $300,000,000. Assuming 10% of the technicians to be Japanese in each of the two several cases, US $25,000,000 would have been required for the salary of 25,000 Japanese (at $1000 per year) in the first instance, and US $3,000,000 as salary for 3000 Japanese (at the same rate) in the second instance.

Respectfully yours,

O. Edmund Clubb
American Consul General
(Harbin)

TRANSLATION

Item published in TOHOKU DOHO (NORTHEAST LEADER), Changchun, March 30, April 3, April 4, 1947.

Translated: April 9, 1947
Translator: TT

Outline of a speech delivered by Mr. Tatsunosuke Takasaki, ex-president of the Manchuria Heavy Industry Development Co., Fuhsin Coal Mine, Hokang Coal Mine, Manchuria Mining Co., Tungpientao Development Co., Hsian Coal Mine, Peipiao Coal Mine, etc., at present councillor of the Economic Commission and Resources Commission of the Northeast Headquarters, on the occasion of the Japanese Residents Liaison Conference held in Mukden on March 26, 1947.

First, he recommended to all Japanese residents retained in this country that they should be grateful for their treatment by the Chinese authorities, their lives and fortune protected, their livings assured, with the accommodation of educating their children and the possibility of communicating with Japan.

Second, he suggested to the Chinese authorities that the Japanese retained in this country are selected from more than one million residents and they are specialized in technics, mainly intellectual workers. Therefore they should not be regarded as forced laborers, but by reasonable treatment they could make best use of their abilities.

Third, he expressed his opinion on the elimination of the retained Japanese. At the beginning they were full of expectation in reconstruction work in the Northeast regions, but later they found that owing to the slow progress of reconstruction work, they could not display their abilities in full. This was perhaps due to their low salaries at this time of high price drive, and also due to the impossibility of occupying responsible positions, giving chance to display their full abilities. Most of them held, before the termination of the War, responsible positions in certain branches of official or private enterprises. On the other hand, following points may be cited as the main reasons which hindered the anticipated progress of reconstruction work:

1. Removal and destruction of industrial installations by the Soviets and through riots were unexpectedly large;
2. Miscarriage of budget owing to the inflation.
3. Decline of transport capacity caused by the conflict with the Communists.

Referring to the question of Japanese technicians he expressed his straightforward opinion, based on his industrial common sense, as follows:

"I first valued the remaining industrial equipments at about 10 billion U. S. dollars, deducting the value of installations removed by the Soviets at 2 billion U. S. dollars, the total of Japanese investments in mining and industrial enterprises in Northeast China being estimated

Before the termination of the War some 640,000 workmen had been engaging in industrial and mining enterprises in the country, including many excellently trained Chinese technicians. When they are fully mobilized there may perhaps be little necessity in retaining Japanese technicians, or at least they could be reduced to the minimum number."

SUBJECT: Economic Situation in Manchuria

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to this office's despatch No. 41 of April 10, 1947, in regard to the present state of the original Japanese investment in the industrial and economic plant of Manchuria, and to enclose as of possible interest in this general connection, a copy of a memorandum of conversation of April 12, 1947, with Mr. Tatsunosuke TAKASAKI, a former President of the Manchurian Heavy Industry Development Company and present Councillor to the Economic Commission whose speech was the subject of the aforementioned despatch of April 10.

Mr. Takasaki indicated his belief that the services of American engineers in connection with the exploitation of the existing Manchurian plant would be of benefit to the economy. As things stand at present, he said, there is continued deterioration of the economy in Manchuria.

Respectfully yours,

MEMORANDUM OF CONVERSATION

CONFIDENTIAL

Changchun, China
April 12, 1947

SUBJECT: Economic Situation in Manchuria.

PRESENT: Mr. Tatsunosuke Takasaki (Councillor to Economic Commission), Mr. Tani (translator), Mr. Clubb.

Mr. Takasaki called on a social visit, and in the course of our conversation the talk turned to the subject of the economic situation in Manchuria. Mr. Takasaki referred to the figures given in his recent speech as printed in the TOROKO SUN, and confirmed that his first plans had envisaged the employment of Japanese technicians in the exploitation of a plant retaining a value, after a twist destruction and removals, of US $10,000,000,000. Mr. Takasaki had first thought that 25,000 Japanese technicians could be used in connection with the operation of the remaining industrial plant. It was decided finally to keep 10,000 technicians. At the present time, however, due to the unexpected rapid deterioration of that plant, due to the action of the Chinese Communists and of mobs so that no more than 10% of the original productive capacity and real value of the plant remains, even those 10,000 technicians represent a surplus of technical skill which cannot be used. They have time and again submitted plans to the Chinese for use of such capacities as exist, but the Chinese do not give effect to these plans. It is therefore proposed by Mr. Takasaki that 2/3 of the remaining personnel shall be dispatched to their homes in Japan, leaving only 3,000 technicians (with families).

Mr. Takasaki observed that the Chinese did not have the experience in organization and administration of large industrial plants, and said that they labored under the constant drawback of having no liaison between the several departments within a large organization. At Fushun and Henkeihu, for instance, there is at the present time being piled up large amounts of coal which could be used elsewhere in Manchurian industry. The transport facilities have, however, broken down and the coal does not move. More inexplicably still, at Henkeihu, there is the cement factory which needs coal for its operations but seems to be unable to obtain coal from the coal stock-piles of the local mines. There are other examples of the same situation, the same lack of coordination, in many other enterprises: each little department is independent until itself. In respect to communications, Mr. Takasaki confessed to being worried about the situation, because of the continued deterioration of both rolling stock and the railways themselves. In general, it was his estimate that there was continued deterioration of the economic situation in Manchuria (the agricultural situation not having been touched upon). In those circumstances, it was his estimate, that the newspaper report of a petition being submitted by a local popular body to the Northeast Headquarters to remove from Hukden to Changchun with all of its related organs, had no relationship to reality. He said that in fact it was proposed that the Economic Commission should remove from Changchun to Hukden, and that the removal was being held up only by lack of housing facilities in Hukden.

Mr. Takasaki, who 27 years ago studied in the United States, in California and Chicago, has sent his family back to Japan. He has been in Manchuria for 7 years, with his first chief being Mr. Yoshisuke Hikawa, President of the Manchuria Industrial Development Corporation. Mr. Hikawa's original plan had been to employ the services of American engineers and American capital in connection with the industrial development of Manchuria, this being based upon the Japanese appreciation that they did not themselves.

# SCARCITY OF SPECIAL STEEL OF GOOD QUALITY IN JAPAN

The Japanese Army and Navy are gravely concerned over the present inadequate supplies of steel and special steels for production of munitions and allied materials.

The present difficulty is a result of external and internal factors over some of which the government has no control. The European war, with a subsequent blockade of Germany and extension of hostilities to Scandinavia, has strangled the importation of high-grade special steel from Germany and Sweden. Anxiety also exists over the possibility of further American restrictions on export of scrap and steel to Japan.

Further complicating the situation, stocks of raw materials required for the local manufacture of special steels are small, and diminishing steadily. Supplies of nickel, molybdenum, cobalt, and vanadium on hand are practically exhausted. Heretofore, American scrap iron and steel imported by Japan contained percentages of alloys which were reclaimed in melting down. Recently, the Japanese have complained that the scrap from America no longer contains these alloys. It is probable that since scrap prices have risen, the Japanese are buying only the lower grades of scrap and feeling cheated because of the absence of alloys.

Finally, the prices of scrap iron and steel, and all other metals, have risen to new highs. The Military forces, having planned certain objectives for production in 1939-40, refuse to consider demands of the steel manufacturing companies for an increase in prices for munitions. The Budget allotted certain amounts for the armed forces and they are determined to obtain munitions for prices contemplated during compilation of the budget. This requires that either: '1, the steel producers operate at a considerable loss, or, 2, that the government, from some special extraordinary account, compensate with subsidies all losses incurred. In either case, the government loses money while the Army and Navy apparently remain within budgetary limits.

The Army prices upon which the budget estimates were based, were those of the third quarter, 1938. Since then, the prices of scrap iron and steel have risen more than thirty percent, and prices of the nickel, molybdenum, cobalt, and vanadium alloys have sky-rocketed even more. A choice had to be made between a volume reduction of thirty percent in production for the armed forces, or increased appropriations. Since the latter method required convening the Diet, which might have asked embarrassing questions on other policies of the government, and possibly caused a cabinet crisis, the government has announced that it will divert funds from other departments to subsidize losses. The amount of the subsidy was announced at ¥170,000,000 (170 million yen) and further subsidies for coke and coal will amount to ¥100,000,000 (100 million yen) more.

During May and June, the Army authorities have tried to force industry into totalitarian control, but have been opposed by the Commerce and Industry Ministry, presided over by one of the major capitalists in Japan. Apparently, a battle between the major industrialists and the Army over control of industry will continue, with no decision probable unless a coup by Army factions gives them complete control of governmental policies.

After consultations between the Ministry of Commerce and Industry and Military representatives, the following initial steps were decided upon:

LAW MATERIAL FOR THE JAPANESE STEEL INDUSTRY

By J. Jos. W. Palmer,
Assistant Chief, Metals and Minerals Division.

On October 16, 1940, the exportation of all grades of iron and steel scrap from the United States became subject to license with the further qualification that the issuance of such licenses would thereafter be limited to shipments moving to countries of the Western Hemisphere and to Great Britain. The war having already brought the trade in this material with Continental Europe virtually to a standstill the practical effect of this action was to put an end to the trade in scrap with Africa and the Far East. The outstanding market in these affected areas was Japan for when the United States had long been the principal source of imported scrap so that the question immediately arises as to what effect if any this action will have on the production of steel in that country.

The Japanese is the world's sixth largest steel industry - only the industries of the United States, Germany, Russia, Great Britain, and France have greater capacity and/or output. (See Table I) But Japan, more than any other major steel producing nation, is dependent upon sources outside its immediate physical boundaries for the ferrous raw materials necessary to support its steel industry. Recognizing that a large steel industry could not be created on the basis of their limited domestic iron ore supplies the Japanese have, over the years, concluded arrangements which, under normal conditions, appear to have assured her of a supply of ferrous raw materials more than adequate to her needs and resulting in the addition annually to a stock pile of considerable tonnages of these same materials against the day when one or more sources of supply might be put off.

Unfortunately there is little precise information to be had concerning either this program or the operations of the Japanese iron and steel industry since mid-1937 when Japan, as a war measure, stopped the publication of statistics describing both her production of and her foreign trade in these materials. All discussions of the economics of Japan's raw material supply program must, therefore, of necessity be based upon data available for 1936 and earlier years.

The production of iron ore in Japan, Chosen and Manchuria was not reported for 1936, but probably exceeded the 2,591,822 tons mined in 1935 when Japan produced 515,841 tons, Chosen 598,109 tons, and Manchuria 1,477,872 tons. However, little if any of the 2,075,000 tons mined in the latter two areas reached Japan as ore - the presence of coking coal near the respective deposits of ore coupled with the relatively low mineral content of the latter making it more advantageous to smelt the ore in the respective countries and to ship the resulting pig iron to Japan for conversion into steel. And since little if any pig iron was consumed as such in either Chosen or Manchuria at that time it may be considered that the entire output - 245,184 tons in Chosen and 607,920 tons in Manchuria - moved to Japan proper to augment the 1,906,699 tons of pig iron produced there, chiefly from iron ore imported from China, the Straits Settlements, and elsewhere.

# BALL BEARING PRODUCTION IN JAPAN

An adequate supply of ball bearings of good quality may be regarded as essential to the Japanese war effort, and there are indications that their position in this respect may not be satisfactory. It was stated in the Economic Report prepared by the U.S.A. Commercial Secretary in September, 1941, for example, that the need for ball bearings in Japan was urgent, and that as great efforts as possible would be made to import S.K.F. bearings from Sweden by way of the United States. This source of supply is now practically cut off except by blockade-running and possible shipments by indirect air routes, and the same conditions are imposed on supplies from Japan's allies. It therefore appears probable that Japan will have to rely to a greatly increased extent on her own manufacturing capacity, and it was stated in the report already referred to that special endeavours would be made to develop the home industry.

From a recent report, said to be from a reliable source, it appears that there are now four ball-bearing factories in Japan, Tokyo Bearing Seizo, Osaka; Nihon Seiko K.K., Tokyo; Fujikoshi Kozai K.K., Fukui; and Amatsuji K.K., Amagasaki. It is said that Amatsuji supplies all the balls for the other three manufacturers. Previous to Japan entering the World War, the principal suppliers of balls to Japan were the U.S.A. and Germany, with small supplies from Sweden.

The output from the Tokyo Bearing Seizo is given as equivalent to 30,000 bearings of 30-mm. bore per day, of Nihon Seiko, 20,000 bearings per day, and of Fujikoshi Kozai K.K., 10,000 bearings per day. This gives a total output capacity for the Japanese industry equivalent to 60,000 bearings of 30-mm. bore per day, which may be compared with an estimated total German output capacity of 220,000 bearings per day. It is, of course, possible that Japan possesses other resources for ball-bearing manufacture of which nothing is known, but assuming that the output capacity given is even approximately correct, it would appear that she should be in a position to meet her own probable war requirements so far as quantity is concerned. It is doubtful, however, whether she can produce bearings comparable in quality with those previously obtained from outside sources.

The manufacture of ball bearings is a highly specialised industry. The raw material employed is a high-carbon chromium steel, which presents considerable difficulties in its heat treatment and forging, and it is essential to give lasting wearing qualities that this steel should be of the highest quality and subject to the most exact metallurgical control throughout the whole process of manufacture from the furnace to the finished race. The machining and grinding processes on both the races and balls demand a high degree of precision working throughout. This applies with increasing force the larger the race, and it is only comparatively/

=== Page 18 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

comparatively recently that one of the leading German ball-bearing companies has been able to produce the forgings for the heavier races, these forgings having previously been obtained from the Skefko company in Sweden. The parent Skefko company in that country has paid particular attention to the development and manufacture of the steel used. This steel is particularly liable to develop hair cracks during heat treatment, and the general success of Swedish bearings is due to the exact metallurgical control employed.

Highly specialised machines, made by only a few manufacturers throughout the world, are required for grinding the balls, and it would presumably be extremely difficult for Japan to obtain additional machines of this type at the present time, and almost certainly impossible for her to copy successfully the machines she already has. This factor alone may therefore be decisive in limiting her output of bearings. It would appear from the distribution of the manufacturing processes between her four works already referred to that such ball-grinding machines as the Japanese possess may all be located in one works.

Apart from any question of the ball-grinding facilities available, it has been repeatedly asserted by engineers who have lived in Japan that her greatest weakness in engineering production is in the field of metallurgy, and although there is no reason to doubt that she will be able to turn out ball bearings of any size that she requires, and probably in sufficient quantities for her immediate needs, it appears highly probable that the bearings produced will be of inferior quality, particularly in the larger sizes. It may be said that there has been no information up to the present that the bearings used in Japanese aircraft, tanks, or naval vessels are in any way inferior to those used by the United Nations, but it is reasonable to suppose that she had acquired sufficient stocks of foreign-made bearings before she entered the World War to meet her immediate requirements for all classes of armament.

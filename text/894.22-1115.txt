DIVISION OF COMMUNICATIONS AND RECORDS
DEPARTMENT OF FORENS IN THE ACTIVE ARMY.

1. According to a War Office announcement, the War and Overseas Ministries are drafting regulations to be promulgated as an Imperial ordinance which will permit Koreans to volunteer for active service in the Japanese Army. The ordinance establishing this system will be effective about April 1st, according to Domei. Exemption from active conscript service in the Japanese Army is provided at present by the Conscription Law for natives of Chosen, Taiwan, South Seas Islands and Karafuto, and inu.

2. The allotment of Koreans for the first year will be four hundred and will presumably be limited to those with a thorough knowledge of Japanese. Special six months course will be given those selected before entrance into the Japanese Army, probably consisting of basic military lectures, sanitation, "Seishin Kyoiku" (moral training) military courtesy, etc. The selected trainees will be attached to infantry units of the 19th (Ranau) and the 20th (Jyuzon) Divisions stationed in Chosen. The minimum age limit is expected to be stipulated at 17 years and the term of service, not including the special preconseriential basic training, will be for two years.

3. War Office Statement:

The War Office issued the following statement regarding the entry of Koreans into the Japanese Army, as translated by Domei:

Because of the progress in social conditions in Korea, the time has been thought ripe for establishing a system of volunteer military service for Koreans in order that the people of the Peninsula are received. It is training as befits projects of the Japanese contribute to the defense of Korea and Japan to or. Thus the Korean overseas ministries are well prepared for institutional arrangements for instituting the new system. Those who volunteer are to be selected after a social education has been given by the Korean Government-General and to be provided the same training and status as conscripts in Japan proper on entering the barracks.

This conclusion is partially confirmed by the selection of only a small number of trainees and the maintenance of a volunteer system on a very limited, trial basis rather than as a conscript system.

b. The question of the entrance into the Japanese Army by Koreans has been debated inconclusively in the Diet on several occasions in the past. Japanese officers, known to members of this office, have spoken disparagingly of Koreans as soldiers and disapproved of their acceptance for active service on the ground that they would require more attention than they would be worth. These officers are still believed to have the same convictions which probably are representative of the opinions of many others.

c. Although the press does its utmost to create the impression that all Koreans are anxious to have the Conscription law amended so that they may prove their loyalty to the Japanese Empire by military service, actually any agitation favoring such action comes from a relatively small number of Koreans who benefit from association and friendship with Japanese, especially officials. The loyalty of the greater part of the population of Chosen is still questioned by competent foreigners who have lived in Korea for many years. The vociferous minority of Koreans who want the provisions of the Conscription Law to be applied in Chosen include, however, many apparently sincere young Koreans who believe that the lot of the average citizen will be benefitted under Japanese control if Chosen is regarded as a worthwhile unit of the Empire assuming her share of the defense burdens. The improvement of the status and security of the many Koreans in Manchukuo at present as contrasted with their position under Chinese rulers has also been a strong factor in building up pro-Japanese feeling in Chosen.

d. It is the opinion of this office that the mass conscription of Koreans for active service and their incorporation into separate military units are still many years distant.

# MUTINY OF JAPANESE TROOPS.

1. A certain amount of publicity was given a so-called mutiny of Japanese troops which was said to have occurred either off Taku or at Tangku. According to the press reports, a convoy of Japanese troops arrived off Taku Bar and upon learning that they were being sent ashore for duty in North Shansi instead of being returned to Japan as they had been told when embarking from duty in South China, they refused to leave the transports. Versions of the incident vary considerably, one even went to the extent of saying that a Japanese destroyer had to open fire upon one of the ships with a considerable number of casualties resulting.

2. This office requested the French Assistant Military Attache to obtain any information regarding this so-called mutiny from the officer commanding the French detachment at Tangku. The quarters and posts of this detachment are so located that they are well able to observe events at Tangku. The following report was received from the French Commandant:

"About the 25th or 26th of January, 1939, a Japanese battalion quartered at the village of Quat-ko, on the right bank of the Pei River, received urgent orders to entrain for Taiyuan, Shansi. In this battalion there were approximately two hundred soldiers whose tour of duty in China was nearly completed and who were awaiting return to Japan. When they learned that they were being transferred to Taiyuan their resentment and discontent manifested itself in refusing to depart.

"The Japanese headquarters in Tientsin thereupon gave orders to employ all necessary measures to force the soldiers to obey. The chief of battalion, aided by loyal soldiers, was able to re-establish order but had to resort to the use of arms resulting in the death of two non-commissioned officers and the wounding of a certain number of revolting soldiers.

"Thereupon having given the rebellious troops assurance that they would be relieved from Taiyuan with a maximum delay of fifteen days, the entire battalion departed for its new station on the 27th of January.

"On the 8th of February the relief of those troops due to return was effected by new troops coming from Japan and the old ones were returned to Tangku to await embarkation."

## Comment.

1. It is believed that the above represents a fairly accurate account of what transpired. The Chinese press (Central Government) made much of the affair, but it is not believed to have been any more serious than described in this report. Reports are extant that disorders occur in the Japanese forces, but there is no reason to believe that the above incident indicates any marked breakdown in discipline.

2. It is fairly certain that troops used in North China for duty against irregulars and for employment at garrisons are relieved after four or five months. It is understood that the guerrillas make the routine of the Japanese soldiers extremely arduous by their innumerable actual and false sorties and attacks. As a result after many sleepless nights and much patrol duty, the morale of these soldiers is considerably lowered and they are taken out for rest periods. Again, this seems to be only a normal procedure any invading army would take under the circumstances to maintain discipline and morale.

# JAPANESE PREFECTURAL, REGIONAL AND OVERSEAS SOCIETIES

## Introduction

Known as great "organizers", the Japanese people are generally found to be members of one or more KAI or KYOKAI (societies). In accordance with this propensity, they have formed in the United States and its territories prefectural and regional societies composed of persons coming from the same prefecture, city, area or region in Japan. Such organizations may be compared to the American "State Societies". They bear many different names, some of which are listed below, and are always preceded by the name of a particular area or district in Japan. However, the function and activities of all are believed to be the same:

|  KENJIN KAI | - prefectural society  |
| --- | --- |
|  KAIGAI KYOKAI | - overseas society  |
|  JIN KAI | - peoples' society  |
|  FU JIN KAI | - city district society  |
|  DOSHI KAI | - regional society  |
|  SHINYU KAI | - friends' society  |
|  CHOJIN KAI | - towns peoples' society  |
|  JIN KYOSAI KAI | - peoples' mutual aid society  |
|  KYOYU KAI | - community friends' society  |
|  CHOCHIKU KUMI | - savings society  |
|  AIYU KAI | - loving friends' society  |
|  SHINEN KAI | - young peoples' society  |

In many cases, the internal organization of these groups includes young peoples' divisions (SHINEN KAI), which, together with the women's auxiliaries, exert a very strong nationalistic influence on Nisei members.

## History

Something of the history of these organizations is contained in the following extracts taken from the "ZAIBEI NIHONJIN SHI" ("History of Japanese Residents in America", pp. 650 to 652), compiled by the JAPANESE ASSOCIATION OF AMERICA (ZAIBEI NIHONJIN KAI):

With the gradual increase of Japanese in California, many organizations were formed, among them the KENJIN KAI, composed of groups of Japanese from the same village or prefecture. Their purpose was to promote social intercourse,

lend assistance to newly-arrived immigrants, provide relief for needy Japanese and organize a system of mutual help in financial matters. The first of these societies was formed about the year 1900 in San Francisco.

On the other hand, in Japan, about 1918, the various prefectures began to organize overseas societies, known as KAIGAI KYOKAI, in order to encourage colonization, especially in Latin America. The Japanese prefectures which provided the largest number of immigrants were HIROSHIMA, WAKAYAMA, KUMAMOTO, OKAYAMA and YAMAGUCHI; hence, these were the first to organize.

Later, as contact grew between the KAIGAI KYOKAI in Japan and the KENJIN KAI in America, the latter in some cases changed their name to KAIGAI KYOKAI SHIBU (SHIBU meaning "branch") and in others formed a committee bearing that name. These changes were accompanied by the addition of new activity, mainly to encourage former Japanese residents to return to the United States. Some parts of our country saw the formation of new KENJIN KAI for this purpose, and the related organizations in Japan have done much to stimulate their growth.

In general, the activities of these societies appear to be confined to the social field. However, because of their great number and extensive membership they constitute an ideal target for Japanese propaganda. During the past few years large numbers of propagandists from Japan have addressed the memberships of these societies as part of Tokyo's program of "cultural propaganda and enlightenment". The effect of these efforts has been demonstrated by the sizable contributions made by each of these organizations to the Japanese War Chest, in some cases transmitted through the Consulate General in San Francisco and in others handed personally by individuals to the governors of the respective native provinces in Japan. For this demonstration of loyalty to the home of their ancestors, letters of appreciation were sent by the highest Japanese Government officials to the presidents of the various associations in this country. In this connection, these societies have for many years maintained contact with the Imperial Japanese Government through the Overseas Departments of their home districts.

An indication of the official Japanese attitude toward these groups may be gathered from the fact that, during 1941, many of them received from Premier Prince KONOYE letters of thanks for their participation in the 2600th Anniversary Celebration ceremonies held in Tokyo.

In conjunction with this celebration, a convention of some 2,000 Japanese subjects living abroad was held between November 4 and 9, 1940. The chief delegates from the United States were, for the most part, representatives of overseas and regional societies. Many of them had, in addition, such known subversive affiliations as memberships in the HOKUBEI HEIEKI GIMUSHA KAI, NIPPON KAIGUN KYOKAI, SAKURA KAI, and ownership or editorship of pro-Japanese (Japanese language) newspapers on the West Coast.

Despite the fact that the convention, resulting in the formation of the powerful OVERSEAS JAPANESE CENTRAL SOCIETY, appeared to be a spontaneous popular movement, it is definitely known that the whole procedure was an official undertaking of the Japanese Government.

In April, 1941, a meeting of representatives of Japanese newspapers published abroad was announced by the OVERSEAS JAPANESE CENTRAL SOCIETY to develop more fully the immigration policies of the Japanese Government. Those policies, it might be noted, have been the same for many years, and can, in fact, be succinctly stated as: Colonization and still more Colonization -- on the continents of North and South America and all of Oceania, looking forward to the "Greater Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere".

Prior to the outbreak of hostilities, and in view of the strained relations between the United States and Japan, many Japanese organizations began to de-emphasize their ties with the homeland, including, in some cases, changes of names to those less offensive to the American public, elimination of drives for war relief funds and the passage of resolutions defining their activities in terms of purely American interests. In October, 1941, for example, the Los Angeles Japanese press announced that forty delegates from the HIROSHIMA prefectural societies of various West Coast cities met at Los Angeles under the sponsorship of the local Japanese Chamber of Commerce and passed resolutions as follows:

(1) To provide assistance in marriages and employment of Nisei.

(2) To maintain contact with the mother country and exchange correspondence on conditions at home and abroad.

(3) To employ HIROSHIMA prefectural society's publication, "GEIBI", as a liaison organ (probably between the various branches of this society in the United States).

(4) To curtail activities contrary to U.S. Government policy and to subscribe, as much as possible, to U.S. National Defense Bonds.

Subsequent to the outbreak of war, the West Coast Japanese press announced that a number of Japanese organizations, including those of the prefectural type, had voluntarily disbanded. In addition, some of them are reported to have donated various sums of money for Defense Bonds, to the Red Cross and to ambulance funds.

In conclusion, it appears that this type of society, in itself, is not of such a nature that it should be forcibly disbanded as subversive. However, the members constitute a group upon whom definite suspicion falls. This is based on the facts that (1) they have been subjected to a considerable amount of pro-Japanese propaganda over a period of years, (2) the percentage of aliens in their membership is high, (3) they have shown their sympathy for the policies of Japan through their financial support of that government's expansionist program, (4) they maintain contact with the Japanese Government, and (5) some of their high officials are known dangerous individuals, now under custodial detention.

December 30th, 1940

Dear Mr. Rogers:

Of late the Japan-America Society lunchloans have taken a turn for the better, according to members of long standing. Formerly programs at these affairs consisted of little more than hands-across-the-sea oratory, and the attendance was far from crushing, even though this organization is about the one common link of such divergent American groups as nisei, church-going missionaries, and club-going businessmen. Now that its meetings are coming to be used as diplomatic sounding boards, interest in the society is perking up.

Ambassador Grew initiated the change, when on his return from America last Fall, he startled the gathering with the outspoken statements, which as you may recall, reverberated here and at home for a considerable time. Since then members and their friends (I still am of the latter class) have been waiting hopefully for another blow off. When it came on the 19th of this month, there was no need for any inside tips: with Mr. Matsuoka, Japan's Foreign Minister, Admiral Nomura, the new Ambassador to the United States, and Mr. Horinouchi who has just returned from Washington, all as speakers, and Ambassador Grew as toastmaster, more could be expected than camaraderie.

The only true speech was that delivered by Mr. Matsuoka, for Horinouchi and Nomura kept their remarks on conventional lines. The Foreign Minister's talk, in my opinion, showed the effect of careful preparation by some able interpreters of America in his department. I was unable to lay finger on any new thesis or approach on running through the newspaper version, but the reading confirmed my impression of the time, that a pretty good job of case pleading had been done. As a good political argument should do, it steered away from some of the contentious current issues and worked from a safe, long-range perspective in dealing with Japan's national objectives.

Matsuoka's delivery was top-notch, to the contrary of what I had been told to expect. His slight accent was not at all unpleasant, and his speaking technique pointed to some good training back at Oregon Law School. Alongside the portly Admiral and our Ambassador, he looked very much the tiny, forceful Japanese so typified in the American imagination.

Nevertheless, in my personal award, the laurel for the day went not to him but to our Ambassador, for his brief remarks at the end of the speech. Mr. Grew's position as toastmaster naturally precluded a speech of refutation, and he did not attempt it. Still, through the use of the chair's right of comment, he did as adroit and diplomatic a job of dusting off as it has been my opportunity to see. In an extremely polite yet convincing manner, he took America's exceptions to Japan's appeal for the New Order.

=== Page 16 === (confidence 0.99)
My feeling at the time was that his rejoinder was far too smooth to be the product of the moment, and surely had been worked out and perhaps blessed in advance by the State Department, as is the usual practice with important statements. Yet my embassy friends assure me that it actually was an extemporaneous rendition, and that Mr. Grew himself later was trying to recall exactly what he had said. I know that you, as a friend of his, would have enjoyed the affair immensely.

Thank you for your Christmas cable. The day itself went rather quietly in making calls, running through some of the book purchases in Shanghai, and in sorting out ski equipment for the New Year holidays. I'm slipping off this time at Akakura, a resort on a mountain slope within sight of Lake Nojiri, and intend to remain there until the 8th, when the tutors resume work.

Sincerely,

Tom Blykemoe

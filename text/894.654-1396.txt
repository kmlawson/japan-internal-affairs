JUNE 7, 1938
Mr. WELLES

In the course of a meeting held today in the War Department, at which representatives of E. I. duPont de Nemours who had recently returned from the Far East described their impressions, an officer of the duPont plant in Wilmington stated that the Japanese had been trying for sometime to buy the duPont synthetic rubber process. In spite of the fact that rubber made by this process costs about $0.75 a pound as compared with $0.11 or $0.12 a pound for natural rubber the Japanese are apparently desirous of using the artificial product exclusively in order to make themselves independent of imports from British possessions. They informed duPont that they had $1,000,000 in the bank in New York which they would be willing to turn over immediately in payment of this process. duPont, however, refused to sell.

The duPont representative who gave this information added that one of the Japanese engineers who had conducted the negotiations had admitted that he had previously endeavored to acquire detailed information in regard to the German process for the production of synthetic rubber but had been unable to do so.

C. W. Yost.

I have the honor to report that Mr. Carl H. Boehringer, Assistant Commercial Attache, was informed by General Wang Peng-sheng, the Generalissimo's adviser on Japanese affairs, that the Japanese have found several new uses for rubber.

According to General Wang, the Japanese have been
structed a specially built type of rubber ski boot which can be used for skiing without the regular wooden skis. Last winter the Japanese held extensive maneuvers of troops and young men soon to be drafted who were equipped with the new type of ski boots. The maneuvers were held in Hokkaido, Niigata, Tohoku, Aomori and other areas in northern Japan. The ski sticks used by these troops can be used as tripods for light machine guns. Troops in Manchuria are also equipped with these new ski outfits, General Wang said. He had no detailed information as to how the boots are made.

The Japanese are also making another type of rubber boot, built along the lines of the ordinary Mongolian boot, but with soles very thick and having large air pockets. General Wang's agents have seen these new boots and are now trying to secure samples. He was at a loss to explain the reason for the thick soles with air pockets and Mr. Boehringer suggested that they might possibly be designed for purposes of insulation against the cold or for use by parachute troops since the boots might be shock absorbent. General Wang was interested in both of these possible explanations. Colonel Morris B. DePass, Jr., the Embassy's Military Attache and Military Attache for Air, later declared that these explanations are reasonable. The thickness of the soles of the boots General Wang indicated with his fingers as being between 1 1/2 to 2 inches thick. If samples are eventually secured an effort will be made to inspect or obtain one of them for transmission to the Department.

General Wang also stated higher Japanese air force officers in Manchuria are equipped with rubber garments

ECONOMIC WARFARE DIVISION, AMERICAN EMBASSY

# MEMORANDUM

July 28, 1943

## Re Japanese Rubber

On the 20th of July, Mr. Allison and I conferred with Mrs. Cotton of the MEW and Mr. Morton, formerly of the Dunlop Tire Company. From 1923 to 1940, Mr. Morton was with the Dunlop Tire Company in Japan. When he left Japan in 1940, he had been for some time head of the factory. The Dunlop factory at Kobe had a peak processing capacity of eight hundred tons of rubber per month in 1941. Its peak output was six hundred tons per month. It manufactured a full line of rubber goods.

Its chief competitor was the Bridgestone Company, which was capable of processing between four and five hundred tons per month. This concern made excellent tires, and had a competent staff of technicians. From 1936 to 1939, its output increased one hundred percent.

The difficulties arising in acquiring naphtha began in 1934 and became acute in 1937. Most of this naphtha was acquired from the Rising Sun Oil Company, a Dutch Shell subsidiary. Mr. Winter of this organization has a greater knowledge of the naphtha situation in Japan than anyone else in this country.

The carbon black situation also grew difficult during the 30's. When Mr. Morton was in England on leave, he went to Budapest to observe the operation of a machine for the recovery of activated carbon. This machine permitted the recovery of from 85-90%, and he made an attempt to purchase one for use at his factory in Japan. He eventually succeeded in having one sent to Japan, but the machine was held up for four months by the Japanese customs and demands were made by the officials for blue prints for the machine. While negotiations were going on for the release of the machine, the Dunlop Company was approached by the Sumitomo Cable Company which stated that it could supply activated carbon direct. It was discovered that they were using a similar machine, based on a German design, principally in connection with their acetone factories.

However, the Japanese had developed a substitute termed "hakuenka." Hakuenka consists of cross carbonate plus carbon dioxide in an emulsion of stearic acid. Mr. Somerville, of the Vanderbilt Corporation, a

/concern

concern with offices in New York City and engaged in making chemicals for the rubber trade, knows more than anyone else about hakusaka and a similar product which the Vanderbilt Corporation merchandises under the name of "Haleto." Zinc oxide had been used as a filler with carbon black, but it was discovered that by the use of hakusaka the zinc oxide was not required, and the imports of this product into Japan decreased considerably. They tried to make carbon black from creosote, and it proved to be a good filler, but a bad agar. The mixture of lamp black, hakusaka, with a small amount of carbon black proved to be quite satisfactory.

In 1938 to 1939, the Japanese sought to force the Dunlop Company to use a considerable amount of reclaimed rubber in the manufacture of tires. Dunlop threatened to refuse to do so, but finally capitulated. When the Government cut their quota of natural rubber, Dunlop developed an automobile tire using 25% reclaimed and a bicycle tire made completely from reclaimed. To Mr. Morton's "horror" the latter tire was just as good as one made from natural rubber. Finally, Dunlop reached the point where they used 30% reclaimed rubber in the regular treads manufactured by them, and it was discovered that tires made in this way were able to obtain over 90% of the mileage of the ordinary tires made entirely of natural rubber.

Japan was stocking tremendous piles of reclaimed rubber during this period. Most of this rubber was purchased from Mulhsteines in New York City, who should be interviewed. This not only gave Japan stock piles of rubber, but also gave her substantial piles of carbon black, contained in the reclaimed. The Dunlop Company was forced to bring up its capacity for processing reclaimed rubber from 5 to 11 tons a day. Apparently this same procedure was followed in other rubber factories in Japan, for it was noticed that after the increase in the use of reclaimed rubber the imports of carbon black were cut by some 30%. It is believed, therefore, that Japan can overcome any deficiency in carbon black.

Japan built up huge stock-piles of hydrochloric acid.

While on fishing trips, Mr. Morton observed a large chemical plant, marked "C" on the attached Exhibit A. He first saw the plant in 1936, and recognized it as one suitable for the manufacture of nitric acid, sulphuric acid and other similar chemical products. Subsequently, from 1936 to 1939 the plant had been increased by 50% and was still growing. Mr. Morton described it as being as large as the Lever Brothers near London. He also observed during this same period a large plant which is identified as "R" on Exhibit A. His chauffeur, whom he believes, told him it was a rayon plant.

Mr. Gansmoe, honorary Norwegian Consul General, told Mr. Morton that most of the wood pulp for Japanese rayon plants was purchased from Pacific Mills, Ltd. (Pacific Mills, Ltd., is a wholly owned subsidiary of the Crown-Zellerbach Corporation, whose head offices

The chemical plant and the rayon plant are believed to be the two largest structures in the general vicinity of Mount Hiei.

Mr. Morton is firmly convinced, and apparently for sound reasons, that there is no serious limitations to Japan's ability to make tire manufacture machinery, and that her capacity to produce additional tires is tremendous. He said, for example, that Japan could have doubled her output of tires in the two years following Pearl Harbor, in so far as tire output depends upon equipment for the manufacture of tires.

Further, a former superintendent of this shop, a Japanese named Murakanei, in 1938 left Dunlop and established his own factory for the manufacture of tire-making machinery. His assistant, Nikinya, had left Dunlop in 1937 to get the new plant organized. It is called the Tokio Roll Company, and is located at Eichi-no-tani, outside Kobe.

In Mr. Morton's view, there were as many technically trained people competent to manufacture tire-making machinery in Japan as there are in England. He explained this by saying that also there was not as large a market for tire-making machinery in Japan as in this country, but a much larger percentage of the Japanese workers were capable of performing supervisory work than is the case with English workers.

As an illustration of Japanese tire manufacturing capacity, Mr. Morton said that in 1935 Dunlop purchased two 60 inch mills with reduction gears and motors, for £8000 in England. Subsequently in 1937, he purchased two from Minato in Japan for £3000, and in his view Japanese mills were better than the British. Further, the Japanese mills had a safety device to protect the hands of the workmen feeding material into the rollers, which was vastly superior

so that on the British mill, because the latter simply stopped the rollers, while the Japanese device reversed them, thus rejecting the hand and arm of the feeder.

According to Mr. Morton the four main rubber companies in Japan are as follows:

1. Dunlop Rubber Company, Kobe; capacity of from 600 tons of rubber a month

2. Bridgestone Rubber Company, Kurume (Kyushu); capacity approximately 500 tons a month

3. Yokohama Rubber Company, Tokyo; Mr. Morton did not give the capacity of this Company, but stated that relatively it was No. 3 in the production of tires and No. 1 in the production of belting.

4. Sumitomo Cable Company, Osaka; no figures as to capacity here available.

In his view, lack of proper cotton is the limiting factor upon the production of tires in Japan, if there is a limiting factor keeping tire production below requirements. However, Mr. Morton said that Dunlop had developed a process and a formula for the treatment of rayon as the substitute for cotton in the production of tires which had not been put into use at the time of the outbreak of the Pacific War. While instructions had been given that this formula should not fall into the hands of the Japanese, it is not known definitely whether or not they did succeed in obtaining it. Up to the outbreak of the Pacific War the Japanese had been unable to treat rayon properly, but, if they should have succeeded in obtaining this formula, they may have overcome this limitation by now.

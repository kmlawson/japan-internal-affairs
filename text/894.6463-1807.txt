Dear Mr. Secretary:

During the time that the Shanghai Power Company was operated under the military control of the Japanese Government severe damage and loss of property was experienced, particularly in the steam electric generating station.

During this period one complete unit was dismantled and removed. Another was damaged by bombs to such an extent that a major part of it may have to be entirely abandoned. A third unit was damaged so severely that it cannot be repaired and put back into service for approximately one year. A survey and estimate is now being made of the damage suffered and a reparation claim will be filed by the Shanghai Power Company.

In the meantime, however, because of the damage suffered, it is expected that in the very near future the Shanghai Power Company generating capacity will be insufficient to meet the needs of the territory which it is required to serve by the Chinese authorities, and the Company is investigating the possibility of obtaining additional steam electric generating equipment at as early a date as possible.

My dear Mr. Robertson:

Your letter of December 10, 1945 addressed to the Secretary of State has been referred to this Division. The Department of State regrets to inform you that at present the transfer from Japan of one or more 25,000 KW complete steam electric units can not be authorized.

At present all Japanese capital equipment, except that subject to restitution claims, is being held to meet reparations claims and none is available for sale. As yet no Allied authority has been set up to allocate Japanese equipment on reparations account. Pending its establishment, the creation of United States Government machinery whereby Japanese equipment will be made available on reparations account to American concerns is being held in abeyance. An announcement will be made as soon as such deliveries become possible.

SUBJECT:

This despatch forwards a memo from Mizutani, Minister of Commerce &amp; Industry, to ESS, SCAP, concerning delays in the rehabilitation of Japan's power generating plants. As the despatch indicates, Mizutani's memo seems to have a three-fold purpose:

1) To refute criticisms by the Strike Mission concerning delayed reconstruction of Japanese electrical power facilities.

2) To ascribe most of such delays as do occur to the obstructive tactics of labor unions and thereby to recommend that special SCAP-Jap Govt. inspection teams be sent to generating plants "to improve the laborers' will to work".

3) To request additional allocations of coal for use in thermal generating plants.

It is evident that Mizutani is attempting to pass the buck to the labor unions. The memo is another example of how Japanese Government officials rely on interference by SCAP to solve their problems.

I have the honor to enclose copy of a memorandum informally presented to the Chief of the Economic and Scientific Section of this Headquarters by Mr. MIZUTANI, Chosaburo, Minister of Commerce and Industry, on December 24, 1947. The memorandum concerns problems confronting the electrical power generating industry and, according to an official of the Commerce and Industry Ministry, represents an attempt to refute allegedly critical statements made by representatives of Overseas Consultants, Incorporated, concerning delayed rehabilitation and repair of Japanese electrical power generating facilities. The latter are understood to have stated that a number of electrical power plants are operating at only one-seventh to one-eighth of total generating capacity.

In the memorandum, obstructive tactics of some labor unions are described as one of the important causes contributing to the delay in repairing power plants. Labor organizations are said to be employing a "business-strike" to restrict company communications and to prevent the transmittal of business reports to company management. It is stated that unions are demanding "democratization of the electrical industry", a "sliding-scale" wage system (in which wages are raised in proportion to living costs), and extra rations of food and clothing. 1. Rehabilitation and Repairing Work for Power Generation Plants.

With the exception of a few plants, repairing work for hydro-power generation plants damaged by flood or other causes, rehabilitation of war damaged thermal power generation plants and supplementary construction work to restore output of plants, have been put, though delayed a little, on the right track and are making considerable progress. However, due to the tactics adopted by labours unions for workers of electric industry, the rehabilitation or repairing work are forced to be delayed. More concretely, these labour unions resorting to strategy of developing the struggle in local chapters and they are meeting everyday during working hours at each plant, etc., for the purpose of demanding tentative revised allowances, ration, in arrear, of fatigues, socks, gloves, and additional rice ration for workers or under the pretence of inefficient delivery of materials. Under these circumstances, any little hitch incurred at the thermal power generation plants has come to be unable to be repaired speedily, greatly affecting daily output. Prerequisite to speeding up rehabilitation or repairing work of power generation plants is to increase workers will to work. To achieve this, it is deemed most effective to dispatch to plants inspection teams, composed of G.H.Q. staff and Japanese authorities, such as were dispatched for coal mining. But as a matter of fact all out inspection can not be conducted for electric industry because the number of plants are so many including large number of minor factories. However, in view of the present status of progress of rehabilitation work, if an inspection team is sent to such plants as the First Plant, Inawashiro or Yamazato which are being utilized by labour unions through a handful of extreme leftists to agitate labour disputes, the inspection would bring remarkably good results. Hitherto this industry was given inferior treatment, for the promotion of construction or repairing work, with respect to accommodation of funds, procurement of materials and daily necessities for workers, but it was decided lately at the Cabinet meeting that this industry be given priority next to that given to coal mining. So it is expected that the prevailing situation will be gradually improved.

2. Coal for Power Generation.

With the low water season approaching special attention should be paid, in order to tide over the present demand and supply crisis of power, to the problem regarding coal for thermal power generation. Appendix

(A) Dispute of Electric Industry Workers' Unions

(Abbreviation: E. I. W. U.)

The "E.I.W.U." is an integrate Union Organized by all employees of the Japan Power Generation and Transmission Co. and all the Power Supply Cos. throughout the country, the membership totalling about 100,000, belonging to the Japan Congress of Industrial Organization. The "E.I.W.U." started its activity in April 1946 as a federation unifying all labor unions of the said companies and at the 1946 October offensive of the Japan Congress of Industrial Organization, made a vigorous campaign under the leadership of the J.C.I.O., pushing the following demands: (1) Introduction of minimum wage system (2) Democratization of electric enterprise (3) Establishment of retirement allowance system, but at the close of 1947 an interim agreement was reached between the "E.I.U.W." and the management on the basis of a mediation plan of the Central Labor Relations Committee.

Taking into consideration the experience of this campaign, the executive staff of the "E.I.W.U." became inclined to change the existing structure of the "E.I.W.U." into an integrate union Organization and finally re-organized it as such in April this year.

Since then it was increasingly strengthening activities, centering efforts on establishment of "Sliding wage system" for the time being. The recent characteristic of its activity is to avoid any negotiations with the management at the central and to make a struggle at the locality where every local union under the "E.I.W.U." leadership exists, which tactics has been dictated by the J.C.I.O.

Namely, in September this year, the "E.I.W.U.", while introducing the following three demands before the Central Labor Relations Committee: (1) Democratization of electric enterprise (2) Sliding wage system (3) Conclusion of labor agreement, presented a demand to the management concerned at every locality where an union branch exists, concerning an interim measure for "sliding wage system" and has been struggling for it to date. Its struggle method is given below, threatening a smooth operation of electric enterprise. As to the appeal to the Central Labor Relations Committee, a mediation draft was made public to labor and management on 18 December and both sides are now scrutinizing it. Answers from both sides are expected by 10th January next year.

At present in the locality of the Japan Power Generation and Transmission Co. and in almost all the localities of the Power Supply Companies, the so-called "Business strike" is prevailing. The contents of business strikes are: (1) Suspension of any communications with the company management. (2) Suspension of all desk work (except those

Before the cut-off of power from North Korea on 14 May 1948, South Korea was using an average of 100,000 kilowatts of electricity, of which about 60,000 kilowatts came from north of the 38th parallel. Production of power in South Korea was stopped up about 50% after 14 May, and production during the last half of May and the month of June has averaged about 60,000 kilowatts. With the completion of repairs on the Chong Pyong and Sum Jin River hydroelectric plants during the first half of July, it is anticipated that power output can be stepped up to an average of about 75,000 kilowatts during the remainder of July and in August, when sufficient water is expected to be available for near-capacity operation. However, hydroelectric-power output must be expected to drop sharply during the fall and winter because of insufficient water. Continuing efforts are being made to rehabilitate and expand existing thermal-generation facilities in South Korea in order to insure maximum possible power during the fall and winter months when power requirements are the heaviest.

A-355, December 12, 1949

Following news item released December 12, 1949, by Public Information Office, General Headquarters, (U.S.) Far East Command, Tokyo, under heading "SCAP approves Counterpart Fund Loan:"

"A Counterpart Fund loan to the Nippon Hassoden Co., Ltd., amounting to 1.834 billion Yen has been given SCAP approval, the economic and scientific section announced today.

"Of this total 1.480 billion Yen is ready for immediate release with the balance to be made available next quarter. This is the first of a series of Counterpart Fund loans expected to be made to the electric power industry.

"Nippon Hassoden will use the proceeds of the loan to finance the construction of the projects. Five of these will be hygro electric generating plants with an aggregate maximum capacity of 27,000 kilowatts; two will be steam generating plants with an aggregated capacity of 70,000 kilowatts. The remaining projects will provide 122 kilometers of badly needed transmission lines.

"Increased population and the scarcity and high cost of other fuels in Japan have created an acute demand for electric power. In view of these factors and the low cost of hydro power, the Counterpart Fund loans to the electric utility industry are regarded as a constructive step in the rebuilding of Japanese economy.

"Counterpart Fund loans are made only to those companies making an essential contribution to the Japanese economy who are unable to finance their capital needs from private sources. The Nippon Hassoden loan is in conformity with these policies. The interest rate is seven and one half percent per annum with repayment to be completed by March 31, 1980."

SABALD

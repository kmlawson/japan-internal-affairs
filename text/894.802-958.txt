PROMINENCE OF THE SUBSIDY QUESTION.
With the convocation of the World Economic
Conference at London this month the attention of
every government has been focussed on all forms of
artificial stimulants to trade and shipping. The
statements of the President of the British Board of
Trade, Mr. Walter Runciman, and the Chancellor of
the Exchequer, Mr. Neville Chamberlain, during the
first week of the Conference leave no doubt as to
the serious concern with which Great Britain, at
least, regards the question of shipping subsidies.
Particularly is Great Britain exercised over the
subsidies and subventions provided the merchant
marine of the United States and the merchant marine
of Japan by their respective governments. Straws
in the wind of British shipping opinion may be seen
in the fulminations of the President of the Orient
Line, of high officials of the Penninsular and
Oriental Steam Navigation Company (induced by loss
of passenger traffic on the Australasian run to the
magnificent new liners of the Oceanic (Matson)
Steamship Company). The resolutions of the British
Marine Exchange and Liverpool Shipowners' Society
leave no doubt that English owners are united in
their determination to "do something" about ship
subsidies paid to their competitors on the trade
routes of the world. Whether something will be done
remains to be seen; but at least the question of ship
subsidies is on the agenda of the World Economic
Conference as well as of the International Chamber
of

I.
SHIP BUILDING SUBSIDIES.
The Ship Construction Law Effective October 1, 1932.
Toward the end of September, 1932 the special
session of the Imperial Japanese Diet passed the
first legislation for ship construction bounties
since l909. For sixteen years no shipbuilding
subsidies had been paid by the Japanese Government.
The decision, therefore to inaugurate a two year
plan for the renovation of the merchant marine
was highly significant. It was motivated by
l. The incubus of a large fleet of obsolete
vessels, the presence of which disturbed
charter and freight rates; impeded the
construction of new ships and acted as
a drag to the Japanese merchant marine.
2. The necessity for faster and more economical
vessels to enter the competitive trade routes
of the world.
3. The naval value of a fleet of new, fast
merchant vessels -- a value made more
evident by unsettled international
conditions affecting Japan.
4. The industrial and strategic value of
maintaining private shipyards in production.
To meet the objectives outlined above the
Imperial Diet enacted legislation which within two
years would provide 200,000 gross tons of new
vessels and at the same time would remove 400,000
gross tons of obsolete ships from Japanese register.
Tho

-6-
The new vessels would be constructed under the
supervision of the Ministries of Communications
and Navy. An incentive to extra speed was offered
in the promise of higher bounties for extra knots.
Specifically, the terms of the new law provided:
1. The ships to be scrapped should be over
twenty-five years of age and over 1,000
tons gross.
2. For each new ton built shipping companies
were to scrap two tons of obsolete vessels.
3. The new vessels constructed should be cargo
ships of not less than 4,000 gross tons and
13) knots speed. The subsidy should be
45 per ton for ships of less than 14 knots
and ¥46 per ton for 14 knots, increasing by
1 per half knot, to ¥54 per ton for 18
knots and over.
4. Half of this subsidy should be paid at the
time the vessel was laid down and half upon
its commission for sea. However, this
second installment of the subsidy would
not be paid until the outmoded vessels to
be scrapped had actually been placed in the
hands of the breakers.
5. All new construction should of course take
place in national yards and all materials,
fittings and engines should be of Japanese
construction.
6. Vessels built under the terms of the subsidy
should not be disposed of or rebuilt without (2)
the approval of the Minister of Communications.
To make effective the terms of this law the Diet
(3)
appropriated 11,000,000.
Although the administration of the new law was
vested in the Ministry of Communications, there was
created, under the auspices of that department, a
quasi-official, quasi-private organization to assist
in earrying out the Government's program. This body
was known as the Senshitsu Kaizen Kyokai or Shipping
Standard Improvement Association. It combined the
triangular

(1) Adjustment of demand and supply of vessels

(2) Establishment of a subsidy scheme for each year.
(3) (Formulation of a) Programme for scrapping and

(6) (To) Watch the observance of the various terms

(7) (To supervise) Collective bargaining in the

(8) Prevention of the importation of foreign vessels.
(9) Adjustment of the price of breaking-up vessels.
(l0) To afford facilities to shipowners for raising

It must be noted that the new merchant fleet
resulting from the Government's subsidy policy has
characteristics which make it unique. Every one of
the new vessels contracted for, so far as can be
ascertained, is diesel driven. Most of the new ships
are of large size, ranging from 7,000 to 10,000 gross
tons. Most of them are fast. There are at least a
half-dozen of these motor freighters which are designed
for a speed of eighteen knots or over, ranking them
in the class of most of the great diesel mail liners
of the world as regards swiftness. There are no
freighters under the American flag which can sustain
a sea speed equal to that of these Japanese cargo
ships, if they meet contract requirements on their
trials. There are few freighters in any merchant
marine which can approximate 18 knots. The advantage
the new vessels will have in the silk run to the
United States, either to New York via Panama or to
the West Coast, is obvious. The strategic importance
of such ships as naval auxiliaries, combining as they
do the qualities of high speed and large carrying
capacity, is equally obvious. It may be said that
the shipbuilding subsidy program of the Japanese
Government has resulted in a fleet of the fastest
freighters in the world.

At the same time the ship construction law is
reducing the amount of over-age tonnage laid up.
Of the total of approximately 700,000 gross tons
of vessels over twenty-five years of age in Japan
as of August, 1932 (ercluding cannery vessels and
(8)
Government ships) 400,000 tons will be scrapped
under the terms of the new legislation.
As for tonnage laid up, as distinguished from
tonnage more than 25 years old, Japan has 426 vessels
laid up, of which only 29 are over 1,000 tons register,
representing 158,700 of the total of 181,600 tons
now idle. As compared with this figure, the United
States Shipping Board announced that on April 1, 1933
of privately-owned vessels alone in the United States
there were 405 laid up, aggregating a gross tonnage
(9)
of 1,903,112.
While 20% of the world's merchant
marine is now idle, only 6% of the Japanese mercantile
fleet is laid up. This compares with 29% for France,
28% for Great Britain, 26% for Holland, 23% for
Germany, 22% for Sweden, 20% for Norway and 18% for
(10)
Italy.
Approximately a quarter of the privately-
owned American merchant marine was laid up on April
1 of this year.

In the first place, the Kokusai Kisen Kaisha

of such low speed and irregular service as to exclude
them from the "Ordered Services" of the Ministry of
Communications. Secondly, the Kokusai Kisen Kaisha
is in a sense partly owned by the Japanese Government.
Of $16,l85,000 invested in domestic enterprises up to

Kisen Kaisha, equal to the actual value of the entire
(14)

would almost be paying itself should service subsidies
be granted.
The situation of the Dairen Kisen Kaisha is
likewise peculiar in that it is a subsidiary of the
South Manchuria Railway. The South Manchuria Railway
is in part owned by the Japanese Government. Here
also the payment of subsidies would be approximate
to taking money out of one pocket and placing it in
another. Furthermore, the Dairen Kisen Kaisha has

COMPARATIVE SUBSIDIES.
In conclusion it is appropriate to review the
aggregate service subsidies paid by the Japanese
Government in comparison with those granted by other
governments. It is common talk among shipping people
that every Japanese line is heavily subsidized and
that the ability of even the most humble tramp to
quote lower rates than foreign lines is due to
financial support from the Government. The representa-
tives in Kobe of the great transpacific steamship
companies are united in the conviction that in
competing with Japanese lines they are bucking the
economic power of the Empire expressed through
subsidies. Everyone is sure of this general thesis,
but no one knows the actual amounts of service
subsidy paid. The writer himself had the notion,
on commencing this study, that Japanese shipping
companies were among the most highly subsidized in
the world. It is therefore amazing to note that,
contrary to the popular impression, Japanese service
subsidies at current exchange are about one-tenth of
the annual subventions paid by the United States

THE FUTURE OF SUBSIDIES IN JAPAN.
The Japanese Government has been convinced by
the experience of four wars of the necessit of
maintaining a merchant marine large enough not only
to move troops overseas but to maintain the maritime
lines of communication essential to the prosecution
of any successful campaign. An island Empire, the
segments of the nation are linked by ships rather
than rails. The maintenance of the sea services
assumes a degree of importance not felt in continental
nations. The people are "ship conscious" and behind
the Government in solicitude for the proper maintenance
of the merchant fleet. Since the war with China in
l894 the national government has followed a policy of
state aid to shipping. Construction bounties and
service subsidies have been paid since the very
emergence of Japan as a modern sea power. In view
of this background it is not to be supposed that
Japan will align itself with Great Britain and Norway
in combating ship subsidies. The success of the new
ship-construction law in removing the load of obsolete
vessels and at the same time providing a fleet of the
fastest cargo ships on earth is not calculated to
influence either the Government or the nation to take
the opposite course advocated by the maritime nations
which oppose subsidies. The fact that Japanese
shipping now occupies a more favorable position in
relation to tonnage laid up than any other nation
provides

Shanghai adventures makes the`Government more
concerned than ever for the maintenance of the
commercial fleet. Finally, the present wave of
anti-British sentiment sweeping the country, although
induced by the abrogation of trade treaties in Africa
and India and the increase of tariffs in India and
British Malaya, will not induce the Japanese
representatives at the world economic conference
to see eye-to-eye with Great Britain in the matter
of subsidies.
The conclusion must be reached, therefore, --
and it is supported by the evidence of national
opinion given in the Press -- that Japan is as firm
a believer in the efficacy and value of ship subsidies
as ever, and that no change in national policy in this
respect will be made in the slightest degree.

"World Economic Conference And
Shipping Agreement.
"The British delegates, Mr. Neville Chamberlain,
Chancellor of the Exchequer, and Mr. Walter Runciman,
President of the Board of Trade, emphasized the
necessity of the establishment of an international
agreement for the abolition or reduction of shipping
subsidies in their speeches at the plenary sessions
of the World Economic Conference. We may accordingly
expect that numerous questions in the shipping circles
will be brought into the present conference.
"As for the reason, Great Britain contends that
the British loss accruing from "freightage dumping"
by the Powers is alarming. She charges the other
Governments' heavy subsidies with being responsible
for the decline of the British shipping industry.
As a matter of fact, the British freight receipts
have fallen from  200,000,000 to  60,000,000. As
much as 28 per cent of the British ships are tied
up: 36 per cent of the British seamen are jobless.
Because of this sombre outlook, Britain has made it
known that she will be obliged to have recourse to
retaliatory action if the British proposal is
rejected.
"Calculating the Japanese annual shipping
subsidies of  10,000,000 as 100, the Powers line
up in the following order: the United States 560
( 28,000,000); Ita1y, 264 (258,000,000 1ire);
France, 213 (278,000,000 francs); and Britain, 70
(5 700,000). Britain thus ranks the lowest.
According to the present exchange rate, however,
the amount of the British and Japanese shipping
subsidies is about the same, whereas America, which
tops the list, is firmly opposed to the abolition
or reduction of her subsidy to marine transportation.
"Far from the abolition, the United States is
rather desirous of increasing her subsidy.
"In this connection it must be borne in mind
that the Japanese mail subsidy is limited to the
regular routes indispensable for our trade, unlike
the aggressive subsidies of the other Powers, and
therefore does not fall within the category Britain
wants abolished. However, since Britain and the
United States, the two great shipping nations, are
divided on the issue of the abolition of subsidies,
not to mention the divergent purposes of the nations
giving subsidies, the establishment of an inter-
national agreement is certainly not an easy task.

=== Page 30 === (confidence 0.99)

"As for the ship construction subsidy, it is
a glaring contradiction to encourage ship building
with a subsidy on the one hand, while suffering
from an increasing number of tied-up vessels on the
other. In order to adjust the excess of ships, it
is necessary to arrive at an international agreement
on the suspension of the ship building subsidy.
The suspension of this subsidy should be aimed at
only the prevention of the increase of the number
of ships. But a country like Japan, which is
endeavouring to replenish her merchant fleet by
the construction of 200,000 tons of new vessels,
scrapping 400,000 tons of old vessels, must be
dealt with differently from the others.
"The proposition for a joint tie-up at a uniform
percentage of the global tonnage of the merchant
marine of each nation is equally unfair to Japan.
The existing percentages of the tied-up ships are
29 for France, 28 for Britain, 23 for Germany, 18
for Italy, and 6 for Japan. These figures are due
to the different conditions of the shipping industry
in these nations. It is unbearable for Japan, which
ranks third in global tonnage, to sacrifice her
shipping industry under such a formula.
"The most important prerequisite to the
rehabilitation of international shipping is,
therefore, the removal of the tariff walls and
the establishment of the principle of liberty of
trade. When trade is restored and the equilibrium
between the number of vessels and the volume of
freight is reestablished, the other questions will
naturally solve themselves. It was quite timely
that the conference of the International Chambers
of Commerce submitted to the World Economic
Conference, a resolution urging the freedom of the
sea, the abolition of discrimination against ships,
and the end of the anti-foreign ship boycott.
"Notwithstanding the mutual desire for the
promotion of the international trade through the
means above mentioned, we are told that Britain is
threatening the conferees in the World Economic
Conference with the effecting of a powerful British
economic bloc, shutting foreign ships from coastal
trade in the British Empire, unless the Powers agree
to the British proposal. In that event, the recovery
of the international trade is but a dream, even if
the Powers reach an accord as regards the removal
of the tariff wall and the freedom of trade.
"Under the circumstances, Japan must strive for
the world shipping industry's rehabilitation at the
World Eoonomic Conference in collaboration with the
United States and the other nations."
(From Osaka Mainichi Shimbun, June 23, 1933.)

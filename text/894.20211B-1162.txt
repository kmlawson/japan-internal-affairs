SOUTH CHINA MORNING POST Hong Kong, November 24, 1936.

# JAPANESE INVASION

## Davao Province Manchuria Of Philippines

### LAND APPLICATIONS

Manila, Nov. 20.

Davao province, focal point of Japanese residence in the Philippines, threatens to become the Manchuria of the Islands—an entry point for the same Nipponese wedge which started to split China, Sur Theodore Hsiao, special inspector of the Chinese consular service, has reported to Nanking.

Mr. Hsiao studied the Philippine situation on the first stop of an extensive inspection tour which Nanking hopes will improve the consular service, promote Chinese trade and achieve better co-operation between China proper and the "Little Chinas" scattered throughout the Orient.

He reported his belief Japan aims to achieve at least economic penetration of the islands, with Davao as the pry point, "such as was done in Formosa and South Manchuria."

He praised the thrift and industry of Japanese in making Davao perhaps the most prosperous and well-organized community in the Philippines, but warned the forces working beneath might be sinister.

He submitted to Nanking detailed information designed to support the charges, which Japanese and Filipino leaders have often ridiculed. The report pointed out China held first place in trade with the Philippines before 1900, but dropped in succession behind America, Britain and Japan. Japan has now jumped to second place and is climbing fast.

After completing a fortnight's intensive study in the Philippines, Mr. Hsiao left for Borneo, planning to proceed to Java, Sumatra, the Straits Settlements and other Oriental points before returning to Nanking.

Educated in the United States and England, Mr. Hsiao was formerly director of the department of foreign languages in Peking National University and commissioner of foreign affairs for Hunan and Hupeh provinces.—United Press.

## Emigration Proceeds

Manila, Nov. 20.

Lurking fear that Japanese in the southern province of Davao might eventually cause friction between Japan and an independent Philippines flared anew among Filipinos with the recent report that Hawaii-born Japanese were quietly emigrating to Davao for the purpose of acquiring public lands prohibited to foreigners.

In a report submitted to the Secretary of Labour, Inspector-General Jose Figueras pointed out the constitutional provision limiting ownership of Philippine public land to citizens of the islands and of the United States can be invalidated through these Hawaii-born Japanese, who are technically citizens of the United States.

General Figueras, who made a recent trip to the southern islands to investigate labour conditions, told the United Press there were at least two cases of Hawaiian born Japanese who filed papers—one to own land and the other to establish a firm to engage in the fishing industry.

Philippine Army officers in the province where Japanese have invested about 25 million dollars in hemp plantations, he added, reported some 30 Hawaiians of Japanese parentage recently arrived from aboard Nippon steamships which ply regularly twice a week to the open port of Davao.

However, the Japanese consulate in Manila said they found only one Hawaii-born and one American-born Japanese in the whole province. In spite of denials, the Secretaries of Justice and Agriculture and Commerce have announced an investigation of the affair.

Filipinos are concerned over the report. Davao is admittedly a thorny problem for the year-old Philippine Commonwealth to solve. President Manuel L. Quezon paid a visit to Davao over six months ago, but no announcement as to his decision has been made.

Japanese residents of Davao total some 13,000 while Japanese own 26,000 hectares of land comprising virtually half of the cultivated territory in the province.

Figueras was the Philippine Labour Commissioner to Hawaii, where he stayed for about a year, prior to his present post.—United Press.

I have the honor to report that Mr. A. H. J. Lovink, the Advisor on East Asiatic Affairs to the Netherlands Indian Government, recently asked me to ascertain if it would be possible for him to obtain some information from our Government on the subject of Japanese penetration into the Philippine Islands. Mr. Lovink, the Department doubtless remembers, has been most useful to this Consulate General in supplying it with accurate and detailed information in regard to Japanese activities in the Netherlands Indies.
The reason for the request is as follows: Mr. Lovink and his staff (consisting of some fifty persons of various races and nationalities) have now completed a most exhaustive report on the activities, backing and raisons d'etre of all Japanese enterprises in the Netherlands Indies. Mr. Lovink states

states that he has also obtained accurate data (sources not disclosed) of Japanese activities in French, Indo-China, Siam, British Malaya, British North Borneo, Australian New Guinea, Portuguese Timor and Northern Australia. He can therefore draw up a complete chart of Japanese activities in what the Japanese designate as the "South Seas" (Nanyo), except for the Philippine Islands. He has no definite information regarding Japanese activities in the Philippine Islands outside the widely-known facts of their participation in Philippine hemp production and trade and in the fishing industry. He desires more definite and detailed data on their concentrations of population, the activities engaged in by each concentration, the backing of their activities (whether semi-governmental or private), and similar information. With this data he will be able to complete his chart of Japanese activities in this part of the world. It is hoped that it will be possible for the Consulate General to obtain a copy of the completed chart.

From my experience as Acting Chief of the Office of Philippine Affairs I realize that complete data such as is desired is probably not available in the Department of State, but it is possible that it may be found in the archives of the War Department.

Respectfully yours,

Erle R. Dickover,
American Consul General.

AUSTRALIA AND THE STRUGGLE FOR THE PACIFIC OCEAN.

London. (From our own correspondent).

The report which recently appeared in the foreign press regarding a secret Japanese naval base near Australia and Japanese landing fields on the Island of Borneo (Dutch Indies) has aroused anxious response in Australia. An article has been published in the last number of the London DAILY HERALD by the Australian journalist Connolly, in which facts are given showing how far Japan is going in its "study" of the Australian coast and the islands adjoining it.

The author emphasizes that expansion in the Pacific Ocean and particularly in a southerly direction is one of the constituent parts of Japan's expansionist policy. He recalls that the World War had hardly begun when Japan hastened to seize the Caroline and Marshall Islands belonging to Germany.

"A glance at the map of the Pacific Ocean," writes the author, "will show the strategic importance of these archipelagos, not to speak of their commercial significance. They are situated less than 500 miles north and northeast of New Guinea, which in its turn is separated only by Torres Strait from the fertile, sparsely populated and rich Australian state

state of Queensland."

"If Japan is allowed to reach the limits of the northern side of Torres Strait, Australia will consider a Japanese invasion inevitable. Within the course of a few days an army could be landed on Cape York Peninsula and a Japanese fleet could bombard and seize the ports of northern Queensland."

In confirmation of the probability of these apprehensions the author observes that a considerable part of Australia, situated on the northern and western coast, almost the size of Europe, is very sparsely populated. With the exception of individual mail and tramp steamers, traffic in these Australian waters is restricted almost exclusively to pearl fishing vessels. The crews of these vessels are composed, either partially or entirely, of Japanese who, as the author states, "know more of the coast and of the Great Barrier Reef than the Australians themselves."

"One of the Australian pearl 'kings'," relates the author, "recently told me that the Japanese, who are working with their fleet in the district of the Thursday Islands, have charted parts of the Great Barrier Reef and the coast which have not yet been marked in the Admiralty maps."

A few years ago a group of Japanese capitalists applied to the Government of Western Australia with a proposal to rent the region near Yampi Sound which is rich in iron ore. "That was the first time," writes the author, "that the overwhelming majority of the Australian people ever heard of this region. Moreover, a great part of the Japanese fleet would be able to find shelter within its limits. At that time

Yampi Sound was not marked on the map of Australia, nor, to my knowledge, is it marked there now. But Japanese pearl fishers knew of this place and disclosed the iron ore deposits there."

"Australian naval officers relate astonishing facts regarding observation platforms discovered by them, built on the wild coast and hidden by forests not visited by anyone since the time of Flinders, one of the earliest explorers of Australia."

The author states that what has been said above is enough to justify Australian fears. "The Australians are afraid," he writes, "that aggressive Japan, having strengthened itself in Manchuria, will take advantage of Europe being engaged in other matters, and the United States' experiencing financial difficulties, to carry out their expansionist policy in the Pacific."

Fear of Japanese aggression drives Australia even closer to the United States. The author points out that "in case of danger Australia will be compelled to seek assistance somewhere else if it receives none from England." "The evergrowing friendship between the eastern states of Australia and the people of the United States," writes the author, "is testimony that both nations realize the community of their interests in the Pacific Ocean."

This observation very significantly characterizes the imperialist struggle for hegemony in the Pacific. It emphasizes particularly the conflicts of interest between the principal imperialist rivals of the world - the United States and England.

JAPAN (Political)

Subject: Japanese Activity in the Torres Strait.

The London Daily Herald publishes today an Australian (Brisbane) dispatch of October 8 on the above subject, as follows:

Thirty-eight members of the crew of a Japanese ship have raided and sacked a British island in the Torres Strait. The island is that of Haggerstone, which is about 125 miles from Thursday Island (Northern Australia). The Japanese crew - according to reports reaching here - landed during the temporary absence of settlers, who had left the island to get supplies. When the settlers returned they found that the Japanese had - smashed up the landing stage; stolen property from houses; and set fire to account groves.

Natives, who have come to live around settlements in the northern islands, are becoming afraid of marauding Japanese. The measures so far taken by the Commonwealth to afford protection for settlers in isolated outposts are apparently proving ineffective.

There has been for a long time increasing nervousness about the growing activities of Japanese around New Guinea (part of which is an Australian mandated territory). Traders and fishing vessels have shown a curiously strong interest in localities which have neither commercial nor fishing importance, but which have distinct strategic significance. And there have, from time to time, been reports of Japanese submarines having been sighted in the Torres Strait, between Northern Queensland and New Guinea.

From: M. A. London.

AUSTRIA (Political)

8800.

Subject: Japanese Vessel Detained.

The following Melbourne dispatch of October 11, on this subject, is of interest in connection with our import No. 36416, Japanese activity in the Torres Strait, dated October 9:

Following a report by a New Guinea district officer that a Japanese vessel, the Yosikino Maru, had been discovered poaching off the western islands of the Admiralty group, the Administrator of New Guinea, General McNicholl, ordered the detention of the master of the vessel, Captain Goni, and his crew of 24. It is stated that the vessel was loaded with six tons of trechua (greenmail shell).

General McNicholl has advised the Minister of Territories (Sir George Pearce) that the Yosikino Maru is believed to have come from Formosa, and that she is without ship's papers or bills of health. She was taken to Lorungan, the headquarters station of the Manus district, where the captain and crew were held as prohibited immigrants and also on charges of breaking the fisheries quarantine ordinance of the territory.

Several reports of mysterious movements of Japanese sampans off the Queensland and North Australian coasts have been received recently, but this is the first time that an opportunity has arisen to investigate the reasons for the presence of one in Australian waters. It is believed that the sampans are fitted with high-speed engines and collapsible masts.

From: S. J. London.
Date: Nov 13, 1934.

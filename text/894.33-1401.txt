February 1, 1941.
The American Ambassador, Mr. Grew,
with the Vice Minister for Foreign
Affairs, Mr. Ohashi, and the Vice
Minister of the Navy, Admiral Toyoda.
Subject: Japanese Warship ASAKA.
Mr. Ohashi, Vice Minister for Foreign Affairs, sent
for me "urgently" this afternoon and an interview took
place in the presence of Admiral Toyoda, Vice Minister of
the Navy, and another Japanese naval officer. The matter
had to do with the Japanese warship ASAkA which had sailed
from Japan on January 16 for the purpose of proceeding
via the Panama Canal to Europe, the sole mission of the
ship being to transfer certain Japanese naval and military
officers to Europe and to bring other officers back to
Japan. Difficult questions of visas rendered this procedure
important.
According to Mr. Ohashi, whose information and re-
marks were followed and supported by Admiral Toyoda, the
Department of State in Washington had informed the Japanese
Naval Attaché on January 14, two days prior to the sailing
of the ASAKA from Japan, that the Department perceived no
objeotion to the passage of the ship through the Panama
Canal but would have to consult the War and Navy Depart-
monts. Nothing further was heard from our Government
until January 28.
In the meantime, said Mr. Ohashi, a "Presidential
Ordinance" (presumably an Executive Order) had been issued
in Washington on January 16, the very day of the sailing
of

=== Page 68 === (confidence 0.98)
-2-
of the AsAkA from Japan, to the effect that ships passing
through the Panama Canal must conform to certain "security
measures", and this "ordinance" was brought to the atten-
tion of the Japanese Embassy in Washington on January 28.
On January 30 the Embassy had been further informed that
these "security measures" would take the form of an armed
guard to be placed on the ship during passage through the
Canal.
Mr. Ohashi, still with the concurrence of Admiral
Toyoda and consulting a document in Japanese, expressed
regret at the delay in their receipt of this information
because the ASAkA was now within six days of the Canal,
where she was due to arrive on February 7, and if they
had known sooner of the conditions for her passage through
the Canal they would have routed her by another course.
They could not agree to the placing of an armed guard on
the ship as this, in their opinion, was contrary to the
"privilege of warships".
Mr. Ohashi then said that if the American Government
feared some sabotage against the safety of the Panama Canal,
such as the sinking of the ASAKA therein, they would be
glad by way of compromise to invite one or more American
naval officers, to be designated by the American Navy De-
partment, to accompany the ship through the Canal as
"guests". He said that this proposal had already been
conveyed to our Government through the Japanese Naval At-
taohé in Washington, and their purpose in inviting me to
call today was to appeal to me to support this proposal
whioh they felt was a reasonable "compromise".

"Oral" Statement handed by the American Ambassador to the
Japanese Vice Minister for Foreign Affairs during conver-
sation on February 3, 1941.
Oral
My Government has authorized me to convey to Your
Excellency the following information with regard to
the passage of the Japanese naval transport ASAkA through
the Panama Canal:
Since the receipt of a note dated January 7 from the
Japanese Chargé d'Affaires ad interim this matter has
been under discussion between the Department of State,
the War Department, the Navy Department, and the Japanese
Embassy. An officer of the Navy Department on January 17
informed the Japanese Naval Attaché that the regulations
for the placing of United States armed guards on ships
during transit of the Panama Canal would probably apply
to public vessels. The Japanese Naval Attaché's expression
of hope that such regulation would not become effective
was noted and communicated to the appropriate authorities,
but without any encouragement being given to him.
Although my Government has given careful consideration
to the views expressed to me by Your Excellency and by
the Vice Minister of the Navy, and to similar views com-
municated by the Japanese Government to the Japanese Em-
bassy at Washington, no change has been made in the regu-
lations, which are believed to be in the interest of all
nations. My Government has informed the Japanese Embassy,
in response to its most recent inquiry on February 1, that
the Japanese Naval Attaché will be apprised of the situation
by an officer of the Navy Department if the Attaché will
approach the Navy Department.
In this connection I may state that the procedure was
not questioned when the new regulations were applied in the
case of a public vessel of another foreign power which re-
cently passed through the Panama Canal.

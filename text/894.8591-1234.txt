Referring to the letter of October 28, 1940 from the Navy Department requesting the Department of State to obtain cable or radio reports concerning the movements of Japanese vessels, may I point out that these messages may cost a considerable amount for which no provision has been made. I assume that the Navy will be prepared to pay cable charges and it is therefore suggested that there be added to each of the three attached telegrams the following:

"Charge cost of telegrams to the Navy in accordance with Section V-45, Foreign Service regulations."

Fw 894.8591/21

Since Consular Agents do not report direct to the Department, it is suggested that St. Lucia and Paramaribo be omitted from the list of offices to whom telegrams should be sent and that the following be added to telegrams to Barbados, Port of Spain, Curacao and Georgetown:

"Instruct Consular Agent report through you."

Finally, in order to save telegraph costs, it is suggested that Surabaya, Medan and Bombay be omitted from the list and that the words "repeat to consul under your jurisdiction" be added to the telegrams to Batavia, Wellington and Sydney, Australia. To the telegram to Calcutta add: "repeat to Bombay" and to the one to Capetown add: "repeat to Port Elizabeth and Durban." In this way, the message will reach all offices listed by the Navy in the most economical manner.

Except for telegraph costs, the task requested by the Navy should not be particularly burdensome except at offices in the Japanese Empire and there it may be necessary to employ additional personnel to collect and transmit the information. That will be a particularly delicate task in view of the attitude of the Japanese Government in such matters, as revealed in the Matsuo case.

Commander McCollum called and said that he had been asked by Captain Schuirmann to discuss the subject of the Navy Department's request of October 28 with a view to clearing up a possible misunderstanding on the part of officers of the Department as to what the Navy Department desired. He said that in the first place he felt that the amount of work that would be entailed by compliance with the Navy Department's request would not be great. The Navy Department had index cards on all Japanese merchant vessels over 4,000 tons; the number of such vessels did not exceed 160, of which a large number were engaged in Japanese transport and other nearseas services, leaving only about 50 or 60 engaged in services concerning which

which information was desired. In the second place, Commander McCollum stated that the Navy Department positively did not expect that the consuls would engage in any investigations to which even the Japanese would be likely to take exception. He thought that the main purpose of the request would be accomplished if the consuls could make a routine matter of reporting by telegraph when bills of health were granted by consuls to Japanese vessels of over 4,000 gross tons. This procedure would take care not only of vessels destined to American and Philippine ports, but also of other vessels which stopped at American ports en route to Central and South America, as well as of those calling at the Philippine Islands and having ultimate destinations elsewhere. He said that the Navy Department would also like to have information, so far as was publicly available, in regard to departures of vessels for other destinations; he said that the Navy Department was willing to drop the request so far as naval vessels were concerned, as he did not think that the consuls would be in position to supply such information except in the cases of Japanese tankers and others which obtain bills of health for American ports.

Mr. Ballantine asked Commander McCollum why the Naval Attache could not obtain the desired information.

Mr. Wilson:

Reference the Navy Department's letter of October 28, 1940, requesting reports by telegraph from consular offices in regard to the movement of Japanese vessels and the attached draft telegram to the Embassy at Tokyo requesting that consuls be instructed to report the information called for by telegraph.

FE concurs in the opinion expressed by FA in the last paragraph of its memorandum of November 9 that it may be necessary to employ additional personnel in the Japanese Empire to collect and transmit the information desired and that compliance with such an instruction will be a particularly delicate task in view of the attitude of the Japanese Government in such matters.

There are two features of the proposed instruction which would inevitably cause the Japanese authorities to regard with special suspicion any activities by our consuls of which the Japanese might become aware in connection with the carrying out of the instructions, namely, the fact that reports are being submitted only in respect to Japanese vessels and the inclusion of naval vessels in the scope of their reporting. If the scope of the reports were confined to Japanese merchant vessels engaged in trans-Pacific trade and in trade with the Philippine Islands, our interest in such information would be less likely to invite Japanese

Japanese suspicion. That portion of the draft instructions which relates to naval vessels would be almost certain to be a source of difficulty as information in regard to the movements of Japanese naval vessels is not readily obtainable and any activity on the part of consuls in endeavoring to uncover information in regard thereto would be likely to impair the usefulness of our consuls.

In view of the foregoing considerations, FE feels that the draft instruction to the Embassy at Tokyo should not be sent.

It is believed that there could be available to the Naval Attache at Tokyo as much information in regard to the entries and clearances of Japanese vessels as there is available to the Consuls. It is understood that either the Kobe-Yokohama Ship Owners Association or the Kobe-Yokohama Customs Brokers Association puts out monthly in advance schedules of arrivals and departures of passenger and freight vessels, which is furnished to the consuls and also should therefore be equally available to the Naval Attach. In addition to this list, it is understood that there is published in the press notices of arrivals and departures of merchant vessels. In as much as the principal ports of Japan are in quick communication with Tokyo, it should

Sir:

For the past several years the Navy Department has had under observation the movements of Japanese commercial shipping in the belief that unusual or abnormal changes in the operation of the Japanese merchant marine would serve as a prior indication of important political developments affecting United States-Japanese relations. The Department of State has cooperated in obtaining information concerning Japanese commercial ship operations in localities where U.S. naval facilities for observation did not exist and the information thus collected has been of material assistance in furthering the Navy Department's study of this subject.

The continuance of disturbed conditions throughout the world has led to many changes of commercial shipping routes, the suppression of published schedules of operation, and where such operating schedules are still available they have been found to be particularly unreliable, due to the necessity of frequent changes of ship schedules in order to meet rapidly changing world conditions. It appears, therefore, that the forwarding by mail of information concerning operation of Japanese commercial shipping by the various foreign service offices located abroad, as has been the practice in the past, will no longer furnish information adequate to meet the current situation.

A study of the conditions now existing leads to the belief that in order to observe adequately Japanese commercial shipping operations, radio or cable reports of the name, date of arrival, date of departure, last port of call and port for which bound of certain classes of Japanese commercial ships and all Japanese naval vessels touching at ports in certain areas are essential. In general, reports by radio or cable concerning Japanese commercial vessels of approximately 4,000 gross tons and above and all Japanese naval vessels are desirable from the following areas:

(a) Central and South America, including the West Indies and except the Republic of Panama.
(b) Netherlands East Indies, Borneo and Singapore.
(c) Australia and New Zealand.
(d) Bombay and Calcutta, India and Colombo, Ceylon.
(e) Capetown and Port Natal, Union of South Africa and Mombasa, Kenya Colony.
(f) Japanese Empire - port of first arrival or last departure for vessels operating to and from areas (a) to (e) above and to and from North America, Hawaiian Islands, Japanese Mandate Islands and the Philippine Islands.

DEPARTMENT OF STATE
THE UNDER SECRETARY
LIAISON OFFICE

December 21, 1940.

Mr. Wilson:

To save you detail, I 'phoned
Commander McCollum, O.N.I. (Br. 598)
with regard to the attached telegram.
He stated that the Navy Department did
not wish the schedules telegraphed, but
desired information with regard to only
the actual departures of Japanese vessels.
I have drafted the attached telegram for
your approval.

Commander McCollum stated that
his Department could easily arrange
for messages teletyped by the State
Department to the Navy Department
with regard to the movements of
Japanese vessels to be sent to him
in O.N.I. so that no change in the
method of our transmittal of such
messages need be made.
B. A. B.

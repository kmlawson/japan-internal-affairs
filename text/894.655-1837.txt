Your Majesty:
We are pleased to advise Your Majesty that cotton cloth and cotton yarn
are now being produced in substantial quantities for export from both Germany and
Japan. The attached list indicates the types of fabric constructions and counts
of yarn produced from imported American cotton which are being sold by the U. S.
Commercial Company, an agency of the United States Government, for export from
Germany and Japan.
It has been agreed in regard to Japanese textiles, that Far Eastern
countries will be given a prior option. However, such quantities as are not taken
up under these options are to be offered immediately to the Governments of other
countries, as far as possible, in accordance with their net import requirements.
Uw
Prices are comparable to those for similar textiles exported from the U. S., and
payment will be required in U. S. dollars. The terms of sale for German textiles
are similar and distribution will be made in approximately the same manner.
We have been informed that supplies of American cotton textiles available
for importation into Yemen will probably fall considerably short of meeting require-
ments during the first half of 1947. Limited quantities of both cotton cloth and
yarn are now available for purchase from both Japan and Germany to assist your
Government in meeting these deficits.

us entirely reasonable, providing, of course, the size of
the Textile Industry itself is kept down to a reasonable
figure, which we feel should be in the neighborhood of
three million spindles. The point we want to emphasize as
emphatically as we can is that a Japanese Textile Machinery
Industry of proper size for a three million spindle Textile
Industry should be about one-eighth of its present size.
This woulc put it on a comparable basis to the Textile
Machinery Industry in this country which always had export
business of from ten to twenty-five percent of its production
while satisfactorily taking care of the needs of the domestic
industry. To put it in another way, if you allow the
Japanese Textile Machinery to get into production again at
one-quarter of the rate it was producing in 1937, it would
be proper in size to maintain a six million spindle Textile
Industry (which we are sure would never be considered) and
still provide a good-sized export quota.
We believe that control of the Japanese Textile
Machinery Industry through control of iron and steel supplies
to that industry is sound procedure but will be effective
only if the foregoing facts are kept well in mind.

The subject report, one of the special industry studies prepared at the
request of OE, is transmitted herewith. The study points out that Japan's
ability to satisfy domestic requirements for textiles from indigenous
production and in addition produce and sell sizable quantities for export
are vital factors in the recovery and future stability of the Japanese
economy.
In the period 1930-34, accepted by the FEC as an adequate base for
normal needs, Japan's domestic consumption of textiles averaged about
260,000 metric tons per year, or roughly 8.7 pounds per capita. On the basis
to
of this consumption, but with suitable adjustments for an increase in
population, Japan in "1950" (selected for the purpose of this study as a
normal postwar year) would require abcut 312,000 metric tons of textiles
for domestic consumption. Even if the consumption pattern were modified to
allow for a greater use of rayon, a change initiated by the Japanese
themselves in prewar years, total tonnage requirements would remain roughly
8
94.
the same.
Restoration of normal consumption, however, would be possible only
under manufacturing and export conditions that are not likely to obtain in
9
the foreseeable future. In sharp contrast to the normal consumption of abou
8.7 pounds per capita per year, the Japanese at present consume about two
5
pounds per capita and production has reached only 20 percent of the 1930-34
level. In terms of Japan's balance of payments, moreover, the disparity
between present production and prewar levels poses one of the most crucial
problems for Japan's economic future.
In the prewar period Japan's textile exports normally represented about
50 percent of all exports, netting in the period 1930-34 an average of 140
million dollars anmually. In contrast, net receipts from textile exports in
fiscal 1947 totaled less than 30 million dollars. Since the war, a
combination of factors such as the need to rehabilitate the plant and
equipment of the industry and shortages of food, labor, and certain raw

Cotton.
Mr. Cleaves summarized Japan's current cotton industry
situation for the group. He pointed out that:
1. Since April lst, orders for Japanese cotton textiles
have dropped off considerably owing to (1) the lack of dollars
on the part of the NEI and India, (2) the recent decision on the

and (3) the actions of American exporters who are dumping Amer-
ican-made goods in order to reduce large stocks. There are no
current orders to speak of and there is no sign that Japan's ex-
port business in cotton textiles will improve in the next few
months.

lir. Cleaves said that the charges made by the American woolen kmit

The leading American woolen knit glove producer has not taken a new cus-

2. The price comparisons used by the American industry are mis-

American industry uses average prices including all types of these gloves,
whereas Japanese gloves are 25% lighter in weight than American gloves
and are much less stylized than the American product. Likewise, the
quality of Japanese gloves is inferior to that of American gloves.
3. When prices are compared for gloves of a comparable nature,
the actual prices do not show Japanese gloves to be significently cheaper

4. Figures used by the American industry on the volume of

ing the first ten months of 1948, when actually only about 34,000 dozen
pairs were shipped. When this error was pointed out to the industry,
they nevor corrected it and continued to use the wrong figures.

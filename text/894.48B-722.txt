The inclusion of the foreign representatives and
the enshrinement of the spirits of the foreign victims
was due largely, if not entirely, to the single-handed
offorts of Dr. Issa Tanimura, at present supervisor of
one of the dairy farms of the Imperial Household. Dr.
Tanimura, who was personally acquainted with many of the
foreigners who lost their lives in the catastrophe, after
a series of conferences with the Foreign Office and Tokyo
City officials, succeeded in having invitations sent to
those missions whose nationals were victims of the Great
Earthquake. Quite obviously the idea occurred to Dr.
Tanimura only recently because the invitations to the
foreign missions were not delivered until late Friday
afternoon, August 30th and, being in the Japanese language,
were not available in intelligible form until Saturday
morning. Dr. Tanimura called at this Embassy in person
shortly after noon on Saturday and explained the purpose
of the invitation and expressed the hope that represen-
tatives could be sent from the Kmbassy, the Consulate
General and the Consulate at Yokohama.
After consultation with the British Embassy both
missions sent three representatives as requested, while
the French and German Embassies were also represented.
The American representatives were Mr. Crocker, Second
Secretary, Vice Consul Hutchinson of Tokyo and Vice Consul
Rowe of Yokohama.
The ceremony was attended by Premier Okada, the
Minister of Education, representatives from the other
Ministries, the Governor of Tokyo Prefecture, the Mayor
of

of Tokyo, the heads of the Prefectural and City Assemblies
respectively, and many others.
The ceremonies were held first according to Shinto
and then according to Buddhist rites in the Earthquake
Memorial Hall in Honjo, while several thousand members of
families who miffered bereavement during the catastrophe
stood in silenoe outside for the two and a halr hour ser-
vices despite the rain which was pouring down in sheets.
The Premier and several cabinet representatives left at
the conclusion of the Shinto rites but their places were
taken by other representatives for the Buddhist rites;
among the latter was General Araki, former Minister of War.
The foreign representatives joined in the Shinto cere-
mony of laying branches before the altar and also in placing
incense before the Buddhist altar.
In view of the fact that wreaths were sent by the

it was deemed appropriate and necessary that a wreath be

AMERICAN KINDNESS RECALLED
(Editorial)
Tokyo, JIJI, January 31, 1930.
With the completion of the reconstruction work
of Tokyo toward the end of March this year, that of
Yokohama having been finished in March last year, the
material vestiges of the terrifio earthquake of 1923
will entirely disappear in so far as appearances are
concerned. But the extraordinary spiritual test in-
flicted on us on September 1, 1923, was too tragic to
The fratern-
be effaced from our memory for many years.
al love displayed at that time, ignoring frontiers,
racial disorimination, and religious differences, was
really a magnificent display of the true sentiment of
humanity which will never be forgotten by the Japanese
people.
In view of the completion of the reconstruction
of Tokyo and Yokohama, we recall once more the cordial
sympathy and assistance extended to us by various nations
at the time.
. The nations that presented written con-
solation to us numbered more than forty, while the relief
contributions in money and articles by the United States,
Great Britain, France, Italy and China amounted to several
million yen.
Above all, the contributions by Americans
in offioial and private circles on a large soale and the
promptitude with which they extended their relief toward
sufferers are deeply insoribed in the minds of sufferers
as well as of the Japanese people in general.
With

With regard to these generous contributions from
the United States, we owe a debt of gratitude to the
efforts of Mr. Cyrus W. Wood who was the American
Ambassador to Japan at the time. Notwithstanding the
faot that the American Embassy buildings were destroyed
by the earthquake fire and that some of the members of
his family were injured more or less seriously, Mr.
Woods lost no time in applying to his home Government,
the Government-General of the Philippines, and the
American Asiatic Squadron by wireless telegraphy for
prompt relief. Mr. Woods himself took appropriate
measures for relief work in person and thus won the
respeot of all olasses of the Japanese as a patron of
Japan and her people.
Instantly,on receipt of instructions, vessels be-
longing to the American Asiatic Squadron in Chinese
waters and the neighborhood took on board foodstuffs
and other neoessary relief materials and entered Tokyo
Bay in the early part of September. The BLACK HAWK was
the first to arrive here, followed by other vessels in
quiok succession. Under the command of Admiral Anderson,
the squauron remained here for more than half a month,
assisting all the while the Japanese navy in the trans-
portation of relief materials and people in distress and
also in wireless comnunication. In the interval, the
Government-General of the Philippines oollected a large
quantity of foodstuffs, medicines, tents, beds, olothing,
eto., which were promptly brought here by the MERIT and
other transports.
.General Reed, Commander of the

Amerioan garrison in the Philippines, at the head of
military surgeons and nurses, took passage to Japan on
those ships and set up tent hotels in Tokyo and Yokohama
on a gigantio scale capable of accommodating more than
1,Oo0 suffers each. At Kobe, ten hospitals were also
established for sufferers under the direotion of Major-
General MoCoy. On September 3rd, President Coolidge
issued a message, advising the Americans to make all
possible preparations in their power for prompt relief.
With the American Red Cross Sooiety as relief head-
quarters, a plan was made public for contributions to
the amount of five million dollars. This was received
by the Americans with enthusiasm.
American newspapers
likewise supported the relief movement in real earnest.
Even those American individuals, public organizations,
and newspapers which were anti-Japanese helped in the
movement. By virtue of their fraternal feeling, con-
tributions covered the above specified amount within
three days and by the middle of September the contribu-
tions reached $8,000,000 which swelled to more than
$10,000,000 by the end of the month.
A considerable
amount in contributions in money and articles was raised
by religious and other organs in the United States.
Some railroad and shipping companies proposed to trans-
port relief articles free of charge. Relief materials
were transported here by some thirty steamers which
left either San Franoisoo or Seattle for Shibaura and
Yokohama where, we still remember, these materials were
piled up as high as hills. Monetary contributions in
the United

whioh was used as a fund for the construotion of the
Fraternal Commemoration Hospital (Doai Kinen Byoin) at
Honjo in Tokyo.
This hospital will remain forever as
asymbol of the goodwill and sympathy of the Americans.
The prompt relief measures taken by the Americans on a
oomprehensive soale have never before been recorded in
the history of the world. This was doubtless in part
attributable to the immense wealth of the United States
and to geographical proximity, and yet it was largely
due to the noble American sentiments of humanity and
fraternity. This munificence has called forth a pro-
found admiration and respect towards the Americans, not
only by the Japanese but also by the peoples of the
world.
We have since been grateful to the Americans not
for material reasons but because of the display of
humanity and fraternity in a practical manner when we
were in distress.
. We shall never forget their good-
will; it is a duty of the Japanese as a nation. Our

In the company of Mr. Tompkison, the undersigned
called at the Water Police Statlon on August 28, 1940
to discuss the case of Isaacs A. Levy, applicant for a
transit certificate at the American Consulate in Casa-
blanca, Morocco, which was referred to this office by
the Consulate General on receipt of a cable from the
Consul in that city.
Mr. S. Hosh1, Inspector in charge or the Foreign
Section of the Water Police, Mr. Ogawa, also of the
Water Police, and Mr. Amano, of Kagacho Station, took
part in tne discussion. A ratner lucid explanation
of the Japanese stand was set forth.
Japanese consular officers issue twc types of
visas: transit visas, valid for entry for a fifteen
day period; entrance visas, valid for an entry or
entries into Japan during a period of one year. In
certain exceptional cases, e.g., white Russians, the
latter may be limited to three months instead of one
year. (A further proviso may be added to the effect
that the person receiving a limited visa must reside
eitner in Tokyo, in Yokohama or in Kobe. It seems
that this tipulation 1s not actually put in writing
1n the travel dooument and it 1s done only in cases
of persons without nationality.)
The police were unable to explain the limitation
of Levy's visa unless it might be because it was state-
less and of Jewish race.
It is extremely rare that a person in possession
of a Japanese consular visa 1s refused admittance.
The Japanese authorities at Yokohama will not, however,
issue a letter guaranteeing the entry of any foreigner
upon his arrival.
Japanese consular officers are presumed to have
investigated eaon case before issuing a visa. (Mr.
Amano said, off the record, that investigation might
sometimes be hampered by language barriers.) However,
Japanese regulations do provide that prefectural authori-
ties may bar, under certain ciroumstances, the admission
of persons who do have consular visas.
Entry is prohibited to persons likely to become
public charges, those who are "opposed to Japan", those
of bad character and to persons having a communioable
disease. It was stated that persons of ill character
were

any one of these points between the time a person

For this reason, the authorities do not wish to make

Although the Foreign Office Note refers only to

The most important feature of the present revision,
however, is the requirement that all aliens must receive
pernission to leave the country, a provision heretofore

ant features of the revision are discussed below. (It
should be noted that a relatively minor revision of

3969/of June 19, 1939.)
Article 2. An addition to those prohibited
from landing in Japan is made to include those
who ignore the endorsements concerning time and
place of arrival placed on the visas. Visas are
limited to six months validity (formerly one year),
and entrance visas may be used only once (formerly
unlimited within the period of validity).
Article 6. Provision is made for special
entry if an alien has no visa, or if for a suf-
ficient reason he cannot adhere to the endorse-
ments on his visa. When such special permission
is granted, the period of stay and place of so-
journ may be designated.
Article 17. Completely new. An alien wishing
to depart must obtain a permit therefor, filing an
application three days prior to departure. Provi-
sion is made for an alien without a travel document.
The place and time of departure, and route to be
taken from residence to place of departure, may be
specified in whole or part.

Article 17-A. Completely new. Provision is
made for changing specified conditions of arrival,
sojourn, or departure, such as time and place of
arrival, time and place of departure, upon appli-
cation, when adequate reasons are advanced.
Articles 19 and 20. The penal provision of
the Ordinance are enlarged to cover the scope of
the present revision.
Supplementary Regulations. The valid visas
of persons residing abroad when the present revi-
sion comes into force are valid for a year from
date of issuance, except that they may be used
only for one entry. Visas of persons in Japan at
the time this revision comes into force are invalid
upon their departure from Japan, regardless of the
date of issue.
It will be noted that the regulations coverning the
registration of aliens remain untouched, and that the
application for a permit to depart must be filed through
the police office of the alien's place of residence.
With reference to the issuance of visas by Japanese
consular officers, the Embassy has from time to time
heard reports, principally from China, that applications
were being referred to the Foreign Office, and that
there were frequent refusals. The appropriate bureau of
the Foreign Office was approached for information re-
garding the detailed regulations governing the issuance
of visas, but in accordance with usual Japanese prac-
tice this information was not divulged. The only definite
point learned was that Japanese consular officers have
less discretion than formerly and are required to refer
more categories of applications to the Foreign Office
than heretofore.

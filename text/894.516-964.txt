The financial affairs of the business interests of Nagoya are largely in the hands of eight ordinary banking houses, six savings banks, and one agricultural and industrial bank, all with headquarters in Nagoya, and fifteen banks in Tokyo, Osaka, and other cities, with their branches in Nagoya. The deposits at the end of 1929 for ordinary banks were Yen 373,343,047, and Yen 45,700,000 for savings banks. The loans of ordinary banks were Yen 281,369,221, while those of savings banks were Yen 3,992,000. Compared with the figures at the end of 1924, Nagoya banks gained Yen 108,431,701 in deposits and Yen 14,109,000 in loans. There are no foreign banks represented in the Nagoya Consular District. (One Yen = $0.4900 at the end of 1929.)

## The Three Leading Banks of Nagoya.

The Nagoya Bank, Meiji Bank, and Aichi Bank are known as "Nagoya's Big Three", and their deposits represented 65.8 per cent. of the total deposits held, and 77.1 per cent. of the loans advanced, by the banks with headquarters in Nagoya, or 47 per cent. of the total deposits and 51.8 per cent. of the total loans of all the banking houses in the city.

Some idea of the supremacy of the Nagoya, Meiji and Aichi Banks in this district may be had from the following statistics as of December 31, 1929:

The Nagoya Bank, Limited.

The Nagoya Bank is the oldest of the three large local banks, being established in 1882. Capital authorized, Yen 20,600,000. Amount paid in, Yen 13,950,000. Reserve funds, Yen 7,950,000. The bank has 46 branches and 9 agencies in the cities of Nagoya, Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto, and throughout the prefectures of Aichi, Shizuoka, Gifu, Fukui and Mie. The bank has in recent years gained considerably in deposits. Large sums have been set aside as surplus out of profits, and the bank has become increasingly stabilized. The head office is located at Sakae machi, Itchome, Nagoya; Mr. Kosaburo Tsunekawa, President.

The Meiji Bank, Limited.

Established in 1896. Capital authorized, Yen 14,200,000. Amount paid in, Yen 12,030,000. The bank has 10 branches in Nagoya and 38 branches and 35 agencies in the cities of Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, and throughout the Prefectures of Ishikawa, Fukui, Shiga, Gifu, Shizuoka, and Aichi. As in the case of the Nagoya Bank, the financial panic of 1927 served only to enhance the confidence of the bank's clients and prospects, as evidenced by increased deposits since that time. The head office is located at Sakae machi, Nichome, Nagoya; Mr. Shigehiko Ikoma, President.

An acute

=== Page 10 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

An acute situation in Nagoya banking circles has recently developed, arising out of lack of public confidence engendered by the failure of two minor local banks, the Koromogaura Bank (Capital Yen 1,000,000 - $498,500 at par) at Kamezaki, Chita gun, Aichi Prefecture, and the Nosho Bank (Capital Yen 500,000 - $249,250 at par) of Nagoya, in December, 1931, which culminated in serious runs on four important Nagoya institutions, the Aichi, Nagoya, Meiji and Murase Banks, and the temporary suspension of payments by the two last named. At this date the Murase Bank has again resumed payments but the Meiji Bank is still closed.

A hurried conference was called on March 4, 1932, between the leading prefectural and city officials, bankers and prominent business men of Nagoya, to discuss the situation and a committee consisting of the Governor of Aichi Prefecture, the Mayor of Nagoya, and the President of the Chamber of Commerce conferred with the Governor of the Bank of Japan, who came to Nagoya on March 4th to render necessary assistance and give advice. The latter pledged the support of the Bank of Japan to stabilize the situation as soon as possible, the adoption of a generous loan attitude, and the advance of necessary funds (reported to be Yen 100,000,000) to tide over the crisis.

The Governor of Aichi Prefecture issued a reassuring public statement on March 4th, urging the public, in its own interests, not to aggravate the situation

=== Page 11 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

the situation further through the unnecessary withdrawal of bank deposits. At this writing confidence seems to have been restored and the runs on the aforementioned banks have ceased.

Some doubt still exists as to the outcome of the affairs of the Meiji Bank, which has a paid in capitalization of Yen 12,030,000 ($5,996,955 at par), and had deposits of approximately Yen 95,004,000 ($47,359,494 at par), at the close of 1931. Negotiations for its consolidation with the Aichi Bank (paid in capitalization of Yen 11,800,000 - $5,882,300 at par), were held between the directors of the two banks on February 29th, and reported in the press to have been successful. However, later developments apparently indicate that difficulties in consummating the merger have arisen, although well informed local circles anticipate the eventual success of the arrangement.

# Circular

Several Embassies and Legations have recently been asking the Imperial Ministry for Foreign Affairs for information concerning the application of the Law to Prevent the Flight of Capital (Law No. 17) as well as of the decrees No. 12 and 13 of the Ministry of Finance, published on July 1st (see Official Gazette for July 1).

The Finance Ministry, having been asked by the Ministry for Foreign Affairs to give its opinion, has just stated that, following the intention of the law and decrees, it would seem proper to request the members of the diplomatic corps to fulfill the formalities prescribed by the aforesaid law and decrees. Therefore, as regards their accounts in foreign currency, they should present applications through their banks, in the manner prescribed by Article 4 of Decree No. 13 (a provisional translation is attached).

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs accordingly has the honor to convey the above information to the Embassies and Legations at Tokyo with the additional assurance from the Ministry of Finance that when the applications are submitted it will examine them with all possible good will and will take steps to avoid creating more difficulties than are necessary.

Tokyo, August 26, 1932.

Sir:

At one time I spent three years in Japan. In 1905 I came home to visit and was met on all sides with the statement that the Japanese are so dishonest that they are forced to employ Chinese in their banks.

I was young and uninformed and did not argue. On my return to Japan, I asked our Japanese Secretary, the late Mr. Ransford Miller, to look into the facts and tell me the truth. When I returned to America in 1906, I was in a position to say with authority that no Chinese had ever at any time been employed in a Japanese bank.

I lead a most retired life and yet even I am constantly hearing this same story paraded with conviction. Last night at dinner, I made the usual denial and rehearsed patiently the familiar data: - definition of extra territoriality; treaty ports; Hong Kong Shanghai Banking Corporation bringing over their trained Chinese compradors, etc., etc. I met with this reply, "But that isn't true now. My friends, who have just come from China through Japan, saw the Chinese now in the Japanese banks." I said "Very well, I shall inform myself on this subject." The answer was "You will receive prejudiced opinion," to which I replied, "I shall ask the information of the American Embassy in Tokyo."

Will you be so good as to instruct the proper person to inform me as to whether or no the Japanese have fallen into the custom of employing Chinese in their banks. Will you also be so good as to tell me whether I have your permission to publish this letter and your reply in any reputable newspaper.

I am, Sir,

Very truly yours,

(Signed) Lucy James
Mrs. Wortham James.

Tokyo, September 10, 1934.

Mrs. Wortham James,
Cherry Neck,
Newport, Rhode Island.

Madam:

I have received your letter of August 20, in which you ask whether Chinese are employed in Japanese banks. From the general wording of your letter I presume that what you wish to know is the extent to which Chinese are employed in banks in Japan.

In reply I may state that Chinese are employed in many banks that deal in foreign exchange in Japan. I do not know of their being employed in any other type of bank in Japan. Foreign banks, that is, branch offices in Japan of American, British and other banks, employ a number of Chinese in their exchange departments. Japanese banks at one time followed the same practice and there may still be a number of Chinese employees who come in contact with the foreign public. I should add that there are many persons not of Japanese nationality, including American, British, Portuguese and other clerks and advisers, who are employed in banks here because of some special qualification. The bulk of the bank employees in Japan, and in Japanese banks the officers holding positions of responsibility as well, are naturally Japanese.

Years ago much of the foreign exchange business in the Far East involved the physical transfer of coins and bullion. As you are probably aware, the weight of coins and the fineness of their silver content as well as that of bullion, vary considerably in the different ports, especially in China. It was customary, in fact imperative, for all banks and brokers to employ Chinese silver experts who were, and still are, extremely skilful in detecting faulty coins and gauging the fineness of silver. This practice still obtains in China, but the need for it in Japan lessened with the adoption here of a uniform currency. Japanese banks with branches in China naturally have Chinese employees there, and it is possible that they have some in their offices in Japan which deal in foreign exchange and currency.

The Embassy has no reason whatever to believe that questions of racial honesty are involved in choosing the personnel of banks in Japan.

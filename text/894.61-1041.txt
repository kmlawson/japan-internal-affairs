AGRICULTURE AND SOCIAL UNREST IN JAPAN
I.
Statistical Foreword:
The increasingly adverse situation of the farmer
and agricultural landlord in Japan merits attention
if only because practically half the total number of
households of Japan Proper are engaged in agriculture.
(At the end of 1929: 47.1 per cent. or 5,575,583 house-
holds.)
Although 28 per cent. of these agricultural
households are no more than subsidiarily so engaged, the
economic pressure in practically all lines of occupation
has become su acute that an unfavorable agricultural
situation may vitally affect even them.
. So small is
the area cultivated by most farmers (70 per cent. of the
farms are less than 2-1/2 acres in size) that the bound-
ary between profit and loss is quickly crossed.
The
entire area of arable land in Japan (approximately 15
per oent. of the total) is 22,584 square miles, less in
size than the State of West virginia.

In the recent Diet session three measures were

Control of Rice and Other Cereals.
The text of this 1aw, promulgated on March 9, 1933,

Under this law the Government is to fix a minimum and
maximum price for rice, between which limits the market
price is to be maintained. By purchase of stocks on the
market the Government hopes to prevent prices from falling
below the official minimum, and by disposal of stocks in
Government granaries, prices can be prevented from rising
above the ficial maximums. Moreover, the Government may
regulate the amounts of domestic rice to be exported, and
may restrict importations of cereals, including rice, millet,
kaoliang, panium, et cetera, from foreign countries and
Japanese overseas possessions.
The new measure, which is to take effect from next
November, will supersede the Rice Control Law now in force.
This law, enacted in l92l, has not proved successful. Not

This law, promulgated on March 29th, has provided a new

Tokyo, August 19, 1948

Subject: Procurement of Communications Services for
Non-Occupation Use in Japan.

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to enclose copies
of a letter from the Adjutant General's Section of this Headquarters
to the Commanding General, Eighth Army, dated 19 April 1948, on the
subject of procuring communications facilities in Japan, and copies
of a second letter dated 31 July 1948, modifying the above with regard
to telephone service for non-occupation personnel, together with
a clipping from the Nippon Times of August 17, 1948 commenting on
the difficulties of installing telephones in Japan. This information,
it is believed, may be of interest to prospective commercial visitors
to Japan.

No foreign commercial organization licensed for business in
Japan is considered to be an "Occupation agency" for purposes of procuring
communications equipment. With the exception of certain agencies
listed in Enclosure 1 which are allowed to continue using occupation
facilities already available to them, foreign traders must henceforth
apply directly to the Japanese Central Telephone Office in the
city in which they desire service.

The Ministry of Communications has drawn up a schedule of priorities
to be followed in allocating non-occupation telephones, which
schedule is dated 15 June 1948 and is appended to Enclosure 2. Class
A includes only public utilities, foreign and Japanese government
agencies, Diet members, and political parties represented in the Diet.
The Tokyo Central Telephone Office has informed this mission, however,
that foreign traders are being given the same priority as Japanese
government agencies, and are therefore able to obtain telephones with
a delay of only one to two weeks, depending on difficulty of installation.

This high priority gives foreign traders a marked advantage over
Japanese businessmen, as the latter, except in rare cases, must purchase
used telephones from "telephone brokers" for prices at present
reported to vary from 60,000 to 80,000 yen, depending on the area involved.
Telephone brokers specialize in buying and selling telephone
"rights", and owe their existence to the scarcity of new circuits,
and, at present, to the necessity of maintaining reserve supplies of

To: Commanding General

1. Reference is made to letter, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, file AG 400.12 (5 May 47) CCS, dated 19 April 1948, subject: The Provision and Procurement of Communication Services, Facilities, Equipment, Material, and Personnel.

2. Paragraph 5 of reference letter is hereby amended to read: "Communications Services Facilities, Equipment, Material and Personnel for licensed entrants not a part of the occupation force agencies as defined in paragraph (1) above will be procured by the licensed entrants directly from the Japanese Central Telephone Office in the city in which they desire telephone service."

3. With reference to paragraph 6 of the same letter, a survey made of the handling of telephone applications for licensed entrants reveals that in many instances appeal is made to the occupation forces for assistance before any application has been made to the local Japanese telephone office. This procedure has resulted in delays in obtaining service which, in most instances, would have been avoided had the application been made directly to the Japanese telephone office in the first place. Hereafter when licensed entrants apply to occupation force personnel for telephone service or priorities, they will be requested to make application first to the local Japanese telephone office. The inclosed Japanese priority schedule will be applied by the local Japanese telephone officials and in most cases will result in the applicant receiving sufficiently high priority to obtain service without enlisting occupation force assistance. If the Japanese officials are unwilling to accept the application for service (which may happen in cases where the applicant's business does not entitle him to high priority on the schedule) the aid of the occupation forces may be required and priority will be arranged as provided in paragraph 6 referred to above.

4. Paragraph 7 of reference letter is hereby amended to read: "In instances where it is necessary, in connection with the performance of services for the occupation, for Japanese nationals or organizations to have telephone service and where they have made application to the Japanese telephone office and been informed that they cannot obtain telephone service within a period of three months, these persons may appeal to the occupation force agency for which they are performing official services, for assistance. When this occupation force agency receives the application it will ascertain the status of the work being done for the occupation forces, and, if telephone service will be required, appeal will be made to the Signal Corps agency responsible for service in the area involved. The appeal will contain a statement that telephone service is essential and necessary in connection with official occupation force activities. Upon receipt, the Signal Corps agency responsible for providing local service will notify direct the local Japanese telephone office at which the application for telephone service has previously been filed, that Class A priority will be granted and that no portion of the costs will be borne by the occupation forces. Many reports have been received that Japanese individuals and organizations have appealed to the occupation forces and been granted special priority for obtaining telephone service where service was purely for convenience and not a necessity. Exhaustion of Japanese central office and outside plant facilities has been reported in a number of cities and central offices and is approaching in many others. To protect the needs of essential and necessary applicants, no priorities will be granted Japanese individuals or organizations without first scrutinizing each application. In no case will priority be granted unless service is essential for occupation force purposes and priorities will not be arranged for convenience reasons."

5. In considering requests for assistance, Signal personnel will keep in mind Japanese regulations governing the connection of telephone service. The procedure followed upon receipt of an application for telephone services is for the Japanese to send an engineer to determine the availability of inside and outside plant. This engineering check may require as much as two weeks, although usually the condition of plant is determined in a day or two. If the plant check reveals that over 120 meters of rubber covered wire, or in excess of three poles are required, the application for service will be held up until one or more additional applications in the same area have been received. Where multiple applications have been filed, the necessary telephone plant may be constructed if the average amount of wire per applicant is less than 100 meters. Existing Japanese regulations permit no exceptions to this rule and where licensed entrants apply for assistance to the occupation forces in such instances, an attempt will be made to justify the Japanese regulations on the basis that critical materials are required and it is economically unscound to employ an excessive amount of these materials to serve only one telephone applicant.

6. Even where high priority is granted, applicants for telephone service in Japan will frequently be asked to wait as much as 90 days before telephone service is installed. Construction programs are laid out on a quarterly basis and no attempt should be made to compel the Japanese to connect service sooner due to the disruption that this might cause in construction programs or to the duplication of telephone plant that such an order might necessitate. Applicants for telephone service under existing regulations may purchase telephone bonds on only the first three days of each month. This means that an applicant for telephone service who places his order on the fourth of the month will be informed that it cannot be finally accepted until the first of the following month although the check to determine availability of facilities will be made beforehand.

7. In order to avoid excessive clerical work in connection with the provision of priorities by Signal Corps agencies, administrative channels should be shortened to the greatest possible extent. It is believed that in most cases requests may be referred directly to the local Japanese telephone business office without reference to other administrative offices.

# Cost Of Installing Phone Is Fantastically High Here

By JOHN RICH
INS Staff Correspondent

American and foreign civilians living in Japan must invest $133 dollars in Japan's Government for 15 years before they can get a telephone.

Furthermore, to do so they are violating their license to do business in Japan and must be granted a "special exception" by Gen. MacArthur's headquarters.

The confusion for the eighty odd foreign companies licensed under SCAP to do business in Japan began when the National Diet passed what it officially called, "The Law Concerning Temporary Measures To Make Applications For Telephones, etc. Purchase Bonds."

Under this law everyone, Japanese and foreigner alike, must buy a ¥36,000 national bond before a phone can be installed in his home or office.

At the official exchange rate of 270 yen to a dollar this amounts to $133.

The bond pays four per cent interest and if the purchaser is still around fifteen years later he gets his principal back. However, the chances of ¥36,000 being worth more than a fraction of its present value after 5 years are slim.

Foreign concerns doing business in Japan under SCAP license are specifically prohibited from using yen for investments of any kind. The new Bill, however, was passed in the Diet.

ESS is presently handling the situation by writing covering letters to each company applying for phone service explaining that they are permitted to buy this one Japanese bond, but no other, without violating their license.

# TELEPHONE SERVICE OPERATIONS IN JAPAN

1. The commercial telephone system over which both local and long distance telephone service is rendered throughout Japan is owned and operated exclusively by the Ministry of Communications of the Japanese Government. There are no separate telephone companies in Japan, rendering commercial telephone service, local or toll.

2. Letters concerning telephone matters involving any of the principal city telephone exchanges or any others of Japan can be addressed in English to:

Ministry of Communications
Tokyo, Japan

It is pointed out, however, that any negotiations engaged in between the Japanese Government and Foreign Business firms are carried on under the control of the General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

AMERICAN CONSULATE GENERAL
Hong Kong, March 28, 1941

CONFIDENTIAL

SUBJECT: Japanese War Vessels Observed in Maneuvers Near Kobe by a Passenger on the S.S. PRESIDENT CLEVELAND.
MISE ONI MID

THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

SIR:

I have the honor to enclose herewith, as of possible interest to the Department, a memorandum prepared by Consul John H. Bruins of this office of a conversation with an American missionary who recently arrived in Hong Kong on the S.S. PRESIDENT CLEVELAND.

Mr. Bruins' visitor refers to maneuvers by Japanese war vessels about three hundred miles from Kobe on March 22nd, 1941, on which occasion, he states, the captain of the S.S. CLEVELAND was asked by the Japanese to change his course and refused.

The captain of the American ship will be interviewed when he next visits Hong Kong, and if he has anything further of interest to add to the account given in the enclosed memorandum, an air-mail report will be made accordingly.

Very respectfully,

Addison E. Southard
American Consul General

=== Page 4 === (confidence 1.00)
Enclosure No. 1 to Despatch No. 874, dated March 28, 1941, from Addison E. Southard, American Consul General, Hong Kong, on the subject, "Japanese War Vessels Observed in Maneuvers Near Kobe by a Passenger on the S.S. PRESIDENT CLEVELAND.

MEMO: Movements of Japanese Vessels

Rev. Walter de Velder, an American missionary stationed at Amoy, recently arrived from the United States on the S.S. PRESIDENT CLEVELAND, which ship proceeded directly from Kobe to Hong Kong, and was therefore not on its regular route.

He stated that about 2 p.m., March 22, when they were about 16 hours out of Kobe (i.e. about 300 miles) a group of Japanese naval vessels was sighted, which were going through what appeared to be practice maneuvers. They passed near one cruiser and one destroyer, and there were also four or five other armed vessels farther away, some of them quite large, including one aircraft carrier.

According to the gossip on board, the Japanese commander requested the Captain of the PRESIDENT CLEVELAND to avoid the region of the maneuvers, but the latter said he was not altering his course as he was late and taking a short cut. Thereupon the destroyer laid down a smoke screen to hide the naval vessels, which did not interfere with the American ship. There were no other ships in sight, the region being well out of sight of land. The passengers amused themselves by taking many photographs of the Japanese vessels.

J. H. B.

March 28, 1941.

# THE RAYON INDUSTRY OF JAPAN

By

H Wickliffe Rose

Member of State Army Textile Mission to Japan

The Textile Mission to Japan, which recently returned after making its report in Tokyo, was different from those sent to Germany. It was instructed to learn the capacity to produce textiles in Japan, the needs of the Japanese people, and the amount of production that might be made available for export to relieve shortages elsewhere. The Missions to Germany were technical missions to learn about every phase of technology and machinery in the German industry, while this one to Japan was prompted by the world shortage of textiles, particularly cotton, and the thought in the Textile Committee of the Combined Production and Resources Board, composed of representatives of the various Allies, that Japan might contribute to the world needs.

At the same time, in order for the Mission to develop the necessary information on which a report could be based and policy could be formed, it was necessary for it to learn all it could about the entire textile industry of Japan, including technology, machinery, and machinery manufacturers. Thus much of the same type of work as performed by the Mission to Germany was done in Japan, but the report which was left with General MacArthur to be forwarded to the State Department was quite different in nature. It will be the purpose here to give some of the aspects of the rayon industry in Japan not required in that report.

=== Page 3 === (confidence 1.00)
The Textile Mission to Japan was an American mission with foreign observers. The American members were Fred Taylor, Departments of Agriculture and State, Stanley Nehmer of the Department of State, Frank Rowe of Riverside and Dan River Cotton Mills, Harry Bailey, and myself; the observers were Frank Winterbottom of England, Bharat Ram and M. D. Parekh of India, and Andrew S. Young and Sih-Zung Yang of China, who had considerable experience in the textile trade and markets of the Far East.

My assignment was the rayon industry. We decided to work in teams, and as Dr. Parekh was a graduate of M.I.T. in chemical engineering including rayon, I found him to be a most valuable team-mate to survey that industry. When we went into the field to see the plants, I would go through with the management, taking photographs and noting technology, machines, layout, capacity, materials, research, labor, costs, and such general information, while Dr. Parekh went through with the chief chemist and noted procedures, formulae, time, temperature, batches, and so on. We would then combine our notes afterward into a report on each plant.

We had more than a month's notice before the Mission left the United States and I used the time to gather all possible information on the rayon industry of Japan. Colonel Stephen Kennedy of the Office of the Quartermaster General, who did such excellent work in organizing the textile missions to Germany, compiled a questionnaire on the textile industry of Japan, which proved to be most helpful to this mission. We undertook to answer all the questions in it, and so far as rayon is concerned I believe the answers are fairly complete.

There were numerous directories and reports on the rayon industry of Japan in and out of our government offices and I gathered all that I knew about, including those of Stanley Hunt of Textile Economics Bureau.

Studies of FEA and the Tariff Commission on rayon and allied industries, as well as on the trade of Japan were made available to us and we took them with us. In addition, I had the opportunity to see some men who had been in Japan and who were most helpful with information and suggestions.

The Mission left Washington by Air Transport Command, Tuesday, January 15th. The trip to Tokyo took just 50 hours in the air. The elapsed time was five days, after losing a day at Topeka for weather and another at Hamilton Field, San Francisco. We flew in C 54 transports, Douglas four motor planes of forty passenger capacity.

After leaving Hamilton Field we flew day and night, stopping only long enough to refuel. The route was via Hickam Field, Hawaii, Johnston Island, Kwajalein, Guam, and Atsugi Field, Japan, making a total of four stops between the United States and Japan. The shorter route via Wake and Marcus had just been discontinued when we went out.

The trip was interesting and pleasant. We had heard much about bucket seats and dreaded being assigned to such a plane, but after flying across the continent in plush seats we had their nearest equivalent to bucket seats, made of pipe and canvas, on the hop to Hawaii. Within a few minutes after reaching altitude, blankets were spread on the deck and five card games were underway. We found them to be more comfortable and more sociable than the plush jobs in several respects, particularly as to sleeping on the deck, which is difficult in a seat.

We flew over the Rockies at night, by full moon, with snow on the land, and it was an inspiring experience. From Hamilton Field we flew into the setting sun with beautiful cloud formations. In the moonlight on Oahu, the surf and shore, the shoreline drive, the mountains and the city of Honolulu were all plainly visible as we crossed to a landing.

3

=== Page 5 === (confidence 0.99)
at Hickam Field after midnight. There we waited in the palm garden with the full moon directly overhead until the take-off a couple of hours later. At Johnston Island we had breakfast under a sky of all colors surrounded by a sea that was unbelievably blue.

Over the Marshalls we saw a number of atolls, each a gem of many colors surrounded with white surf and set in blue ocean. We were reminded of the fighting that recently had taken place on the atolls by the fact that we could not fly over one where the Japanese were still firing on planes. Kwajalein is only one of the many atolls, and the landing strip is almost all its width from sea to lagoon, but the difference is that the lagoon, about half the size of Long Island Sound, is the largest in the Pacific and it provides a busy harbour at Kwajalein.

We reached Guam after midnight and also after having skipped a day on the calendar. There it was warm and tropical, and the Southern Cross dominated the sky.

After dawn, on the way to Japan we passed over Iwo Jima and a number of volcanic islands, including one called "Lots Wife," which is well named for it resembles a pillar of gleaming salt standing on the ocean.

On the thirty mile ride by bus from Atsugi Field to Tokyo through Yokohama we had our first, and the most vivid impression of what the air raids had done to Japanese cities. Yokohama and Tokyo were practically continuous in industrial plants, docks, and city, but we found them a continuous stretch of destruction. We had seen air views of the results of the raids, and many photographs from the ground, but even from the air the camera is limited in scope, and a drive of an hour straight through thirty miles of rubble gives an impression that does not register in any other way.

4

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.98)
Many concrete buildings remained, and in Tokyo a good part of the central business section escaped, but very little that was industrial or inflammable remained.

Upon traveling more than twenty two hundred miles in Japan and seeing many cities and towns it was evident that Japan's industries were badly damaged. The cultural cities of Kyoto and Nara, and the shrine of the town of Nikko, without war industries, were not destroyed, but the others visited even to small country towns where there had been war plants, and single plants in the country side had been bombed.

Upon arrival in Tokyo we reported to SCAP and made the acquaintance of Major General Wm. F. Marquet, Chief of Economic and Scientific Section, which includes all industry and export import of Major Harold Tate, Chief of the Textile Branch of that Section, and of Richard May, Chief of the Export Import Division. We spent a day in meeting the organization and in getting settled in an office in the Dai Ichi Building, GHQ of SCAP.

On the second day the Mission met with the officials of the Japan Textile Association, the organization which has combined all previous textile control associations and with a Japanese government representative of the Ministry of Commerce and Industry. That association is divided into departments for the various branches of the textile industry.

I made the acquaintance of Mr. Ohata, Head of General Affairs and of the Rayon and Pulp Department. He was formerly of the Teikoku Jinken Co. Ltd., the oldest and largest rayon company in Japan. He was in charge of much of the control of the industry and of the scrapping program which destroyed the major part of the industry during the war. He speaks English quite well, and we spent much time together during my entire stay in Japan. He provided the statistics and information I required of the Association,

5

=== Page 7 === (confidence 0.99)
and arranged meetings and trips for me to get other information from the companies and the plants

The Mission planned its work in three phases; first, a broad survey of the textile industry and of general related information available in Tokyo at the Association the Japanese government, and in SCAP; second a period in Osaka the center of the textile industry, to see the company of ficials and the plants, and finally a return visit in Tokyo to summarize our findings and write the report.

In the first phase I undertook to find out the actual production capacity of the rayon industry in Japan. We had been puzzled here by the tremendous reported capacity which production figures never remotely approached. In 1939 the industry had a registered capacity of over one billion six hundred million pounds, not only considerably more than any other country but more than all the others combined. In 1938, Japan's peak year of rayon production 585 million pounds was only slightly over a third of the capacity registered the following year. It was suspected that in some way this registration was padded but rather than try to sort out the actual from the fictitious since the scrapping of machinery made a physical check of past capacity impossible. We gave the Association a number of categories in which to account for the registered capacity of 1939 and to report the present actual production capacity. The categories were 1939 capacity, Obsolete Scrapped War Damaged Reserved Present Registered, and Present Actual.

The result was that three days after the first meeting with the association we had such a report on the rayon industry, which proved upon surveying the plants to be quite accurate. In the obsolete column all the fictitious registration was written off. The scrapping program proved to be

6

=== Page 8 === (confidence 0.99)
far more destructive to the rayon industry than the air raids, and together
the scrapping and the obsolete category accounted for the major portion of
the 1939 billion and six hundred million registered capacity. War damage
accounted for only a nominal percentage, far less than the proportion in
the cotton industry. A reserve of machinery was reported, which capacity
could be placed in position to operate if given some parts and equipment.

We found that the actual production of yarn averages only a certain
percentage of registered capacity, and staple even a smaller percentage.
We were able then to reduce the registered reserve capacity to actual production
quantities and by adding it to the actual capacity reported, to arrive
at a figure which represents Japan's potential annual rayon productive
capacity as of January.

That figure as reported by SCAP in Tokyo, was 354 million pounds,
and it was the essential figure reported as a guide in administering the
rayon industry in Japan. Of that potential capacity 120 million pounds is
in yarn machinery and 234 million in staple. We do not know what percentage
that total is of Japan's past maximum capacity, but we do know that
it is about 60% of her maximum production.

One other statistical point is of interest. The maximum production
of 585 million pounds in 1938 was more than enough to supply the
needs of Japan and her export markets at that time, for in that same year,
while production capacity was still increasing, a large part of yarn production
machinery was sealed and the warehouses were filled with the largest
stocks of rayon fiber and fabric in the history of her industry. A
very good reason for the large stocks was the shrinking market just when
the production of rayon was increasing. The market decline was not due
to any less demand for rayon but to the fact that Japan's invasion of
China closed some of the markets to her goods.

The lesson to be learned from that situation in Japan's rayon industry during the years preceding World War II, is that there is a limit beyond which production of rayon in Japan becomes a burden to her own economy, as well as to that of the rest of the world. Regardless of how the control is established, whether by good business judgement of the Japanese industry management by their association regulations by Japanese Government, or by the Supreme Command of the Allies in the Pacific, there should be some control to prevent another over building of the industry. If there should be a determination of the level at which Japan's rayon, or any other industry should operate, it must take into account the current economic needs of Japan and her potential foreign markets. Our Mission did not make specific recommendations but gave the essential information upon which such decisions can be made.

On February 2nd the Mission moved to Osaka, the center of the textile industry. There we lived at the New Osaka Hotel, the newest in Japan, and had offices in the Sumitomo Building, one of the Zaibatsu companies which had copper mines and various war industries.

Soon after our arrival the rayon producers gave us a luncheon, and I spent the whole day with them discussing the industry. Any doubts about language were overcome at that meeting. They had appointed as spokesman, Mr. Tashiro, President of Toyo Rayon Co., who had represented Mitsui in New York, and who speaks excellent English. After he had presented a very able summary of the current position of the industry and the discussion had become general, other producers entered into the conversations, and from then on I found no difficulty in conversing with any of the companies. When I made a statement as to the purpose and plan of the Textile Mission in Japan, and they learned that we were there to help them start the industry to operate again and not to close it completely, it was natural to find them.

8

=== Page 10 === (confidence 1.00)
responsive and cooperative. The completely cooperative and friendly attitude encountered by the Mission everywhere in Japan was constant evidence of the successful occupation program which General MacArthur has been conducting in Japan.

It is also interesting that the Japanese are taught English as a required course in public school. While only a minority have had an opportunity to use it, most can make themselves understood in basic subjects. In driving out to Tsuda College, some thirty miles in the country from Tokyo, we asked the way eight times and all but one elderly woman, who shook her head, replied with understandable directions. Furthermore, the directions of all seven agreed, which is better than the average in this country.

At the industry meeting we gave the producers a list of the subjects in which we were interested. We told them that they could give us the information in any form most convenient to them, and that we would fill in any gaps on visits to plants or meetings later. We found in Japan a possible danger of the Japanese being questioned to death. There was an occupation survey of war industry, a survey of all industry, a bombing survey, a reparations survey; there were surveys for science, education, research, and labor; there were surveys for missions, commissions, the Army, the Military Government, and the economists. In Tokyo we found much information that was helpful to our Mission, but some, which had been gathered and might have been useful, had been forwarded to Washington and was not available in Tokyo.

After interviewing staffs of the various companies in Osaka, we undertook to visit their rayon plants. In addition to many silk, cotton, woolen, finishing, and other textile plants, we visited one synthetic fiber plant for polyvinyl alcohol fiber, a calcium carbide plant of interest in

-9-

=== Page 11 === (confidence 1.00)
the production of synthetic fiber, and twenty rayon plants, including all of the operable rayon plants but four, and eight which had been converted to other industries.

As we knew to be the case before the war, we found no acetate rayon production. There had been a small semi works production on Kyushu which had closed when the cotton linters supply ceased. The one cupra rayon plant is still operating, although its capacity suffered under the scrapping program in proportion. All the rest is viscose, with a larger staple capacity than yarn.

The present production is only nominal because of coal and salt shortages. Pulp figures published up to the war show that Japan could produce enough pulp to supply the quantity of rayon capacity which now remains, and reports on the present pulp industry in Japan verify that the capacity is still there.

The present quality of rayon is below that which could be produced, and which can be produced again, given adequate raw materials. The quality before the war was various. Some mills were making excellent yarn and staples, and there were various levels of quality from the best to the worst. No rayon is being bleached at present, as chlorine is not available. No high tenacity is being produced, as coal is too scarce for the hot bath required, and no cotton linters have been available for cupra production or high tenacity yarns since the war started.

Price was formerly based on quality, and the quality of all staple production was determined at the Experimental Station of the Textile Industry, at Yokohama. A committee of the Ministry of Commerce and Industry of the government determined the price at one of five levels for each product of rayon staple offered for sale. They had a detailed standard for grading. Not only did grades below standard quality receive a lower level of price,

10

=== Page 12 === (confidence 1.00)
but grades above the standard were awarded premium prices.

Tests for staple strength and elongation are made on McKenzie single fiber testers. Yarn tests are made in the single on a Japanese instrument similar to Suder, and in the skein on one similar to Scott and to Goodbrand, arc type, constant rate, varying load. They have no incline testers, although the single fiber tester with a rocker arm is similar in results. The standard rayon test is at 65% relative humidity, 20° C. The room is air conditioned, double windowed, and on a separate air system from the rest of the building. Instrument tables are of solid concrete 4 3/4 inches thick, on pillars resting upon rock foundation. The floors are cork covered.

This Yokohama Station also conducts research in textiles for the benefit of the entire industry. It has four departments, with complete textile equipment for experimentation. First is research on physical properties of fibers, yarns, and fabrics which uses the testing laboratory already mentioned. Second is research in pulp and in artificial fibers, viscose and acetate yarn and staple. The third is research in spinning on the silk system only, tow breaking on the Perlok type of system, winding, weaving, and hosiery knitting. The fourth is scouring, dyeing, finishing, and bleaching.

In this Station we saw the negative results of attempts to make textile fiber from mulberry trees. Those trees were largely cut during the war to force the farming population to seek employment in war industries.

They were dyeing cotton with acid dyes after impregnating thiourea. In printing they were getting complete penetration of the fibers and fabric by the use of wetting agents. They were printing scarves and handkerchiefs by rotary block printing, an Alsace-Lorraine machine, and copper roll printing on a machine by Alsacienne de Construction Mechaniques of Mulhouse. They

-11-

=== Page 13 === (confidence 1.00)
were screen printing through silk bolting cloth and through wax paper stencils on bolting cloth. This screen printing included designs in fabric effects by making the screen from the photograph of such intricate textiles as lace.

A new plisse method was being perfected using calcium nitrate or calcium chloride on silk. Calcium thiocyanide had been found to be too toxic and the nitrate is most favored. Japanese patent No. 111,700 covers the process.

They were making a combination silk and rayon in which the former was creped and the latter not. By the use of a stencil, with such a pattern as polka dots, the silk was shrunk by chemicals which did not shrink the rayon.

At the Kyoto Imperial University we visited the Institute of Fiber Chemistry, a part of the Engineering Department, and a sister of the Institute for Chemical Research, which is also a part of the university organization. We spent an afternoon with Professor Kita, the founder, now retired, and Professor Horio, the present Director of Research of Kurashiki Kenshoku Co., one of the most progressive of the rayon companies.

At this Institute much research on rayon has been done and is in progress, and polyvinyl alcohol fiber also is a subject of research. Low caustic is the largest and most active project on rayon at present. Caustic is so scarce that the project was born of necessity. The work involves the entire industry and industry wide meetings have been held within the past year to discuss and compare results.

Low acid and low temperature bath is a research project found active in the industry as a result of the low caustic.

Most of the industry was found to be making xanthate and viscose.

12

=== Page 14 === (confidence 1.00)
in a closed single vessel, a system found in Germany by the missions there. One plant was found to be making rayon by the short age method, without stopping the flow of alkali cellulose for aging, but keeping it in motion practically all the way into viscose.

Cake washing was found in one rayon plant, but most are still processing skeins.

Continuous steeping was found in one plant, but they preferred the steep press method, possibly because of the quality of the pulp available. The continuous steeping was with powdered pulp, which was said to be of a grade not entirely suited to the process.

Many rayon staple producers finish the fiber before cutting. Some produce it both ways, depending upon the type of staple required. Excellent crimped viscose staple is being produced, and high tenacity staple has been produced also. The finishing of the fiber bundle continuously is the process with the greatest variety of machines found in Japan. There were hardly any two alike. Only where the fiber bundle was to go through the Perlok type of process was it dried in that form. One such dryer was seen, while all the rest were staple dryers.

Many rayon mills in Japan are integrated, and some are integrated backwards as well as forwards. Plants were found which included on the same grounds sulphuric acid, caustic soda, and carbon disulphide production. Two rayon plants, by the way, included carbon disulphide recovery units which were extremely simple. Some plants included textile mills for spinning and weaving. One plant had a soybean protein extraction mill, and they produced a 30% protein viscose rayon staple. The plant of Toyo Rayon at Matsuyama, on Shikoku, is the only one of its kind in the world. It goes from wood pulp to finished garments. The garments at the present time are allocated by the Home Department of the Japanese Government through the Governor of the Prefecture

13

=== Page 15 === (confidence 1.00)
to the many homeless people.

In quantity it seems that Japan will not be able to flood the markets of the world again at least for many years. In quality their rayon and rayon pulp can be improved in some respects, and the improvements probably will be made when materials are again available and they can operate. In price, they will probably continue below the rest of the rayon producing countries, even though all costs have risen since the war. In technology they have made a number of improvements, although the machines were all imported originally and copied. In research they are active and eager for contacts with the rest of the world from which they have been isolated for nearly five years. In finance they are threatened by the huge cost of maintaining organizations and plants with only nominal production, or none at all.

We can only touch upon some of the outstanding subjects here. Fuller details on the rayon industry of Japan will be released later. Meanwhile, in closing, I wish to mention a strong impression I gained while on this mission in Japan. Everywhere we went, Army and Military Government officers and men were found doing a conscientious job, in many cases working twelve hours a day every day in the week. The occupation is a thankless job for the individual, for it is almost impossible to see results as soon as one would desire. The occupation is a tremendous undertaking, with little opportunity for full recognition of all the individual effort that goes into it. But so far, in spite of great difficulties, it is successful, and we at home can work to keep it so. It is as important to support the occupation, which will be necessary for some time to come, as it was to support the war effort of our forces fighting abroad. Not only do they need young men abroad to fill the ranks of the occupying forces, but they need men from various lines of industry who know the business involved. Just as men with special experience

14

=== Page 16 === (confidence 1.00)
and ability were loaned to our government during the war, so should companies be willing, if called upon to loan them now for the occupation abroad.

We found everywhere in Japan an open and inquiring mind about our country and the principles on which it operates. We were asked particularly about democracy and what it means. It made us aware of the fact that the United States is an example, which is being studied by others, and it made us aware also of what a tremendous responsibility we have in that position.

My dear Mr. Leavitt:

Thank you for your letter of December 3, 1946 inquiring about the
importation of ramie from Japan.

At present, no direct trade or correspondence is permitted between
private firms in Japan and those of other nations. When the situation
there becomes less unsettled, it will be the policy of the Department of
State to encourage private trade. For the moment, all importing is being
handled by a subsidiary of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, the
U.S. Commercial Company. You may address your inquiries to it, in care
of the Marketing Division, Tempo "T" Building, Washington 25, D.C.

I do not believe that any ramie, cipher in finished fibre or the
grass form, has yet been imported, due to the difficulties encountered in
reviving the industry, but it should not be long before you can purchase
it again. Meanwhile, I suggest that you write to the U.S. Commercial
Company and ask when it schedules such importations. It would be well to
state in detail the quantities and types you desire.

Sincerely yours,

Memorandum for: The Chief, Scientific and Technical Division, ESS, GHQ, SCAP.

Subject: Availability of silkworm eggs for supply to India for experimental purposes.

With a view to improve cotton producing industry in India a representation has been made to this Mission to approach the Scientific and Technical Division of the SCAP whether there is some possibility of importing about 25-30 moths in each of four original races (P2 eggs) from Japan. These type of eggs were very popular in India in the past and some of them were actually imported in 1930. It is understood that the Agricultural Experimental Stations in Japan have evolved a large number of races of which about 65 original races are now in popular use. Under the provisions of article 13 of Japanese Raw Silk Industry Law exports of silkworm eggs are prohibited without the sanction of a competent minister as provided by an Ordinance.

2. In the circumstances, the Scientific and Technical Division of SCAP is requested kindly to examine the above proposal and let this Mission know the decision in the matter at an early date.

(J. Chatterjee)
Registrar (Trade)

SUBJECT: Availability of Silkworm Eggs

1. Reference is made to memoranda from the Indian Liaison Mission, No. TCE (3)/49, dated 11 April 1949, subject, Availability of Silkworm Eggs for Supply to India for Experimental Purposes, and No. TCE (3)/49, dated 1 July 1949, subject, Availability of Silkworm Seeds for Supply to India.

2. It should be noted that Mr. H. S. Venkatarao held conferences sometime last April with the Scientific and Technical Section of the Economic and Scientific Section, requesting technical information and access to certain specimens of Japanese silkworm seeds. At that time he was informed to submit a letter to SGAP requesting the exportation of certain silkworm seeds. On 11 April, the Indian Liaison Mission forwarded to Chief, Scientific and Technical Division, ESS, a memorandum requesting whether it was possible to secure about 25-50 moths in each of four original races (P2 eggs) from Japan. When this letter was received, it was forwarded to the Foreign Trade and Commerce Division to obtain information as to the availability of the silkworm egg seeds. Due to the fact that all silkworm egg seeds come under the general heading of the Japanese silk industry, a letter in turn was forwarded to the Textile Division, attention of Mr. J. Kern.

3. On the 3rd of June, Mr. Venkatarao discussed with Mr. Kern the possibility of obtaining silkworm seeds, and Mr. Kern, after consultation with the Japanese Government, informed Mr. Venkatarao that there was a shortage of silkworm seeds in Japan, and therefore, at this time there are none available for export. Because of the fact that Mr. Venkatarao was leaving Japan immediately, his reply to Mr. Kern was that the verbal answer submitted to him was sufficient and it would not be necessary to answer the memorandum from the Indian Liaison Mission, due to the fact that he had instigated it and would have left Japan by the time a written answer could be received.

4. Therefore, no formal reply was made at Mr. Venkatarao's request. However, due to the fact that the memorandum from the Indian

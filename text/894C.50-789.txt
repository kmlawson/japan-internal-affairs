REPORCING IN STAFF

The Honorable

American Ambassador,
Tokyo, Japan.

I have the honor to submit the following report on the Manchu-Japanese Economic Conference which was convened at Dairen on August 15 and terminated on August 17.

According to the press the Conference was attended by 120 "Manchu" delegates, and Japanese from Dairen and Japan, principally Osaka and Nagoya. The first day and a half of the Conference was devoted to addresses and sight-seeing; the second day to sight-seeing and committee meetings. Lieutenant General Koiso, Commander in Chief of the Kwantung Army, made the opening address. His thesis was the economic bloc, as indicated by the following remarks made at the beginning of his speech:

It may seem unbecoming for a soldier to speak on the weighty problem of an economic bloc between Japan and Manchoukuo. But in a new country the problems of economic welfare and national defence are closely interwoven. Prosperity means security.

This statement I believe expresses the Kwantung Army's policy in Manchuria quite accurately and succinctly. As General Koiso's address was the most interesting one given during the Conference a translation of the major portion is enclosed. On the second morning of the Conference, Count Hayashi, President of the South Manchuria Railway Company, spoke on the "Manchuria-Japan Economic Bloc and the Mission of the South Manchuria Railway Company; Mr. Chang, "Manchukuo" Minister of Industry described the "Natural Resources of Manchoukuo"; Mr. Hoshino of the "Manchukuo" Ministry of Finance, read a paper on the "Financial Condition of Manchoukuo", and Mr. Kusaka explained the "Administration and Economic Position of the Kwantung Leased Territory". These speeches revealed nothing, either from the standpoint of fact or policy, that is not already well known. This portion of the Conference might best be described as a lecture course for the 120 "Manchu" delegates who, to use the words of the MANCHURIA DAILY NEWS reporter,

"merely listened to eminent speakers, who were heard with respectful attention and applauded at the end of their carefully prepared oration. Perfect harmony featured the session ..."

Six committees were appointed to discuss (1) communications, (2) taxes and finance, (3) marketing and customs duties, (4) mining, water resources, trade marks, and weights and measures, (5) agriculture, animal husbandry, and fisheries, and (6) immigration, land leasing, and extraterritoriality. A Japanese was chairman of each of the Committees. The meetings were closed to the press

and no authoritative information is obtainable concerning the deliberations or decisions, if such there were. The local press, however, commenting on their work states that group one heard an "illuminating discourse on the unified telephone and telegraph system"; that group two discussed gold and silver standards and taxation but reached no decision; that group three heard pleas for more reasonable tariff rates on special Chinese imports from "Manchu" delegates who were informed by the Dairen Customs Commissioner that there was no discrimination against Chinese goods; that group four discussed a variety of subjects from railway and electric light rates to weights and measures; that group five held a "quiet ruminating session" (sic) discussing agricultural problems; and that with regard to group six, dealing with extraterritoriality and land leases, an "earnest discussion on the matters could be heard coming through the open windows". Committee meetings were held on the afternoon of the 16th and the morning of the 17th. It is probable that the lack of information concerning the deliberations is due to the fact that nothing of a concrete or constructive nature was accomplished.

"Manchukuo" import duties on Japanese goods were, I understand, the subject of earnest and pointed private discussion between the delegates from Osaka and Nagoya and the Japanese officials associated with "Manchukuo". The Osaka "lobby", dissatisfied with the recent partial alterations in the import tariff, were emphatic in their request for lower duties on their products, using the economic

economic block idea as a talking point. I am informed that the Deiren Customs Commissioner and Japanese in the Hsinching Government do not favor further reductions in duties.

I was not favorably impressed with the calibre or representative character of the "Manchu" delegates. I observed them on sight-seeing parties several times and had an opportunity of talking with a few of them at a large garden party to which foreign consuls and business men were invited. It was obvious however that they were impressed by the reception they received and by the orderly development they observed in Kwantung. And as this was no doubt the purpose for which they were invited, the Conference may be called a success.

Respectfully yours,

John Carter Vincent
American Consul.

Lacour No. 1 to despatch of American Consul at Dairen, Manchuria, dated August 23, 1933, to American Embassy, Tokyo, entitled "Manchu-Japanese Economic Conference".

Translation of Lieutenant-General Keise's Speech at the Manchu-Japanese Economic Conference, as Printed in the MANCHURIA DAILY NEWS of August 15, 1933.

"It may seem unbecoming for a soldier to speak on the weighty problem of an economic bloc between Japan and Manchoukuo. But in a new country the problems of economic welfare and national defense are closely interwoven. Prosperity means security.

"You gentlemen come from diverse sections of Manchuria, Korea and Japan, because you are all deeply interested in the mighty question confronting us. Our countries are bound by fate to stand and live together.

## Free Trade Dispersed

"Japan is Manchoukuo's life-line as well as Manchoukuo is Japan's life-line in both military and economic senses. The doctrine of an economic bloc is fashionable at present in many parts of the earth, because the pressing economic factors have blown away the old theory of free trading.

"The capitalistic economic organism of Japan can hardly be transplanted into Manchuria. Its good points may be adopted, but unsuitable items may be kept out. After all, the natural resources and environments of the two lands are different. They complement each other to form an economic whole.

## Communication Urgent

"One key to prosperity of Manchoukuo is a swift and systematic development of communication. This is a land of great distances. Harbin, Hsinking and Mukden have the potentialities for great cities of the future. Particularly, Harbin has a glorious future in store. It stands in the center of an extensive, fertile agricultural empire.

"Cheaper freight by rail, and quick progress in river and highway transport development is essential to the country's well-being. We are building 5,000 kilometres of modern roads this year, and expect to complete 60,000 kilometres within next ten years. Navigable rivers are now being surveyed, and improvements upon them projected.

"Immense cotton plantations in various areas south of Mukden are also planned. When about one-third of the arable land thereabouts is planted to cotton, Japan need no longer import any cotton from America or India. The project may be feasible.

"The colonization project cannot be pushed recklessly. We must choose the right kind of colonists for environments suited to them. The future development of mining and timber industries are under consideration.

"The social problem of abolishing extrality, further progress of coal, gas, electric, glass, cement and iron industries are all urgent matters.

"The economic structure of Japan need not be broken to advance Manchuria's welfare, but it needs some modifications. Take the matter of the Fushun coal exports. People with insular minds wanted to shut out all Manchuria products that may inconvenience home industries.

"The charge that the Army people are too autocratic is wrong. The task we have on hand concerns the very life of Japan and Manchuria. Personal gains or personal ambition must be sacrificed on the altar of that ideal".

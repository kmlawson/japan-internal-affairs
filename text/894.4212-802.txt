It was toward the close of the war, in 1918, that students in Japan became actively interested in social theories. There had been, of course, academic interest shown by individual students before this time, but it was not until 1918 that interest became enthusiastic and organized. The immediate cause was Woodrow Wilson's championship of democracy, which had become at that time a world issue. Thinking Japanese students ranged themselves eagerly on the side of either democracy or imperialism. It was in this year that the first student societies for the study of social problems were formed, which later were to arouse so much trepidation among officials.

Forces were at work, however, which soon caused the students to abandon their more or less theoretical division with respect to democracy. At the close of the war the period of war prosperity brought many new rich; their flaunting of wealth emphasized more vividly than ever before the unequal apportionment of wealth in Japan. This was followed in 1920 by the beginning of the post-war depression, which continues to the present time, giving students grave cause to contemplate the probable poverty that lies before some of them after graduation under existing conditions, despite their expensive equipment of education. The echoes of the German and the Russian revolutions, especially the latter, reached Japan, the Soviet experiment firing the imagination of many. It was during this period, also, that labor unions entered the most important and expansive phase of their development, to be followed later by the

the growth of political proletarian parties. Interest in political theories was also advanced by the activities of adult groups working for "universal manhood suffrage", which was finally realized in 1925, and later by the injustice which many students felt they suffered by the imposition by the authorities of required military training for students. By 1920 interest in the theory of democracy had given way to theories which the students felt were more practically related to the problems confronting them. Syndicalism, socialism, Marxism, and many other "isms" found their advocates. Before long, however, Marxism overshadowed in importance all other theories among students. Now the term is frequently used as an equivalent for radicalism. The word "Marx boy", pronounced as in English, has entered the Japanese language to describe in general a radical student, and more in particular a student who, more or less thoughtlessly, advocates Marxism because it happens to be a slogan popular among students.

This rapidly increasing interest of students in social theories was illustrated by the development of student societies for the study of social problems. In 1922, for example, on the anniversary of the Russian Revolution, there was formed a students' union of societies in universities for the study of social sciences, while in January, 1923, a union of similar societies existing in higher schools was formed. In 1924 these unions, representing twenty-three schools, met; and in the meeting of the following year fifty-nine schools were represented.

In this regard it is interesting to note that there has occurred in recent years a change in the centers of radicalism among students. Whereas formerly private schools were regarded as the more prolific incubators of "dangerous thoughts", today the authorities regard the imperial universities as the chief offenders. Students of private schools, on the other hand, are said to have become increasingly conservative. Among the imperial universities the students at the Imperial University of Tokyo are viewed as the most extreme. As it is from this university that the higher civil service is to a large degree recruited, it is not surprising that in this instance the authorities display some anxiety. The conservatism of students of the private schools, including the various Christian institutions, has apparently redounded to their advantage, for recently the Government has been more favorably disposed toward these schools, and has in several instances raised colleges to the grade of university, which, were it not for this unexpected development, might very probably have been withheld.

It is impossible to hazard an opinion regarding the extent or seriousness of the spread of radical ideas among students. That the authorities have for some years regarded the situation as grave has been shown by the severe measures adopted. There is, however, an almost complete lack of reliable and detailed information as the Government maintains secrecy with respect to that which it discovers or believes it discovers. The Western observer is frequently conscious of the feeling that what

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
-6-

what the Japanese authorities regard as a "dangerous thought" he himself would regard as scarcely more than the normal outlook of youth. A younger teacher in a higher school recently said that most of the students in higher schools are pink to the extent that they sympathize with the lower classes, which is only to be expected during the idealistic period of a young man's development, and that were free discussion of social theories permitted there would exist no problem.

At a conference of the principals of the twenty-five government higher schools in Japan and officials of the Department of Education at the end of last month, to consider the Government's new plan already mentioned, a report was made on the thirteen "disturbing" incidents which occurred at these schools during 1929, a year which, according to the head of the Students' Bureau of the Department of Education, is regarded as the most ominous yet. A description of each of these incidents appeared in the press. Brief mention of the character of some of these incidents may be of interest as indicating the apparently harmless nature of the conditions which are giving apprehension to the officials, at least when consideration is based on the material available. Several incidents arose from dissatisfaction of students with the conduct of school sports; in two cases students opposed the dismissal of fellow students who were alleged to be communists; in another school the students opposed the dismissal of a student caught cheating in an examination; several cases were the result of the desire of students to have examinations postponed or the holidays following

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
-7-

following examinations extended; at one school the students demanded the resignation of the principal because he made a slight error in reading the Imperial Rescript on Education.

If these incidents were merely what the press reports, they would seem to give little cause for great alarm. The press added, however, that the incidents "occurred following instructions from unknown quarters" and that it is rumored that communists are behind them, causing the authorities no little concern. The official of the Department of Education referred to above stated that only university students are in the background of the activities of higher school students. Whatever may be true in this regard, it does seem that the authorities have hitherto failed to realize that radicalism is a prerogative of youth which soon loses its fire when the students, after graduation, become engrossed in the difficulties of earning a living. Nor have the authorities seemed to realize in the past that to forbid the students study of social theories arouses their curiosity, increases their interest, and presents an attractive opportunity for martyrdom. Again, such a ban is probably the more irritating to the students as they know that similar study is freely permitted the students of enlightened Western nations.

The popularity of radical theories does not, however, rest entirely upon the fact that organizations for their study are forbidden. The economic situation in Japan probably gives a number of thinking students pause. A year ago the Department of Finance published figures which

which showed that of the approximately 12,000,000 families of Japan Proper the monthly income of 93.1 per cent. was less than Yen 100 ($50) per month; of 5.7 per cent. between Yen 100 and Yen 400 per month; and of only 1.2 per cent. over Yen 400 per month. In addition to the sympathy which thinking youth normally has for the under dog, there is said to be an awareness on the part of some students that their chances of entering the small privileged class are few and that their lot may be eventually cast with the low-salaried or proletarian classes. This feeling may be enhanced by the unemployment among graduates which has in recent years caused considerable concern in Japan. It would not be surprising if this situation adds to the interest of students in the study of social theories not intended to benefit the capitalist class. An article was recently published by a teacher of the Second Higher School of Waseda University, a private institution. The author wrote that such statements as the following are common among radical students, showing their at least temporary identification of themselves with the proletarian classes: "Present conditions drive students from the bourgeoisie to the proletariat"; "Formerly universities sought to make us scholars or members of the privileged class while now graduates face difficulties in obtaining food or employment and are exploited by the capitalist class"; "Present society destroys our ancestral homes and casts us out into the world," (a reference apparently to the gradual disintegration of the family system under modern industrialization); "Students feel that under modern capitalistic conditions

It would seem, however, that there has at least occurred a change, perhaps only slight, in the attitude of the authorities toward the thought of students. Recently the Minister of Home Affairs publicly stated that the authorities should be more lenient toward students. Several educators have said that they have sensed already a slackening in official restraint, as yet perhaps too vague to be described with exactitude. The recent presentation to the principals of the higher schools of a Government plan for a new policy of direction of student thought also indicates what is possibly a turn toward liberalism in the official attitude. The plan in part, according to an official of the Department of Education, has already been put into effect in some higher schools while in other schools it will be initiated gradually from April, 1930, the beginning of the next school year. The plan will be confined to higher schools for the reason that, in the opinion of the authorities, students in schools of lower grade are not much interested in radical ideas, while students at present in universities are too near the end of their period of study to be sufficiently affected.

Under this plan the authorities intend by four means to bring the students to orthodoxy of thought. (1) They apparently feel that the majority of those higher school students who become so-called radicals are those who suffer from poverty, illness, or who have some other reason for unhappiness or discontent. Aid is to be extended

=== Page 10 === (confidence 1.00)
-10-

tended to such students. (2) The authorities believe that many of these students lack acquaintance with the best that the present political system in Japan produces. The teachers are to be instructed how to be more than mere classroom pedagogues. They are to enter the life of the students outside the classroom so that the students will have firsthand opportunity of communion with "gentlemen". (3) Organizations that exist in the schools today for the development of the minds, spirits, and bodies of students will be improved so that through them they may be helped to a healthy viewpoint.

The fourth measure to be undertaken is perhaps less vague than the three just described. Lectures on radical social theories, including Marxism, are to be given by instructors despatched by the Department of Education so that the students may "cultivate and develop the power of sound judgment and understanding of social questions and attain healthy mental development."

The details of this official plan have apparently not yet been worked out. A printed copy of the outline of the plan which formed the basis of discussion at the conference was refused a member of my staff on the plea that it was still "secret". The significance of the plan rests, of course, on the manner in which it is carried through. According to the press some principals said they would object if students were to be permitted under the lecture system to form their own judgments regarding the rightness or wrongness of Marxism. Although this was denied by the above-mentioned official, it seems quite possible that similar objection may be raised

-11-

raised by other reactionary officials. One teacher of a higher school stated a few days ago that the students are very dissatisfied that the lectures are to be given by men chosen by the Department of Education. It is felt that such lectures will dwell on only the least harmful points of social theories.

The vagueness of the plan at present brings uneasiness to some of those who hope for a more liberal attitude toward students. It is felt that it may retrograde into little more than a few perfunctory lectures which will satisfy reactionary officials and which will be avoided by thinking students. At this moment the plan is chiefly of interest in revealing an inclination toward liberalism in the present Government and in indicating that the officials realize that the past ten years of oppression of students has not been a policy above criticism nor entirely successful.

of 30 percent in the number of students admitted into the private universities and professional schools in the faculties of law, literature, politics, economics, and commerce. The announced purpose is to reduce the number of educated people who are unable to find employment. There is an over-supply of people educated for the professions and the Government considers that they form a dangerous radical element in the country. In order to compensate the institutions for the loss of tuition fees it is proposed that they be subsidized to the extent of 70 per cent of this loss, for which an annual appropriation of Yen 1,000,000 will be asked of the Diet.

The fact that this scheme affects only private institutions, and not Government universities where there are believed to be just as many radically-minded students, raised doubts in the minds of many as to whether the reasons stated in the announcement of the Department of Education are the real reasons for forming the project. It is believed by many that with one or two exceptions the private universities of Japan have allowed their standards of instruction and of discipline to fall to a very low standard. It is stated that in many institutions students are allowed to matriculate, attend classes seldom, and yet obtain degrees. The JAPAN ADVERTISER suggests this as the reason for putting forward the scheme.

There has been no sign of making the project effective immediately.

A group of distinguished Japanese scholars has been trying for some months, to make arrangements for the accommodation in Japanese universities of German-Jewish savants. It is not a Japanese national project, and so far as I know the Japanese Government has nothing to do with it, other than to pass upon requests that German-Jewish professors accepting the invitation to take up residence in Japan be assigned to posts in Government universities.

SIR:

This is an excellent example of a certain form of writing which, by casual reference to things not commonly known, tries to give an impression of profound knowledge on the part of the writer.

It may be remarked that the Japanese people know nothing at first-hand of the Jews as a race. If the Japanese, individually or collectively, view the Jews with disfavor, the responsibility lies with the Occidentals who have given the Japanese an unfavorable impression of the Jews.

The animosity of the Japanese toward Racjmann was primarily caused by the alleged fact that it was he who induced the Chinese Government to refer the Sino-Japanese conflict to the League of Nations, and that he, as an official of the League, sent forward despatches from Shanghai to Geneva which were unduly favorable to the Chinese contention. Similarly, Mr. Hass is disliked by the Japanese for the reason that they supposed him to have been unduly biased in favor of the Chinese. The fact that both Hass and Racjmann are Jews was merely additional grist for the Japanese mill.

Variations from type exist among the Japanese as well as among other people.

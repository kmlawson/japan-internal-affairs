Dear Sir:

This is an urgent request to have the new "The New Purchase of Territory" introduced into international discussions. This to take the place of "aggrandizement" in diplomatic calculations.

People have long known that Japan cannot survive on those few little islands. No diplomat seems ever to have considered a sale to her, with its attendant good feeling. All of them sit back, knowing she must expand and wondering which way she may strike.

I therefore submit this plan to your consideration.

Query in re. alleged occupation of Kurile Islands.

Can the Department of State (or archives) inform me whether or not (and I am chiefly interested in a positive negative to my query) one, or any, of the Kurile Islands have ever been occupied and held in the name of the U. S., and under its flag, temporarily?

According to all the information I can get, these islands have been claimed, and the most southerly ones held without question and uninterruptedly, by Japan, by right of ancient possession for centuries. There was for many years a dispute between Japan and Russia with regard to other islands in the group (of which there are 32 in all); but the dispute was adjusted in 1875 by the cession of Sakhalin to Russia, by Japan, in exchange for a clear title from Russia to Japan of the whole of the Kuriles group.

My reason for asking the questionis to settle an argument between myself and a gentleman here who claims that the Kuriles were once held in the name of the U. S. by a high naval officer, and thereafter relinquished to Japan under a ruling from the then Secretary of State that we had no authority to take them.

I think the gentleman has the Kuriles confused with the Bonin Islands, which were once occupied by Commodore Perry; or to Tai-wan (Formosa), over which our flag was raised in 1857, and which Dr. Peter Parker, a medical missionary, urged that we hold. Both these irregular actions were repudiated by the Secretary of State.

I find references to the Bonin and Tai-wan seizures, but no allusion whatsoever to a seizure of the Kuriles.

R. Charlton Wright

3/10 Ninth St North. St Petersburg, Fla.

No. January 18, 1936.

Sir:

The Japanese Minister for Foreign Affairs advises me that Lieutenant Nobu Shirase, Imperial Japanese Army, retired, who commanded the Japanese Antarctic Expedition in 1912, wishes to tender to Rear Admiral Richard Evelyn Byrd, United States Navy, retired. Commander of the Byrd Antarctic Expedition, the expression of his high appreciation of the broad-mindedness and honorable attitude with which the Admiral has recognized the accomplishments of the Shirase Expedition more than 80 years ago.

As is authoritatively stated in "Mankyokuki", his account of the Expedition of 1912, Lieutenant Shirase penetrated to points as far as Latitude 80°06' South, Longitude 156°37' West, and Latitude 76°06' South, Longitude 151°20' West. On that occasion, he discovered two large bays lying along the ice barrier to the eastward of the Bay of Hales, which he names

=== Page 31 === (confidence 1.00)
he named Kaiman Bay and Okuma Bay respectively. In 1919, Admiral Byrd, without the slightest knowledge of the fact that the bays had been already discovered and named by Lieutenant Shirase, gave them the names of Helen Washington Bay and Hal Flood Bay respectively. At a later date, however, Admiral Byrd learned of Lieutenant Shirase's discovery, and on the map attached to his article "Exploring the Ice Age in Antarctica", in the National Geographic Magazine, October, 1935, he restored the Japanese names to these waters.

In compliance with the earnest request of Lieutenant Shirase, my Government instructed me to take such steps as may be permissible so that his expression of appreciation may be conveyed to Admiral Byrd through the good offices of the Government of the United States of America.

I am therefore taking the liberty of addressing this note to you with the hope that you will kindly see your way to have Lieutenant Shirase's message transmitted to the desired destination.

Accept, Sir, the renewed assurances of my highest consideration.

The Honorable Cordell Hull
Secretary of State

THE EXPANSION OF JAPAN

1874 Japan secured by treaty China's relinquishment of the Loochoo Islands.

1875 Japan obtained from Russia the Southern Kurile Islands in exchange for the southern half of Saghalin.

1876 Bonin Islands incorporated as a part of Japanese dominions.

1879 The Loochoo Islands not already included in Kagoshima Prefectorate incorporated.

1895 Formosa and the Pescadores ceded by China, after war, to Japan.

1905 Saghalin seized during Russo-Japanese War. The portion south of the 50th parallel was ceded to Japan by the Portsmouth Treaty.

1905 Russia's rights over Port Arthur, the Kwangtung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway transferred to Japan.

1910 Annexation of Korea.

1914 (October) Seizure of German insular colonies in the Pacific. Confirmed by Treaty 7 V. 1937.

1914 (November) Capture of Kiaochow and seizure of all German holdings in Shantung Province, China. 2nd copy

1915 (January) Demands upon China. (May) Agreements extending Japan's interests and holdings in Manchuria; confirming Japan's rights in her newly acquired position in Shantung; and conceding various privileges in China and in connection with the Chinese administration.

1931 Military occupation of Manchuria undertaken and subsequent creation of "Manchukuo".

1933. Occupation of Jehol and subsequent infiltration into Chahar

1933. Establishment of demilitarized zone in North China extending from south of the Great Wall to Peiping and eastward to Tientsin.

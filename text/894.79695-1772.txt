15 June 1946

The Honorable
The Secretary of State

Dear Mr. Secretary:

Pursuant to recent informal discussions between your Civil Aviation Division and the opposite in the Air Staff, there are transmitted herewith applications of the Transcontinental &amp; Western Air, Inc. and Asia Airways, Inc., for permission to establish between Japan and Korea, either as contract carriers for the Army Air Forces, or as certificated civil carriers, certain air services.

Because General MacArthur has determined that the military air transport available to him is adequate to meet the requirements of the Occupation Forces, it is evident that these applications, as well as any others for permission to operate in the same territory, including those of the other member Nations of the United Nations Organization, should be dealt with on the basis of national policy, rather than solely from the standpoint of military necessity.

Our informal discussions reveal that the study aimed at the establishment of a national policy now is under way within the State Department and that the results of the study will be submitted to the Air Coordinating Committee for consideration.

I am entirely in accord with this manner of progressing the matter and shall be glad to furnish any information or advice which may be helpful in the course of the study.

Sincerely yours,

Prospectus for Asia Airways, Inc.

## Description of Corporation

Asia Airways was incorporated in the State of Delaware with registry in the State of New York in March 1946. Its principle offices are located at 67 Broad Street, New York City. When operations are begun, the principle offices will be located in the Orient. As provided by the corporation laws of Delaware, Asia Airways, Inc. is authorized to issue 1,000 shares of stock at no-par value. The principle objective of the corporation is to provide air transportation within Japan and from Japan to adjacent countries of Asia. The corporation anticipates a feeder contract with the large trans-Pacific American aircraft certified to and within this area.

## Extent of Operations

Asia Airways, operating four C-47 type aircraft, proposes to serve the principal population centers of the main islands of Japan. The towns to be served include: Fukuoka, Osaka, Tokyo and Sapporo on a daily basis beginning in July 1946, or as soon as permitted. In accordance with SCAP directive of 18 November 1945 all personnel connected with aircraft operations, maintenance and crews will be ex-U.S.A.A.F. personnel. The service is designed to help alleviate the critical transportation shortage in Japan, thereby assisting the occupation forces in their mission of building the economic foundations of a peaceful Japan. However, the controls of civil aviation will be vested in an entirely American company which will both prevent the Japanese from again taking to the air and at the same time provide an outlet for the generally overtaxed surface transportation facilities of Japan.

## Necessity for the Service

There is a semi-breakdown in Japanese surface transportation due to loss of ships, rail and motor equipment during the war. Japanese railroad officials estimate that a five-year period will be required to bring rail beds, equipment, etc. up to a maximum point of efficiency. A similar period may well be required for motor and sea transport. All types of transport equipment have been grossly overworked during the war and will require replacement. It will be extremely difficult for the Japanese to do this under present conditions. The decrease in coal production has already caused a severe curtailment in government railway schedules. The load formerly carried by ships is now thrown on to other forms of transportation. Just a minimum portion of this can be returned to the sea in the foreseeable future.

There is an urgent need in both Japanese government and private business quarters for the rapid transportation of highly important personnel, documents and express. It should be noted that Asia Airways does not propose to operate a luxury service but simply the bare essentials of air transport. The ships will be unconverted C-47 transports with bucket seats, thus allowing a maximum pay load of passengers and air freight. Courier service (formerly provided the Japanese government by the Fifth Air Force) records show that 150 lbs. of government business documents are carried on each ship per four routes per day. The general slowness of government mail and tele-

graph facilities would lead to a wide useage of air facilities by private enterprise which is not now represented in the above figures. In addition, air transport would be widely used by Japanese newspapers which formerly maintained their own planes to insure speed in news service, thus contributing to the freedom and quick dissemination of knowledge within Japan. The same would be true of motion picture films.

Under present conditions in Japan it requires 30 hours travel by rail to the Kyushu area. Great inconvenience is experienced in travel to Hokkaido because of the infrequency of ferry service between Honshu and Hokkaido. Because of the geomorphology of Japan, land travel requires considerably greater time vis-a-vis air than in the United States. The important point of American control in an area which may be a key point in the world air communications system should not be overlooked. This would be particularly true with reference to the continent of Asia in which American foreign policy has shown a consistent interest as attested by the Open Door Policy.

A mail and travel service at reliable American hands would be available to American occupation authorities in the quick furtherance of their official duties. The service will fit in with the general plan of civilianizing the economic scene in Japan. It will also fill the gap left by the discontinuance of the former Courier Service.

Advantages of the Service

The advantages of the service as proposed by Asia Airways are believed to be in the interests of the United States government and its citizens as well as providing a service in the establishment of a peaceful and normal Japanese economy within the framework of general allied policy. Through Asia Airways, a fund of capable civilian reservist flight and operations personnel thoroughly familiar with the Japanese main islands will be maintained against a future emergency. At the same time the Japanese will be denied the chance of again building up an air fleet or air knowledge which will enable them to imperil the United States or its allies. The knowledge gained by air lines personnel will provide a useful source of information for American intelligence should the need arise. The service proposed will enhance the prestige of U.S. commercial aviation and afford gainful employment for a number of deserving ex-armed forces personnel.

Qualifications of the Corporation for the Job

Asia Airways brings certain qualifications to the job not found in a similar corporation. The leadership of the corporation is well qualified in Japanese economic aspects (both President and Vice-President having experience with the country, its language and institutions, as well as a five-year study of the old Japan Airlines). In addition, the nucleus of ground and operating personnel has at least five to ten years of practical airlines and/or Army Air Force operational experience. Unofficial information from the Japanese government indicates that they would be receptive to the idea, although it is not the intention of the corporation that this shall in any way enable the Japanese government or its citizens to once again gain control of an air transport company unless allied policy should so dictate.

Due to the planned aircraft maintenance schedule, only one hanger will be needed. This will be located at an airfield in the Tokyo area, and it will also house the main offices of the corporation. The corporation plans to use existing fields on a rental basis. Ticket offices would be leased in each of the cities served.

Owing to the acute housing shortage which exists in Japan, consideration has been given to the problem of quarters for American personnel. Japanese real estate groups have been consulted regarding the possibility and economic advisability of buying a small, damaged apartment house and rebuilding it. The company plans to buy the necessary materials to rebuild to modest American standards. The corporation may buy surplus quonset huts and import these into Japan. Living accommodations will then be rented to American personnel of the corporation at standard rates.

## Maintenance

With the proposed personnel strength, the corporation would be able to accomplish all periodic overhauls and repairs, but it would not be able to economically handle major engine overhauls without an unwarranted increase in expenditures. Considering the ease with which the desired type engine may be purchased, the corporation plans, during the initial phase of operations at least, to purchase a sufficient number of spare engines to enable it to ship them to points outside of Japan for work on a contract basis.

From the point of view of maintenance, the proposed schedule cannot be operated with less than four aircraft. If one considers the intended flying hours per day, two aircraft will be flying while a third is being serviced.

These three aircraft represent an absolute minimum which could be used if there were a day per week break in the schedule. This three-ship service leaves forty-eight hours for the servicing of each aircraft at the fifty-hour check periods—leaving only a negative margin in the event of unexpected work.

In order that these three aircraft shall be used continuously up to fifty hour cycles (rather than be serviced at forty-five and forty-six hour cycles) a fourth aircraft would be needed approximately once every six days. This would allow six days for the overhaul of the fourth aircraft and should not interfere with the continuous forty-eight hour servicing cycle.

The average time between fifty-hour inspections on the planned schedule is six days. Each aircraft of the principle three will have an eight-day cycle of which 25% is non-earning (i.e., grounded). This 25% cannot be reduced under the proposed schedule. It is therefore important that the six flying days be made to give the nearest approach to fifty full hours as possible. Hence the need for the fourth aircraft which not only gives a reasonable safety margin, but which also allows for the possibility of heavier schedules and slight changes.

All personnel in any way whatsoever connected with aircraft maintenance will be ex-U.S. military personnel in order to conform with Allied policy as regards Japanese subjects.

From the above table it will be seen that the Japanese employed by the corporation will not be connected with aircraft operations, maintenance or any of the fields covered by the SCAP directive on aviation of 18 November 1945. As indicated, Japanese personnel will be used in such departments as traffic, sales and advertising. According to present SCAP policy, Japanese subjects are permitted to engage in meteorology. It is therefore the intention of the corporation to use six Japanese meteorologists in addition to making use of all other available public meteorological services in Japan. At the date of this writing Japanese are also permitted to act as radio operators. Although aircraft operating within Japan need no airborne radio operators, ground offices, particularly in regard to reservations and space, etc., will of necessity have to keep either in radio or teletype contact with each other. Presumably, SCAP policy does not delimit the use of Japanese personnel here. All air-ground radio communication will be carried on with airways traffic control stations and airport control towers in use at present.

Finances

The corporation's operations require $300,000, which is conservatively estimated to permit a non-profitable operation for more than a four-month period. However, present estimates insure a reasonable rate of profit from the early stages of operation. If the corporation can commence operations by July 1946, it is planned to base passenger fares on the rate of fifty sen per kilometer, based on the

present value of the yen (15 to 1) and subject to any future changes thereon. Rates may be changed to the extent of the inflationary curve in Japan. This rate compares favorably with the last rates charged by the Japan Airways Company*. Air express and air freight tariffs, in keeping with former Japanese tariff systems, would be one fiftieth of the passenger rate on a kilogram per kilometer basis. Thus the basic freight rate would be 0.01 per kilogram per kilometer. The above mentioned tariff rates are estimated to be economically sound as to the ability of the corporation to realize a profit without excessively high fares. The corporation hopes to gain an airmail contract from the Japanese Government as an additional source of income.

The National City Bank of New York handles the account of Asia Airways, Inc. Information regarding the corporation or its officers may be obtained from this bank.

*International Aviation, Dec. 14, 1945, stated that Japan Airways, Inc. was formerly the third largest aircraft in the world.

## Description

We learn from Ambassador Muccio that on September 7 the scheduled military airlift between Japan and Korea was terminated in contravention of a State-Military-ECA agreement of last year. At that time the Military Establishment agreed to support the combined State-ECA-Army Mission in Korea by an airlift between Tokyo and Seoul, consisting of one C-46 round-trip flight daily, without reimbursement. Muccio says that this "comes as a heavy blow to functioning of all segments of this Mission."

This letter offers to discuss the problem further but asks that immediate action be taken to restore sufficient airlift to cover the minimum needs of the Mission as specified by Muccio.

Attached is the series of telegrams concerning termination of the air lift provided by the Air Force between Japan and Seoul. You will note that Ambassador Muccio's No. 1175 of September 22 explains why an increase in commercial air lift will not meet the needs of the combined American Mission. Furthermore, in as much as this air lift was to be rendered without reimbursement to switch to commercial transportation would require funds which are not available to us.

This letter has not only been cleared personally with Mr. Hoffman, but was prepared in consultation with members of the staff of Under Secretary Voorhees of the Army who felt that it was not necessary to go into a detailed discussion at this time covering the inter-departmental agreement, which should be readily accepted by the agencies concerned. If any questions are raised we can support our statements and certain members of the Army staff are prepared to participate in such support.

This is the second instance where the Air Forces have taken action ignoring the inter-departmental agreement. The first arose last February when without warning they removed the GCA equipment from Kimpo Air Field at Seoul. We managed to effect other arrangements at that time with considerable inconvenience. This time there is no adequate substitute.

Further, because of our present negotiations relative to transfer of responsibility in Germany, it seems important that we bring this matter to the attention of the Secretary of Defense to justify our continued reliance on support agreed to by the National Military Establishment. General Magruder of Under Secretary Voorhees' office is willing to assist in any way possible in resolving this matter.

# MEMORANDUM FOR RECORD

Colonels VanWagoner and Foster called me this morning to question whether a June 30, 1950 terminal date could be set on the non-reimbursable air lift between Tokyo and Seoul. I stated that the original arrangement had no specific terminal date and that it seemed to be a matter for decision by the Bureau of the Budget.

I discussed the question by telephone with Mr. Lawson of the Bureau of the Budget who was of the opinion at this time, while the entire Korean air program was pending before Congress, it was too early to decide budget responsibility for the air lift beyond June 30, 1950. He suggested that as soon as practicable when the Congressional action on Korea has been completed, the Bureau would consider and make a decision.

I again talked with Colonels VanWagoner and Foster and explained Mr. Lawson's point of view. Colonel Foster agreed that when the time seemed appropriate the military would raise the question with the Bureau of the Budget, looking toward a decision as to whether such air lift would be provided after June 30, 1950 on a reimbursable or non-reimbursable basis; that if it were the former, a decision would be sought as to the agency or agencies having budgetary responsibility.

Colonels VanWagoner and Foster indicated that they were not certain at this point whether the air lift had been reinstated on a weekly basis, but they would endeavor to get out a further wire to CINCEUR reiterating the previous instructions that such air lift be reinstated on a non-reimbursable basis, at least for the current fiscal year.

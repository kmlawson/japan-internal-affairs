A-A:

There is attached a memorandum of a conference held in the Treasury Department on October 2, with representatives of this Department participating, in regard to the financial position of the New York agency of the Yokohama Specie Bank. The situation fundamentally is that the dollar assets of this Bank are dwindling rapidly relative to liabilities to a point where the interest of the creditors of the Bank, which the New York State banking authorities are charged with safeguarding, is being jeopardized. The situation has been brought about by the cessation of trade between Japan and the United States with the consequent inability of the Bank to replenish its dollar assets.

The underlying memorandum in regard to this question is brief and it is suggested that it be read in its entirety.

It will be noted that there are a number of alternatives, most of which are rather drastic. However, Mr. White of the New York State banking department, suggested that a mild preliminary approach might be made (see top of page 3) which would consist of stating to officials of the New York agency of the Yokohama.

CONFERENCE MEMORANDUM WITH RESPECT
TO NEW YORK BRANCH OF THE YOKOHAMA SPECIE BANK

Conference held at the office of Mr. Bernstein
at the Treasury Department on October 2, 1941,
at 9:30 a.m.

Present: Mr. White and Mr. Ludemann of the New York State
Banking Department; Mr. Gibbs Lyons, National Bank
Examiner; Mr. Knoke of the Federal Reserve Bank of
New York; Messrs. Luthringer, Miller and Jones of
the State Department; and Messrs. Bernstein, Pehle,
Fox and Kehl of the Treasury Department.

Mr. White opened the discussion by stating that in the near future
the ability of the New York Branch of the Yokohama Specie Bank to meet its
obligations to creditors might become a critical issue. With that in mind,
a conference had been suggested to discuss alternative solutions and methods
of handling the problem.

Background. By way of introduction to the problems involved, a brief
summary of the financial position of the Yokohama Specie Bank, to date, may
be helpful. The drawing down of funds of the Yokohama Specie Bank in this
country for several months prior to the freezing order, together with
certain practices of the bank in the week preceding July 26, 1941, raised
some question as to the ability of the bank, after the date of the Order
to pay the claims of all of its creditors. This uncertainty as to the
position of the bank led to the adoption by the Treasury Department of a
policy whereunder a distinction was drawn as to license applications to pay
liabilities of the bank accruing prior to July 26, 1941, and those accruing
thereafter. While this policy was generally adhered to, it has been felt
advisable by the Treasury Department, in consultation with the State Depart-
ment, to permit the servicing of obligations of the Japanese Government
and of certain Japanese corporations out of the blocked assets of the
New York Branch of the bank, even though these liabilities accrued after
July 26, 1941. A recent examination of the bank discloses that the over-all
requirements for the servicing of Japanese Government and corporate debt
(the latter being guaranteed in almost all cases by the Japanese Government)
is, on the average, approximately $1,400,000 a month. In addition to the
debt service payments, there are two other sources of claims upon the New
York Branch which may affect its solvency. To date, licenses have been
granted authorizing the withdrawal from the New York Branch by California
Branches of the bank, of approximately $1,400,000 but these withdrawals
have been held up by the National Bank Examiner. In the second place, the
requirements of the Japanese Diplomatic and Consular representatives in
this country will approximate $200,000 a month and remittance of these
funds will be made through the New York Branch.

Mr. Ludemann stated that their examination showed cash on hand of
$8,300,000 and liabilities of $9,200,000. In addition to cash, the Branch
has

has approximately $19,000,000 of bills receivable, principally on silk shipments. In the majority of cases the silk itself has been released against acceptances and the Branch now holds the obligations of the importers, usually Japanese silk firms. Deliveries of silk are now subject to OPM release and this may defer realization over a considerable period of time. Furthermore, there is some doubt as to the solvency of the silk importers and to that extent as to the value of the receivables in the hands of the Yokohama Specie Bank.

**Present Financial Position.** Whether or not the Yokohama Specie Bank is presently insolvent is at least doubtful. However, Mr. White's men have completed an examination of their own and he stated that unless the Bank acquires new assets it will not be able to make payments under licenses for any considerable length of time without jeopardizing the position of present creditors. He said he would feel it was necessary to act on behalf of New York State when it became apparent that creditors were in danger of losing adequate protection. When such action would become necessary depended, in his judgment, upon the rate of allowance of licenses. It was clear that the principal drain upon the bank's assets is the servicing of Japanese debt obligations and that if action by New York State was taken, it would affect the servicing of these obligations.

**Courses of Action Suggested by Mr. White.** Mr. White suggested three possible methods of handling the situation: (1) The State authorities might be informed as to all license applications and action thereon by the Treasury. If at any point it became apparent that the financial position of the institution was in danger, the State authorities could take appropriate action. (2) An escrow arrangement might be worked out whereby sufficient assets of the bank were frozen to meet creditors' claims and licenses could be paid only out of the excess. (3) If it was felt that the other measures were merely temporizing and that liquidation of the bank was inevitable, steps might be taken to close it immediately.

It was suggested to Mr. White that any of the courses of action suggested involved considerations which the State Department would like to consider.

**Matters to be Covered with the State Department.** Mr. Pehle suggested to the representatives of the State Department that they canvass the question whether the State Department would have any objection to the State of New York stepping in to protect the rights of creditors of the Yokohama Specie Bank when such action was deemed necessary. Specifically, Mr. Pehle suggested that they inquire whether there was any objection:

(1) To the New York banking officials asking the Yokohama Specie Bank to voluntarily escrow a part of its assets to protect creditors.

(2) To compelling the bank to do this if the officials did not voluntarily agree to such a program.

After these suggestions had been made, Mr. White suggested the possibility of an even milder approach. It would consist of stating to the Japanese bank officials that the position of the bank was somewhat doubtful, with the request that they make any suggestions that they might have as to its improvement. In this regard Mr. Lyons advised that the National Bank Examiner in New York had been told by Japanese officials that they would be willing to transfer dollars to New York, but the source of such dollars was not indicated.

It is to be recognized that any of the suggested courses of action, such as escrowing the assets of the bank, are purely temporary expedients designed to meet a situation which may become urgent within a period as short as three weeks. Any permanent solution of the problem must involve the replenishment of the funds available to the bank. Possible methods of doing this are - (1) transfer of Japanese bank balances in South America; (2) transfers from branches of the bank in Hawaii and the Philippines; (3) shipment of gold from Japan.

**The Problem of Default on Japanese Obligations.** Under the program as outlined, presumably, Foreign Funds Control would continue to license payments of Japanese sinking fund and interest obligations by the Yokohama Specie Bank. The New York State authorities, when the financial position of the bank became critical, might prevent the Branch from effecting licensed transactions unless new assets were brought in. Thus it might be said that any default in Japanese obligations could occur only from failure to bring adequate funds in to meet the same. There would thus be no refusal to permit the Japanese Government to pay its obligations if it chose to do so.

Memorandum • UNITED STATES GOVERNMENT

DATE: March 27, 1944

TO: LA - Mr. Reinstein

FROM: LA - Mr. Moffat

SUBJECT:

The suggested basic designs for military yen have been examined by the appropriate political division, as well as two or three other officials concerned with Far Eastern policy.

It is agreed that the background should be totally neutral and contain no symbolism. For this reason the design which appears to include a cherry blossom should be excluded. The use of a bamboo background in the design is thoroughly acceptable.

There are several points on which there is complete unanimity of opinion:

(1) The denomination and amount should be printed both in English and in Japanese ideograph. Care should be taken to employ the more formal ideograph which it is customary to use on currency. Japanese ideographs have been borrowed from and are identical with Chinese characters. Space should be allowed for English words longer than "sen" and "yen" such as "piastres" and "dollars", use of which may be defined in some areas.

(2) It is suggested that the term "Military Government Currency" would be suitable in areas both of sole naval jurisdiction and of inter-allied operations. This term should appear both in English and in ideographs. It is requested that the State Department be consulted on the Japanese translation to be adopted.

(3) The area in which the note is issued should be specified both in English and in ideograph.

(4) Different sizes and different colors should be used for each denomination; the larger the denomination the larger the size. It is not necessary to match the size and shape or colors against the equivalent Japanese currency, but this might prove convenient in actual operation.

(5) With the low standard of wages and other expenses which the military authorities will have to meet, it is urged that plenty of small notes including 10 and 50 sen should be printed. Suitable provision of small notes may help prevent such mistakes as were made in Italy in paying laborers 100 lira when the highest pre-invasion daily wage was 25 lira. The decision as to what denominations ought to be prepared should be made now on account of the need for progressive size and different colors and design. Present Japanese notes include, 5, 10 and 50 sen; and 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 yen. It is not thought necessary to make either a 5 sen or a 200 yen note.

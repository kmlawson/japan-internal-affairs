Tokyo, June 7, 1934.

Hon. Joseph C. Grew,
U. S. Ambassador to Japan.

My dear Mr. Grew,

I have devoted considerable thought to our conversation. As a representative of the Hearst Press I would like very much to have an audience with his majesty, the Emperor.

A solution of the difficulties that you suggested has occurred to me. Inasmuch as you presented the representative of another newspaper organization at the request of the Foreign Office, you would certainly be glad to present me under the same circumstances.

With the excellent staff at your disposal you could informally and indirectly intimate to the Foreign Office that a similar request, in my behalf, would be favorably considered by you. I am sure that such an intimation would be effective.

As far as credentials are concerned the enclosed should be sufficient. The King Features Syndicate—one of our many organizations—is just as important as the United Press.

In the event that you can arrange the audience you can reach me quickly through Capt. Varges, whose address I append.

With kind regards,

Sincerely,

(s) Rudolph Block.

Tokyo, June 7, 1934.

My dear Mr. Block:

I hasten to reply to your letter of today's date concerning your desire, as a representative of the Hearst Press, to have an audience with His Majesty the Emperor.

The question has been taken up this morning with the proper authorities at the Foreign Office who gave the matter serious and sympathetic consideration. I was informed, however, that it is a standing rule of the Imperial Household that no requests for interviews or audiences can be granted to press representatives. It was explained at some length that the representative of another newspaper organization was received by the Emperor not in his capacity as a newspaper representative but as a prominent industrialist, the head of a large syndicate of newspapers. In other words, it was further explained, he was received because of his prominence in the field of industry, much as if he had prominence in the field of finance, banking or politics.

I am extremely sorry, therefore, that there seems to be nothing further which I can do with propriety in view of the fact that I am estopped by reason of the strict rule of the Imperial Household.

At all events, from the practical viewpoint, an audience would be out of the question at present because of the fact that His Majesty the Emperor has left the Capital for the summer and will not return until September excepting for the conduct of strictly official business. I was informed that during his absence it is impossible to arrange for audiences.

The letter from Mr. Connolly is returned herewith.

I am, my dear Mr. Block,

Yours very truly,

Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador.

Mr. Rudolph Block,

MEMORANDUM
August 6, 1935.

SUBJECT: Complaint of Mrs. Edytha Macy Mickles.

On the evening of Monday, August 5, 1935,
Mrs. Edytha Macy Mickles spoke with Mr. Dickover by
telephone and complained of discourteous and in-
different treatment by the Embassy.

Mrs. Mickles, an American citizen whose husband
is a Canadian, was introduced to Mr. Andrews, Third
Secretary, about August 1 by Cuban friends of Mr.
Andrews who were also stopping at the Imperial Hotel
where the Mickles had recently arrived. Mr. Andrews
asked Mr. and Mrs. Mickles to call at the Embassy if
they might care to do so, which they did on the follow-
ing day, Friday, August 2. At that time Mrs. Mickles
made application for a permit to visit the Imperial
Palace at Kyoto. When asked by what date she would
require it, she replied that their itinerary does not
take them to Kyoto for some time, but that if the
permit be sent to the Imperial Hotel upon its receipt
the hotel would be informed of a forwarding address.
Mr. and Mrs. Mickles also exhibited a letter of in-
troduction from Governor Curley of Massachusetts
addressed to His Celestial Highness, Son of Heaven,
Emperor of Japan, in which it was stated that the
Governor

=== Page 13 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

Governor would appreciate any courtesies rendered to Mr. and Mrs. Mickles. Mr. Andrews arranged for Mr. and Mrs. Mickles to call upon Mr. Kishi at the Foreign Office.

At four o'clock on August 5, Mrs. Mickles telephoned to the Embassy and spoke to me. At the start of her conversation she showed her annoyance at the delay which she and her husband were experiencing in having an automobile which they wish to use in Japan cleared by the authorities in Yokohama. She said that nothing but red tape is delaying them, and that Mr. Kishi had told her that a letter of introduction from the Embassy would be of assistance. Mr. Mickles explained that by 7 o'clock the following morning (August 6) she and her husband would be in Yokohama in connection with clearing their automobile, and requested a letter of introduction to the Chief of Police, Yokohama. I wrote out one which was in her hands at the hotel within an hour of the time that she telephoned.

In the same telephone conversation Mrs. Mickles asked for an explanation as to why she had not already received the permit to visit the Kyoto Imperial Palace for which she had applied on August 2. The application was forwarded immediately (August 2) to the Imperial Household Department and by August 5 the permit had not been received by the Embassy. As mentioned in the attached

=== Page 14 === (confidence 0.99)
-3-

attached copy of my communication to Mrs. Hickles on the subject, it should be noted that Mrs. Hickles did not name a date by which she might need the permit. She referred to the fact that her husband's permit had already been received through the Canadian Legation. There is, of course, the possibility that the Imperial Household Department was slightly delayed by reason of the fact that according to its ordinary procedure a permit for Mrs. Hickles would have been handled with that of her husband through the husband's mission.

In complaining to Mr. Dickover, Mrs. Hickles admitted that the police permit will not be used before several weeks, and that her complaint was therefore not on the grounds of causing a delay in her itinerary but a matter of principle. She also stated that she understands the Japanese people and she knows that my letter addressed to the Chief of Police, Yokohama, is an extreme discourtesy because it does not address him by name.

Mrs. Hickles implied that she would make a complaint to the Department of State.

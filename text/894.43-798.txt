PROSPECTUS
OF
THE SOCIETY FOR INTERNATIONAL
CULTURAL RELATIONS
As the international relations of the world at the
present day grow in complexity, difficult problems
crop up in increasing numbers and delicate forces
come into play, as is obvious to anybody acquainted
with the prevailing international situation. Besides
political and economic contacts, the feelings of one
people towards another, their relations in art and sci-
ence, the promotion of mutual knowledge through the
medium of the screen, the cultivation of friendship by
means of sport—all these and other factors exert on
international relations an influence which, with the
continuous development of the means of communica-
tion, ever continues to increase in force and impor-
tance. It is patent, therefore, that a nation, in order
to secure and strengthen its position in the comity of
nations, must endeavour to promote the value and pre-
stige of its own culture, besides fostering its national
resources, so as to merit greater respect, affection and
sympathy on the part of other peoples. The diffusion
1

=== Page 21 === (confidence 1.00)
of knowledge regarding a nation's culture is not only
necessary for the purpose of enhancing its prestige,
but will also serve to make its people alive to their posi-
tion in the world and to strengthen their sense of
self-respect. It is well known that many civilized
countries possess great organizations for developing
and spreading their culture in all directions, both at
home and abroad, and are doing all in their power to
carry on their cultural activities.
However, the Japanese people, since the begin-
ning of the Meiji Era, have been too eagerly absorbed
in introducing occidental culture to be properly con-
scious of their own. Of late years there have been
discernible signs of awakening to the value of their
own culture, but self-confidence is apt to be tinged with
self-sufficiency. We appear to lack a sufficiently
liberal intention to make known to the world the full
extent of our culture and to cause it to contribute to
the furtherance of the culture and welfare of all man-
kind, in happy unison with other nations. As to the
means which may secure the international dissemina-
tion of our culture, there appear to exist almost no
important organizations appropriate to the prestige
of a great nation. Although politically we are going
to leave the League of Nations, yet, because of this
very fact, we must participate in the increasing num-
ber of enterprises for the promotion of world culture
2

=== Page 22 === (confidence 1.00)
and human welfare, and in this connection the respon-
sibilities of the Japanese people will assume all the
greater importance, as is clearly stated in the Imperial
Rescript concerning Japan's notification of withdraw-
al from the League.
Moreover, at this juncture when the culture of
the world is being threatened, Western people are
turning to the Orient in the hope that the East can
contribute some cultural benefit for the future wel-
fare of mankind, and there are already signs of a
growing inclination on the part of the West to a deep-
er study of the East, more especially of Japan. To
take advantage of this opportunity to accelerate that
movement and to make the world better acquainted
with the real significance of Japanese and Oriental
culture, is perhaps an important duty which the Japa-
nese people should undertake, not only for the sake
of Japan but, indeed, of the whole world. That this
task is beset with difficulties in many directions there
can be no doubt, and its accomplishment will demand
the establishment of a strong organization, support-
ed by the whole-hearted cooperation of the Govern-
ment and the people. It is with this end in view that
we hereby propose to organize a foundation in the
form of a Society for International Cultural Rela-
tions. Its object will be to undertake the necessary
tasks falling within its province and also to keep in

contact with, or to extend proper assistance to, indivi-
duals and organized bodies, both at home and abroad,
actuated by analogous motives, with a view to fur-
thering the international promotion of culture, and
more especially to familiarising the rest of the world
with the culture of Japan and other Oriental nations.

SCHEME OF ACTIVITIES
OF
THE SOCIETY FOR INTERNATIONAL
CULTURAL RELATIONS
There are wide and different fields of activities
through which to make the culture of Japan and the
Orient known abroad, and to stimulate interest in and
sympathy with Japanese culture among peoples of
other nationalities. But some of the activities men-
tioned below might well form the first works of the
Society. Of course consideration and study will have
to be given to the order of their precedence, and length
of time, in accordance with the situation at home and
abroad, financial position of the Society, and the per-
sons necessary to carry out the work.
1. Writing, compilation, translation and publication
of various works:
(a) Writing, compilation and publication in Japa-
nese or other languages concerning history,
etymology, literature, science, religion, ethics,
education, law, politics, economics, art, art manu-
facture, architecture, music, drama, national
traits, customs, sports and other fields of cul-
tural activities.
5

=== Page 25 === (confidence 1.00)
(b) Translation into foreign languages and publi-
cation of the whole text or a summary of writ-
ings on Japanese and Oriental culture, and the
translation and publication in foreign languages
of the scientific articles written in Japan.
(c) Translation into Japanese and publication of
books written in foreign languages on the cul-
ture of Japan or other countries.
(d) Endeavour to unify and adjust the terms used
in translation.
2. Establishment of chairs, sending d exchange
of professors:
(a) Establishment of chairs on Japanese culture in
important universities abroad.
(b) Establishment of chairs on Japanese language
in foreign schools, or establishment of Japanese
language schools in foreign countries.
(c) Sending to and exchange of professors with
foreign countries.
3. Holding of lecture meetings, exhibitions, and
concerts:
(a) Exchange of authoritative people and holding
of lecture meetings, etc.
(b) Holding of exhibitions abroad on Japanese cul-
ture, and exhibitions for introducing foreign
culture to Japan.
6

ally to introduce drama, dance, and music.
4. Donation and exchange of documents on cul-
ture:
(a) Donation of publications on the culture of
Japan as well as the Orient to universities,
libraries, etc. in foreign countries or the ex-
change of such publications.
(b) Donation or loan of works of Japanese art and
other cultural articles to foreign museums, or
their exchange.
5. Invitation to Japan of authoritative statesmen,
business men, scholars, journalists, thinkers, novelists,
artists, etc. from other countries, or provision of faci-
lities to be given to them to come.
6. Provision of facilities for the study of Oriental

(a) Provision of facilities to foreigners coming to
Japan to study the culture of Japan and the
Orient.
(b) Provision of facilities to people abroad study-

A number of considerations can be enumerated as re-

the desirability of concentrating in one important body

The activities of the International Society for
Cultural Relations should not, however, be understood as
being only of a popularizing nature. Its promoters are
firmly convinced that assistance to foreign research stu-
dents (whose conclusions of course can not be controlled)
will bear fruit in the permanent national interest. In
the near future an American scholar (Dr. John Henry Wigmore)
is to be aided in research in the law of the shogunate
period, and Count Kabayama has high hopes that there will
be no lack of such work later on. In fact, it is the plan
of the new institution to keep its head above the flood
of the necessities of routine cultural conveniences for
foreigners and to devote imnortant aid to a few larger
undertakings in turn as they may be possible. It hopes
to pay the cost of publishing studies which would otherwise
be impossible .
This organization does not represent all the extra-
official activities promoted by the Foreign Office for the
good name of Japan abroad. The Foreign Office continues to
lend financial assistance to such purposes as the Japanese
Davis cup tennis team and motion picture news reels de-
picting Japanese subjects. In fact, the Embassy is in-
formed that the allotment for such purposes in the present
fiscal year is more than double the amount set aside for
the International Society for Cultural Relations. Nevertheless,
the society represents an important effort to bring under
unified, intelligent direction a considerable number of
activities worthy of future development.

The foundation is of course flooded with requests
for aid, the great majority of which are refused. In
those particular instances which are found acceptable,
support is lavish. The special object is to promote
a very" few undertakings at a time, in a manner more
thorough and consequential than would otherwise be possible.
As time goes on it is possible that scholars and in-
stitutions in the United States may be able to take
more and more advantage of the opportunities offered
by this organization. It is under scholarly direction
of a notably liberal tinge. Qualified American scholars
might well exploit the opportunity to pursue research in
many Japanese fields which have hitherto invited by their
interest and discouraged by their inaccessibility. Reason-
able requests for sources of information and material for
research could scarcely be refused. A discreet choice of
subjects should make it possible to capitalize the obviously
liberal inclinations of the society's management without
running afoul of the institution's purse strings.

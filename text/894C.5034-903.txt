AMERICAN CONSULATE
Dairen, Manchuria, August 1, 1934.

SUBJECT: Kwantung Army's Refusal to Permit Establishment in Dairen of Assembly Plant for American Motor Cars.

The Honorable
Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador,
Tokyo, Japan.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer the Embassy to my despatch of May 15, 1934, file 800, subject, Changes in Organization and Control of Foreign Companies in Manchuria. In paragraph four of this despatch there are described plans for forming a Japanese-American company to be known as the Teikoku Jidosha Seisakusho, for the purpose of assembling Chrysler motor cars, including Dodge, Plymouth, and De Soto, in Dairen. For ready reference, the paragraph in reference is quoted below:

"Of pertinent interest in connection with the subject of this despatch is the plan for building a plant in Dairen for assembling products of the Chrysler automobile company. Fuku-sho and Company (large Japanese importing and exporting organization) is now agent for Chrysler, De Soto, and Plymouth cars, and Frazer, Federal Incorporated (Frazer and Company in Japan) is agent for Dodge motor cars. These two companies and the agent in Japan for Chrysler, De Soto, and Plymouth cars (understood to be a Japanese named Nakaiya)

have

=== Page 4 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

have joined together to form the Teikoku Jidosha Seisakusho, with a capital of Yen 100,000 of which Fukusho holds half and the other two concerns one quarter each. Fukusho is to have three directors, Frazer two, and the Nakaiya interests two, one of whom is accountant for the company. Land near Dairen has already been purchased and building contracts are now being let. The plant is to have an original capacity for assembling five hundred cars a year and will also manufacture certain automobile parts. Although the Chrysler company has no financial interest in the enterprise, it is offering it full support. The cars assembled in Dairen will be for distribution in Manchuria and China. Low freight rates and cheap labor are the inducements for building an assembly plant in Dairen. Dairen being a free port, import duties are not a consideration.

Permission to organize the company was received from the Kwantung Government and it was duly registered with the local authorities. Also, permission was obtained to proceed with the construction of the plant on land which had been purchased near Dairen. Besides the cost of the land, the company had let a preliminary building contract for Yen 25,800 and had purchased some of its assembly plant machinery, valued at ¥1,600, in America.

Several weeks ago the Kwantung Army became interested in the project. Inquiries were made as to the organization of a company which presumed to call itself "Teikoku" (Imperial) and as to the type of automobiles to be manufactured. The organization was explained and a willingness shown to drop the name "Teikoku" if the Army found it objectionable. In reply to the inquiry concerning the kind of cars to be manufactured, the Army was informed that the company simply planned to assemble American motor cars already on sale in Manchuria and that the name "Seisakusho" (Factory) was used

because

=== Page 5 === (confidence 0.99)
-3-

because there was no suitable word in Japanese for "assembly plant". These explanations were thought to have satisfied the Army.

However, somewhat later the Kwantung Government notified the Dairen organizers of the company that instructions had been received from the Kwantung Army to cancel the permit for the Company to operate in the Kwantung Leased Territory. According to the Kwantung Government, the Army did not wish to have a private concern in Dairen manufacturing or assembling automobiles in competition with the "Towa Jidosha Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha" (T'ung Ho Automobile Industry Company) in Mukden. This is a semi-official "Manchukuo" company under Kwantung Army control which will assemble trucks, using Japanese motors and parts (see Mukden Consulate General's report of July 25, 1934, entitled "Manufacture of Automobile Chassis in Mukden").

The Kwantung Government was sympathetic towards the complaint of the Seisakusho's representative that considerable money had been spent on the basis of the Government's permit for it to operate but admitted that there was no other recourse than to carry out the Army's orders. The admission was made that suit could be brought but this method of redress was considered unwise for the very good reason that it "doesn't pay" firms dealing in automobiles in Manchuria to antagonize the Kwantung Army, assuming there might be a chance of obtaining a favorable decision. The Kwantung Government suggested that a representative of the Company go to

Heinching

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
-4-

Mainching and lay the matter before the Army in the hope of receiving a reimbursement for the losses caused by the Army's action. This has been done, and it is possible that some money settlement will be obtained.

Without reference to the American interests involved, the case has a number of interesting aspects. It illustrates of course how completely the Army is in control and shows, I am led to believe, that the civil authorities in the Kwantung Government thoroughly resent the high handed manner in which this control is often enforced. The Government officials at Port Arthur made no pretense of hiding their chagrin at having to reverse their position in obedience to the preemptory orders of the Army but there was never any doubt that reverse it they must. The case further discloses that the Army is prepared to extend its "Manchukuo" economic control policy to include the Kwantung Leased Territory when circumstances made it advisable. Theoretically, it strikes one as peculiar that a Japanese registered company should be prohibited from establishing an assembly plant on Japanese Territory because the operation of such a plant would run counter to the Kwantung Army's plans for a similar enterprise in "Manchukuo". Realistically, there is nothing peculiar about this phase of the case when it is admitted that "Manchukuo" and the Kwantung Territory form a single territorial unit in so far as the Army is concerned. It is difficult to understand, however, why the Army should object to private Japanese and American capital establishing a Japanese company for the purpose of

operating

=== Page 7 === (confidence 0.99)
-5-

operating an automobile assembly plant in Dairen. The plant would seem to be in line with the Army's plan for making Manchuria more independent in this field of enterprise and nothing certainly could prevent the Army from taking over control of the plant when it considered such action necessary.

The American interests in the assembly plant (Frazar, Federal Incorporated with twenty-five per cent of the stock) do not wish official representations made on their behalf, assuming that it were proper to make such representations. It is a Japanese registered company in which three quarters of the stock is held by Japanese. There is however another side of the case meriting consideration. Officially, the Kwantung Government (actually, the Kwantung Army) has refused to allow a company to assemble American automobiles in Dairen (Japanese territory) on the grounds that such enterprise would conflict with plans for assembling motor cars from Japanese materials in Yukon ("Manchukuo" territory). To me this appears to constitute an unreasonable and unjustifiable restriction on American trade. I have been informed that American motor cars assembled in Dairen could be priced from three to five hundred yen lower in the Manchurian market than similar cars brought out ready assembled from the United States. The Army's position is simply one of refusing to allow motor cars of the Chrysler Export Corporation this advantage in the Manchurian market and the reason given for the refusal is, to say the least, farfetched.

I submit these facts and observations for the Embassy's consideration and should very much appreciate being

being informed of its opinion in the premises.

Respectfully yours,

John Carter Vincent
American Consul.

From L. Neville, Esquire,
American Charge d'Affaires ad interim,
Tokyo, Japan.

I have the honor to refer to the Embassy's instruction of September 7, 1934, relative to my despatch of August 1, 1934, on the above subject, and to submit the following information concerning what appears to be a final settlement of the case.

The site of the projected assembly plant was owned by the Fukusho Company, the largest stockholder in the assembly plant, and was simply returned to the original owner. The machinery ordered from America was taken over by the other Japanese stockholder, understood to be the agent in Japan for Chrysler motor cars. What reimbursement the Kwantung Army made for money expended on preparing the site and in connection with building contracts, I have been unable to ascertain. I do know, however, that the Fukusho Company, building contractors as well as agents for all Chrysler motor cars except Dodge, were given to understand that both in the matter of future contracts and motor car purchases controlled by

=== Page 10 === (confidence 0.97)
-2-

by the Army, it would be given special consideration. The American company interested in the assembly plant was included in the understanding in so far as motor car purchases are concerned.

It now appears that the Army was more opposed to the formal establishment of an automobile assembly company in Dairen than to the actual assembling of motor cars. Hence Fukusho Company has now begun assembling a few Chrysler units at its own place and plans to enlarge the present accommodations to make possible the assembly of a considerable number of cars. Machinery is not being used, the assembly being done by hand.

Both the Japanese and the American company in Dairen, who were shareholders in the proposed assembly plant, seem to be satisfied with the turn matters have taken and with the present situation.

Respectfully yours,

John Carter Vincent
American Consul.

SUBJECT: Closing of Important British Firm in Dairen.

894C.5034

NOT FOR P.O.
The Honorable
Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador,
Tokyo, Japan.

I have the honor to report as further evidence of the gradual exclusion of foreign business from the Manchurian market that the General Electric Company of China, Limited, has been forced to close its Dairen office as of the end of March 1936, on account of lack of business.

The office has operated in Dairen for the past seventeen years, and did a very profitable business until the Japanese absorption of the Manchurian market gradually throttled the opportunities of the Company in common with those of many other foreign firms in Manchuria. Among other past business of the Company were four steam driven winding engines supplied to the Fushun Collieries, equipment for many Chinese collieries before the "Incident", and the majority of the equipment of the electric light and power plants in Manchuria

prior

Mr. P. W. Turner, the former manager of the Dairen office, informed me several months ago of the growing difficulties of his firm (see report entitled "Electric Power Developments - Fourth Quarter, 1936 - Kwantung Leased Territory," dated January 22, 1936), and of its decision no longer to submit bids for electrical equipment for Manchuria unless it was indicated in advance that the Japanese authorities concerned seriously contemplated the purchase of its products. This decision was adopted because the firm felt (1) that the direction in which the business would go was predetermined; (2) that the expense of preparing detailed specifications was not justified either by the short period allowed for their submission, or by the probability of obtaining any business; and (3) that the plans required to accompany the bids were often sought without any intention of awarding the business to the Company even if its terms were advantageous.

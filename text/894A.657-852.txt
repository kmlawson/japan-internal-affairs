Department of State

Mrs. Hosken, in regard to whom I had received a letter from the Japanese Consul General at New York, called on me yesterday morning. She said that she has under consideration a proposal to develop in cooperation with the Hoshi Pharmaceutical Company of Tokyo a cinchona plantation in Formosa, she to deposit with the Bank of Taiwan a certain sum of money and to receive in return the sole agency for the sale outside Japan of quinine produced by Hoshi. She intends further to purchase from Hoshi menthol and pyrethrum, and through certain Japanese connections to export to Manchuria railway materials. She said that if she decided to go in with Hoshi on the quinine project, she would not have sufficient funds to finance the other projects above-mentioned. In view of the fact that her operations, if carried out, tend to promote good relations between the United States and Japan, she hoped that the Department would recommend to the First Import-Export Bank that credits be made available to

to her company for purchases of pyrethrum and menthol and sales of railway material.

I told Mrs. Hosken that the Department would regard her projects as being purely of a commercial character and that it could not express any opinion to the Import-Export Bank in regard to the merits of her projects. I volunteered, however, to ascertain whom she should consult in the Bank, and at her request I asked Major Hall of the Bank to receive Mrs. Hosken on Monday at ten o'clock in the morning. # The Chinas to China and Commutes Between Shanghai and New York

Business Is Good. She Says—Helping Make Orient Modern.

By WILLIAM ENGLE.
World-Telgrim Staff Writer.

"From W. M. Hosken, 56 Pine St., New York, to the Hon. Chao Fong Li, 133 Yuang Ming Yuang Rd., Shanghai—consignment, locomotives, airplanes."

"That's right," said "W. M." today. "That's the way they go." Then "W. M." stood up at her desk, a gracious woman coming on toward middle age, America's feminine exporter to the Orient, a commuter between E. 54th St., Manhattan, and Bubbling Well Rd., Shanghai.

Oil for the lamps of China? No, she said, she does not sell that. She sells intricacies, modernities—Fairchild aerial cameras and airplanes, Lockheed all-metal Electra planes, the newest of ingenious parachutes and locomotives beginning to have streamlines.

In private life she dwells, a widow, Mrs. Wilma Maybelle Hosken, in a suite at the Hotel Weylin when she is not traipsing across the Pacific; in business she is head, succeeding her late husband, of the Hosken Trading Co., and she is not Mrs. Hosken. She is W. M.

## Represent Manufacturers.

"We represent American manufacturers and make their sales in China," she said in her office today, and she is so much at home in China that the last time she came back the farewell parties, she said, embarrassed her, she beginning to think that she might seem to be imposing upon hospitality.

"But I have known them there a long time," she added. (Just sold the government twelve locomotives and a few thousand tons of steel rails.) "I used to meet them first when I went there with my husband."

He used to run the business. When he died she stopped up to his desk, high up above Pine St., to take the job.

On the roads to Cathay the machine age has a spell now, and the quiet, aloof woman behind the desk in Pine St., New York, is going to have a hand in that.

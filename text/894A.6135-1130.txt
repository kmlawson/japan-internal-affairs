# AMERICAN CONSULATE
Taihoku, Taiwan, Japan, October 5, 1939.

## SUBJECT: Promulgation of Ordinance to control Taiwan's Sugar Industry.

I have the honor to report that the sugar industry in Taiwan has now been brought under Government control through the promulgation on October 3, 1939 by the Taiwan Government General of Ordinance No. 6, entitled "Taiwan Sugar Industry Ordinance", and to enclose, in translation, a summary of the regulations as given to the press by Mr. Tatsuro Okuda, Chief of the Special Products Division, Taiwan Government General.

It has been recognized, since the promulgation of the Rice Control Ordinance on May 10, 1939, that similar control of the sugar industry was inevitable. With the present measure now in effect, the Government General will be able to regulate acreage and prices for both crops. By so doing, it is hoped to eliminate any element of competition resulting from profit discrepancies, and

and to direct Taiwan's agricultural production in the way that will most benefit the Japanese Empire.

Chief provisions of the measure are:

1. Sugar manufacturers must be licensed by the Government General.
2. Sugar cane is to be used only for manufacturing sugar except in cases expressly authorized by the Government General.
3. Acreage, buying prices, periods for crushing cane, are subject to control of the Government General.

Public statements were made by both the Governor General, Admiral Kobayashi, and the Director General, Mr. Morioka, setting forth the reasons for the establishment of control and the benefits expected from it. The Governor General emphasized that Taiwan's two leading crops--rice and sugar--were of utmost importance to the Japanese Empire and that to ensure an adequate supply of both, control was necessary. He mentioned that, although in the past producers of centrifugal sugar had been regulated, in that cane territories had been officially designated to supply specified mills, now that rice control was in effect, it was necessary to add supervision of acreage and prices to that control already in existence. The Governor General stated that the new ordinance would strengthen the foundation of Taiwan's sugar industry, enabling it to meet increased demands within

within the country and further Japan's trade expansion through greater exports to countries within and without the yen bloc.

Mr. Morioka pointed out that certain regulations for the control of sugar factories were promulgated in 1905, at a time when Taiwan's sugar production amounted only to 800,000 piculs. However, with a production of 23,000,000 piculs, sufficient not only for Japan's needs but for export to China and "Manchukuo", the situation has completely changed. Mr. Morioka said that the new ordinance will tighten the relationship between grower and manufacturer, will bring the industry into its proper position as regards other industries, and will achieve a constant sugar supply. He asked for the cooperation of farmers and producers.

Mr. Tatsuro Okuda, Chief of the Special Products Division, Taiwan Government General, issued a statement in which he outlined the new regulations. Since Mr. Okuda's résumé is an accurate and concise summary, omitting no important provisions of the ordinance, a translation is enclosed herewith.

Respectfully yours,

John K. Emmerson
American Vice Consul.

Résumé of the Sugar Industry Ordinance by Mr. Tatsuro Okuda, Chief of the Special Products Division of the Bureau of Productive Industries, Government General of Taiwan:

1. The sugar industry is divided into manufacturers of centrifugal sugar and of native brown sugar. No manufacturing of sugar is permitted without license from the Governor General. In view of the public nature of the industry, permission must be obtained from the Governor General for transfer, abandonment, suspension, amalgamation, or dissolution of sugar manufacturing and for any extension or alteration of important equipment.

2. The system already in effect as regards designated sugar cane territories for centrifugal sugar mills is to be continued. Sugar companies are obligated to buy cane in the designated territories. Close contact between manufacturers and growers is aimed.

3. Without permission of the Governor General sugar cane must not be used for manufacturing products other than sugar.

4. Transportation of sugar cane out of or into a designated sugar cane territory to or from a non-designated sugar cane territory requires permission of the Governor General.

5. For harmonious development of the industry, manufacturers of centrifugal sugar are required each year to obtain the approval of the Governor General for scheduled acreage, cane buying prices, and dates for the cane crushing period. The Governor General may, whenever considered necessary, order alterations thereof.

6. The necessity for an even sugar supply demands that when sugar manufacturers have signed a control agreement regarding production or sale of sugar, such agreement must be reported to the Governor General. Whenever considered necessary, the Governor General may order establishment of a control agreement, or may order alteration or cancelation of the whole or any part of such an agreement.

The Regulations also contain provisions enabling the Governor General to require sugar manufacturers, regardless of whether or not they are signatories, to be bound by the whole or a part of a control agreement.

7. For the sound development of the sugar industry the ordinance provides for the supervision of business and accounts of sugar manufacturers.

8. In case sugar manufacturers violate the ordinance or commit acts which injure or are likely to injure the public interest, sugar manufacturing may be ordered suspended or restricted. Penalties are also provided for individual cases.

9. The necessity of a consistent execution of sugar policy, requires that primitive sugar mills, in the past governed only by the Regulation for Enforcement of the Sugar Consumption Tax Law, be also brought under the control of the new regulations.

10. Sugar cane territories for semi-modern sugar mills will be recognized only for a period of five years hence.

(Taiwan Nichi Nichi Shimpo, October 3, 1939)

The attached letter from the Consul at
Taihoku to the Governor General of the
Philippine Islands reports measures recently
enacted by the Taiwan Government General for
the control of Japanese fishing vessels in
the seas south of Taiwan. These new regulations,
it is believed, will lessen the
number of violations of Philippine territorial
waters by fishing vessels registered in
Taiwan, and, by the new markings of Taiwan
fishing vessels, which are compulsory under
the new regulations, it will be easier for the
Philippine customs authorities to identify
and apprehend any Taiwan fishing vessels which
may intrude illegally in Philippine waters.
Friendly relations between Japan and the
Philippine Islands will thereby be improved.

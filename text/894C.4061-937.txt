SUBJECT: Difficulties Confronting American Film Distributors in Dairen.

The Honorable
Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador,
Tokyo, Japan.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that an attempt appears to be making to exercise more close supervision over and coordination of the various motion picture distributors in Manchuria, both Japanese and Foreign.

Hitherto there has existed a very loose and entirely informal association of local motion picture distributors throughout Manchuria, all of whom have their headquarters in Dairen, which according to Mr. Robert Lury, the Manchuria distributor of Metro-Goldwyn Mayer Company, has as its purpose merely the expediting of the films through customs, and in general, the protection of the interests of the distributors.

Last week however, Mr. Lury informed me that the Japanese distributors, most of whom work on a very small capital and bring in relatively few pictures, have decided to organize a formal association, for the obvious purpose of reducing the amount of money (1000 yen) which they are each at present compelled

to deposit

to deposit with the Customs against the eventual re-export of films sent to Manchuria and the payment of the "hirage tax" (10 sen per reel per day with a minimum of 15 days). It is being proposed that the new association put up a collective deposit on behalf of its members. As the American interests much prefer to continue as hitherto, Mr. Lury sought my assistance with a view to ascertaining whether, as stated by Japanese distributors, the "Manchukuo" Customs had demanded that all distributors join the new organization. I discussed the matter informally with the Commissioner of Customs in Mr. Lury's presence, and was assured by the former that the Customs had nothing to do with it, and that although he would recognize a Japanese association were one formed and officially registered in the Kwantung Leased Territory, he would likewise continue to deal with the American distributors separately if they so desired.

I received a visit from representatives of the Paramount, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and Fox companies yesterday in further regard to this matter. They reported that they had been informed by the Japanese association that if they would not join it, they would find that their businesses "would not run so smoothly". strongly warned as to the advisability of joining

better acquiesce. Thus far they have experienced
no difficulties from their membership in the association.

However a new and much more serious threat to the
freedom of this American enterprise in the Kwantung
Leased Territory and Manchuria has just arisen. I
am informed by two independent sources, which I believe

both for the purpose of controlling foreign

Note by Supervising Consul General:

The conditions existing in the marketing of motion picture films in Manchuria and the Kwantung Leased Territory recently received consideration at a meeting of American distributors in Tokyo representing the larger American producers and distributors who in nearly every instance cover the Kwantung Leased Territory and Manchuria. The Consulate General was informed by one of the principal distributors in Japan that rather than submit to any monopolistic dictation, his firm was prepared to close offices and withdraw from the market entirely. He believed that other large American enterprises were likewise inclined.

Arthur Garrels,

SUBJECT: Projected Monopoly of Motion Picture Film Distribution

The Honorable
Joseph C. Grew,
American Ambassador,
Tokyo, Japan.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to my communications
dated February 16 and 23, 1937, regarding the projected
monopoly of the motion picture industry in
Manchuria.

A representative of one of the important American
picture distributors in this area has just called at
the Consulate following a trip to Berlin and reported
the result of his inquiries into the present status
of the projected monopoly. Although he found nothing
in Berlin to reassure all as to its previously reported
scope, one of his employees was at the same
time sent to Heinking where he discussed the matter
with Major Shibata of the Japanese Embassy in that
city, who is understood to be interested in the matter
of monopolizing the motion picture industry on account
of its propaganda possibilities. Major Shibata is
reported to have been reassuring regarding the effects

upon

upon foreign interests of the proposed monopoly. He admitted that the persons interested in the monopoly as yet know little about the distribution of films, and said that therefore for the time its activities will be limited to the production of small pictures for propaganda purposes and to the distribution of the latter as well as of the films of foreign companies that "voluntarily" come into the monopoly. His opinion was that ultimately a monopoly of all distribution will also be undertaken but that it may be several years before that point is reached. Meanwhile a strict control will be exercised over pictures coming from China (principally shanghai), both Chinese and foreign that have not passed Japanese censorship. This precaution is presumably because such films are one of the few remaining means of contact with Chinese thought available to the Chinese inhabitants of Manchuria. By informant states that at the present time not more than 10 or 15 per cent of the foreign films shown in Manchuria are censored here, the balance having passed censorship in Japan, and being, therefore, free from objectionable social or political ideas. Major Shibata informed the employes of the Company in question that the movers of the monopoly idea plan soon to send a representative to Tokyo to discuss the matter with the foreign companies, and to obtain data upon which definite plans can be drawn up. # A FILM PRODUCING COMPANY WILL BE ESTABLISHED IN MANCHUKUO SHORTLY

A new film producing company, probably under the name of the Cinema Pictures Society, Ltd., will be established in February to commence its work in April, according to a plan agreed upon by Major Shibano of Kwantung Army and leading film industrials of Japan.

The company will be formed under a special license of the Manchukuo Government, which will subscribe half of the proposed capital of five million yuan, the other half being subscribed by the South Manchuria Railway. Its object is to manufacture films of things Manchurian for publicity in and out of the country, for the cultivation of right thoughts among the people, and for the prevention of malicious ideas coming in from abroad, by controlling importation, distribution and display of films at theaters. Further, the Company will undertake the production of general artistic films for export.

Mr. R. Masutani, director of PCL., (?) Mr. O. Rokusha of the Shochiku Studio, Mr. T. Tachibana, film critic, Mr. H. Manako of the Osaka Asahi Shimbun and Mr. S. Sakurai of the Osaka Mainichi Shimbun will be advisers to the new industry. They will meet in conference with the promoters of the Company in Hsinking towards the end of January for a concrete plan.

About 33 hectares of land have been chosen outside Hsinking for establishing the Company's offices and studios. Japanese, Korean, Manchurian, Mongolian and Russian actors and actresses will be employed.

Pending construction of permanent buildings and studios, which will take three years, the Company will begin with producing 16 m.m. films for travelling publicity among the Manchurian people in boundary regions.

The capital of the Company (5 million yuan) will not last long unless some means of income be taken. The production and export of artistic films for trade purpose cannot be counted upon for many years to come. In the meantime, therefore, the compulsory display of cultural films in theaters in the country and the monopolization

monopolization of the film industry be the Company are under contemplation by the Government. Perhaps a law governing cinema studies and theaters will be promulgated sooner or later.

In 1936, 5,000 films comprising 30,000 reels were imported into the country from Japan, America, China, Soviet Russia and other countries, for which a million and a half yuan was paid. There were roughly 90 cinema theaters in the country at the end of 1936, besides 60 temporary theaters. The total number of audience was estimated at 15 million people.

As the business will increase rapidly, the control of the display of films only will mean a substantial income to the Company.

The government efforts in publicity with films was first made when a film entitled "A Bird's Eye View of Manchukuo" was created jointly by the Publicity Board of the Government and the South Manchuria Railway in 1935. The picture had explanations in different languages and was shown in every country in Europe and American by the Japanese Foreign Services, with considerable success. Since then the need and importance of constructing a permanent studio was keenly felt in every quarter until the plan has at last ripened as first stated.

The consul at Dairen, in his despatch to Tokyo of August 23rd, writes interestingly about what appears to have been a rather dull event - a Manchu-Japanese economic conference held at Dairen on August 15 - 17.

The Consul reports that the speeches delivered before the Conference "revealed nothing, either from the standpoint of fact or policy, that is not already well known". The proceedings of the committees formed at the Conference were apparently of more substance, "Manchukuo" import duties being the subject of heated discussion between representatives from Japan and Japanese officials of "Manchukuo". The Consul does not believe that anything definite was achieved.

The Consul was not impressed by the "Manchukuo" delegates, who, however, appeared impressed by the orderly conditions in the Leased Territory.

(Address Delivered by the American Ambassador, Mr. Grew,
at the Memorial Service for Mr. Wallace Alexander, Tokyo
Union Churoh, Tokyo, December 13, 1939.)
As we look back through the pages of history we
shall generally find that the lives of those men who
achieved greatness were centered about a single ideal.
Their activities and interests may have been varied and
widespread, their knowledge and familiarity with the
affairs of men may have been all-encompassing, their
sympathies extensive, their understanding of human
nature far-reaching into many corners of the world's
daily life, but generally the lives of those men have
been built around a central interest of paramount
importance, a sort of lode-stone that attracted their
innermost affections, drew out the finest and highest
qualities of their natures and provided a fundamental in-
piration that colored the whole fabric of their lives.
Such a man was Wallace Alexander, a man of broad
outlook and widespread activities but whose central
interest, if not his central inspiration, was the
maintenance and development of friendship between Japan
and the United States. To that high purpose he gave,
over a long period of time, not only his abiding in-
terest but also his active and constructive efforts,
serving as President of the Japan-America Society in
San Franoisco and attending the regular meetings of
that important organization until shortly before his
death. In many commissions, committees and delegations
having

=== Page 22 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-
heving to do with Japanese-American affairs he participated,
and on at least one occasion he visited Japan as chairman
of such a commission and was graciously honored by His
Majesty the Emperor through the bestowal upon him of the
Order of the Sacred Treasure. I remember very well his
courtesy in coming to see us when we passed through
San Francisoo on our way to take up this post in Japan
in 1932 and of the desire which he then expressed that
we should both give our maximum efforts to the develop-
ment of good relations between our two great nations.
Alas, our time was then too limited to accept his cor-
dial invitation to visit his lovely place near San Fran-
oisoo where his flower gardens were said to be the most
beautiful in California. Perhaps it was this love of
beauty and beautirul things that had so endeared Japan
and the Japanese, with their own fundamental love of
beauty, to his beauty-seeking nature. His was a dis-
criminating taste and a search for the higher and finer
things in life.
For today's manifestation of Japanese homage to the
memory of one of my distinguished fellow-countrymen I
express profound appreciation, and I know that Mr.
Alexander's family would wish me to express on their
part a word of similar appreciation. In this sacred
spot let us all pray to Almighty God that Mr. Alexander's
work may effectively be carried on; that we who are still
in the arena of world affairs may be guided by the highest
wisdom and inspiration in our constant efforts to build up
the friendship between Japan and the United States; that
those

=== Page 23 === (confidence 0.97)
-3-
thome efforts may be crowned with success, not with mere
mementary suecess, but with a success that will endure
and be strengthened in the difficult years that lie
before us; and that the enlightened work of Wallace
Alexander will not have been in vain.

(Translation by the America-Japan Society of an Address
Delivered by Admiral Kiohisaburo Nomara, Japanese Min-
ister for Foreign Affairs, at the Memorial Service for
Mr. Wallace Alexander, Tokyo Union Church, Tokyo,
Docember 13, 1959.)
On this the thirteenth day of December, 1939, I, the
Minister for Foreign Affairs respectfully speak unto the
spirit of the late Mr. Wallace MoKinney Alexander.
It is indeed with an unbearable sorrow that we mourn
your sudden passing away at Honolulu at the very hour when
the whole world is confronted by an emergency, and the ex-
treme complexity and tension of the situation calls for
the efforts of such a man as you who have made the pro-
motion of international amity and understanding your
life work.
International friendship is predicated upon a real
mutual understanding between nations. It was under this
conviction that you had early dedicated your life to the
cause of Japanese-American friendship through the removal
of misunderstandings and the cultivation of good will and
mutual appreciation and respect between the two peoples.
Your constant and consistent labors of long years for
Japanese-American amity as President of the San Francisco
Chamber of Commerce and also as President of the Japan
Society and Chairman of the Japanese-American Relations
Committee of the same city are recorded imperishably in
the history of Japanese-American Relations. Those of us
who have Japanese-American amity at heart all remember
with

=== Page 25 === (confidence 0.98)
-8-
with gratitude the countless instances of your unselfish
devotion to the cause. Especially were we moved by the
offorts you exerted in connection with the amendment of
the Immigration Aot of 1924. You who went about from
place to place on the Pacifio Coast to create the right
kind of public opinion concerning the question made us
all feel that here was a true friend of Japan. Though
unfortunately your endeavors failed, your name lives
ever in the hearts of our people.
The passing of so great a citizen as yourself is an
irreparable loss not only to the United States but also
to Japan. But I firmly believe that your noble ideal
will not perish from the faoe of the Pacific as an
eternal beacon light to guide Japan and America on the
path of common prosperity and happiness.

AMBASSADOR'S CONGRATULATORY STATEMENT
TO THE MANAGER OF THE OKINAWA NIPPO AND
RESIDENTS OF NAHA, MARCH 31, 1934

TO JAPAN.
Mr. Tokujiro Yafuso,
Manager, OKINAWA NIPPO
Naha Shi,
Okinawa Prefecture,
Ryukyu.
Sir:
It affords me the greatest pleasure to send you
this message of congratulations and goodwill on the
occasion of your celebration to commemorate the epoch-
marking visit of Commodore Matthew Calbraith Perry
to your city of Naha eighty years ago. Although it
is a far cry to that 26th of May 1853 when the "Black
Ships" of the Commodore steamed into Naha Bay, yet the
years have been perfectly bridged by the uninterrupted
maintenance of friendly relations between the United
States and Japan since that time.
It is a source of pride to me, not only as American
Ambassador, but also personally, to recall that one of
my countrymen was responsible for establishing the first
open and friendly contact that any foreign nation had up
to that time enjoyed with Japan. And it is of no little
historic interest that the city of Naha has the unique
distinction of being the spot where my compatriot Commo-
dore Perry first touched Japanese soil. I am very grati-
fied indeed to realize that the populace of Naha takes
so much pride in that distinction, and feel therefore,
that it is most fitting that the eightieth anniversary
of the opening of the Japanese Empire to foreign inter-
course should be celebrated in your city by commemorat-
ing the visit of the "Black Ships" from American shores.
The records of this Embassy include the Commodore's
complete report on his visit to Japan, and many pages are
devoted to his detailed exposition of the courteous and
friendly treatment he was accorded by the people of Ryu-
kyu during his long stay at Naha. The assistance, pro-
tection and shelter extended to the Commodore and his
party during his sojourn in Naha undoubtedly contributed
to his later success in negotiating a Treaty with the
Government of Yedo, which was finally signed, with ap-
propriate ceremony, at Yokohama on Friday March 31, i854.
Since that historic visit the Japanese nation has
grown tremendously and the amazing progress of its de-
velopment is a blazoned highway of which all Japanese
ean be justly proud. I and my fellow Americans are also
proud that a representative of our own nation has been so
instrumental

rise of a greater Japan. We are then more than eager
to honor the memory of the man whose untiring efforts
so permanently founded good relations between our two
nations that an enduring friendship has obtained for
nearly three generations. I am confident therefore,
that the friendship between the United States and Japan
will continue to weather all the storms that from time
to time beset international welfare.
With all good wishes for a successful celebration,
Iam,
Yours sinc erely,
Joseph C. Grew

Ambassador Grew is cited.
Today is the 80th anniversary of the conclusion
of the first Japan-America Treaty of Commerce and Navi-
gation which was signed at Kanagawa on March 30 exactly
80 years ago. Not only in Japan but in the United
States of America, the event was to be celebrated today
at one and the same time. At Shimoda, there will be a
"Black Ship Festival" in the latter part of April in
honor of the event. Greetings were to be exchanged be-
tween Japan and the United States today through the me-
dium of the Broadcasting Stations in Tokyo and San
Francisco.
As Ambassador Grew justly remarks in his message
given the citizens of Naha, the capital of Okinawa Pre-
fecture (Loochoo archipelago), the suecessful establishment
of the Kanagawa Treaty between Commodore Perry and the
Shogunate Government eighty years ago was largely due to
cordial friendly treatment accorded the Black Ship Squa-
dron by the people of the Loochoo archipelago in official
and non-official circles. Commodore Perry in command of
his Squadron first entered the port of Naha on a mission
to Japan. This fact is especially noteworthy. Records in
the archives of the United States Embassy in Tokyo show
that Commodore Perry arrived at Naha on May 26 of 81 years
ago. In view of the fact that the relations between the
United States and Japan are now becoming more friendly
than ever before, the celebration today in memory of the
arrival of Commodore Perry's Squadron under the auspieès
of the Okinawa Nippo with the support of various public
bodies is quite timely. The people of Okinawa Prefecture
have held a few celebrations of international character
in the past. The celebration in memory of the arrival of
the Perry Squadron by the local people this time is
particularly noteworthy in that it would serve to promote
the relations between the two countries and to foster the
knowledge of the local people as to foreign relations.
The fact that Ambassador Grew has cooperated with
us

us for the carrying out of thepresent celebration
is highly gratifying to the citizens of Naha and to
the Okinawa Nippo. Ambassador Grew, in his message,
remarks among other things that "the friendly relations
between Japan and the United States will in no way be
affected by all issues or questions likely to impair
international relations and that this friendly relation
will undoubtedly continue for a long time". This remark
is especially what the Japanese people desire from the
bottom of their hearts. In conclusion, we join the ci-
tizens of Naha in the celebration of this auspicious
event.

WALLACE McKINNEY ALEXANDER
A descendant of early American pioncers to the Hawaiian Islands, Mr. Alexander
was born on the island of Maui in the year 1869. Upon graduation from Yale in the
class of 1892, he entered business, and his since occupiel many positions of importance in
the business and industrial life of the Pacific coast and the Hawaiian Islands. Until the
time of his death he was President of the firm of Alexander and Baldwin, and was also an
official of many other important commercial and industrial organizations. As President of
the San Francisco Chamber of Commercc, Mr. Alexander made a lasting contribution in his
active promotion of closer commercial relationships between Japan and the United States.
Mr. Alexander's interests were far broader than business alone, however. As Presi-
dent of the Japanese Relations Committee and later as President of the Japan Society of
San Francisco, he was instrumental in many ways in promoting understanding and improving
relations between the two countries. He was also active in the Institute of Pacific Relations
from its beginning, and has been for years a 'Trustee of the Carnegie Endowment for
International Peace.
In May, 1929, Mr. Alexander was decorated by His Majesty the Emperor of Japan,
with the Second Class Order of the Sacred Treasure.
Mr. Alexander's extensive interests in the Hawaiian Islands frequently took him back
and forth between the Islands and California. This past month, after being taken ill
while on his last journey to the Islands of his birth, Mr. Alexander passed away :on
November 22, 1939 in Honolulu.

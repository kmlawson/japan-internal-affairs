SEP 10 1948

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to enclose copies of a letter from the Adjutant General's Division of this Headquarters to the Commanding General, Eighth Army dated August 18, 1948 on the subject of the Telephone Bond Law recently enacted by the Japanese Government, and in that connection to refer to this Mission's despatch No. 540 of August 19, 1948.

The enclosed letter modifies the regulations outlined in the above-mentioned despatch to the extent that it is no longer necessary for foreign commercial residents in Japan to obtain the consent of this Headquarters before buying the telephone bond required by Japanese law.

It will be noted that the Adjutant General's Division considers the bond a method of defraying the expenses of installing a telephone. It is doubtful however, whether it costs the Japanese Government 36,000 yen to install a telephone except in extreme cases. Frequently, as for instance when the telephone and circuit rights have been obtained through a broker, the bond is clearly a revenue measure, the funds obtained thereby, according to the Tokyo Central Telephone Exchange, being used to maintain and expand the telephone system.

Further indication that the measure is akin to a tax on prospective telephone users is seen in the fact that there is an installation charge of 1500 yen in addition to the telephone bond, according to the Tokyo Central Telephone Exchange.

Officials of the Civil Communications Section of this Headquarters admit that the charge is less for services rendered than for revenue to expand the system, the Ministry of Communications being unable to raise money from any other source. They hope that, once the system is improved, the "broker" in rights to telephone circuits will disappear, and the applicant will be able in all cases to go directly to the Ministry of Communications for telephone service. The telephone bond will then represent something akin to payment for the cost of a new telephone circuit, rather than the present forced loan over and above charges customarily levied for telephone rights by private dealers.

This mission has taken the position that such improvements in service should be financed through increased maintenance charges, the present charges being considered more than those in force in many countries.

18 August 1948

SUBJECT: Telephone Bond Law

TO: Commanding General
Eighth Army
APO 343

1. On 28 May 1948 a law was passed by the Japanese Government providing for the sale of telephone bonds to applicants for telephone service. Under the terms of the law the Ministry of Communications determines the average cost of installing a telephone over a six months period and this figure is set as the price of a bond which the applicant must buy to obtain a telephone. The bond is redeemable in 15 years and pays 4 per cent interest.

2. On 25 June 1948 a Ministerial order was promulgated setting the price of the telephone bond at 36,000 yen. All applications for telephones in Japan except for the government or occupation forces, but including commercial entrants, missionaries, foreign banks, etc., became subject to this bond law. Telephone service installed, except for the government or occupation forces, on and after 25 June became subject to the bond law even though the application was made earlier.

3. The Japanese Government hitherto has obtained the capital with which to provide telephone facilities by means of an installation charge of ¥ 800.00 and registration charge of ¥ 150.00 per subscriber. Prior to the advent of severe inflation this was adequate, since in terms of dollars this amounted to between $200.00 and $400.00. At the present time, this is quite insufficient, and, since no other means is available for obtaining the needed capital, it is necessary to borrow the money from the ultimate users by means of the telephone bonds.

4. Telephone service charges are not excessive by comparison with U. S. rates. The rate per month in Tokyo, for example, is ¥ 480.00 for a business telephone and ¥ 300.00 per month for a residence telephone. To these figures ¥ 2.00 should be added for each completed outgoing call. Typical rates in the U. S. for message rate service are $5.00 per month and 4¢ per message in excess of 60 for a business telephone, and $2.50 per month and 4¢ per message in excess of 40 for a residence telephone.

5. Commercial entrants licensed to do business in Japan are not violating their licenses by the purchase of these telephone bonds. Inasmuch as the purchase of such Japanese Government Bonds is not a voluntary purchase, but is a statutory prerequisite under a SCAP approved law for the installation of an instrument necessary in the normal operation of the business, it is considered that such purchase is construed as a cost of doing business. Accordingly, the purchase of Japanese Government Bonds for the installation of telephones does not come within the purview of the individual license which prohibits the use of yen for investments.

6. The attention of personnel arranging priorities is invited to Memorandum for the Japanese Government, file AG 311.3 (12 Aug 46) CCS, SCAPIN 1122, dated 12 August 1946, subject: Residence Telephone Service for Foreign Nationals and Stateless Personnel Employed by the Occupation Forces, which provides a means for obtaining telephone service for foreign nationals and stateless personnel employed by the occupation forces. These applications are also subject to the terms of the bond law and the purchase of the bond will be from the funds of the applicant and not chargeable to occupation forces.

Subject: Regulations Regarding Speculation in Telephone Rights.
^{}[]
The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to refer to this Mission's despatches No. 540 of August 19, 1948, "Procurement of Communications Services for Non-Occupation Use in Japan", and No. 579 of September 3, 1948, "Japanese Telephone Bond Law", and to forward five copies of a SCAP Memorandum for the Japanese government (SCAPIN 1964) of January 17, 1949, "Instructions Pertaining to Treatment of Telephone Rights and Prohibition of Resale of Public Telecommunications Services".
^{}[]
SCAPIN 1964 is designed to curb the flourishing trade in "telephone rights" discussed in our despatch No. 540. The transfer between telephone users of contracts, agreements, and registrations issued on or after February 15, 1949, covering government telecommunications services, is prohibited. Agreements, etc., entered into between the government and a telephone user on or after that date are not to be honored if transferred to a second party by the original recipient. Similar documents issued prior to February 15, 1949, are not covered by the directive.
^{}[]
The Japanese government is enjoined by SCAPIN 1964 from approving applications for telephones which cannot reasonably be filled within three months, and is required to make reserve supplies of telecommunications equipment immediately available to the public. Telecommunications facilities held in reserve for eventual use by holders of telephone rights issued before February 15, 1949 are to be held for such utilization only until April 1, 1949. The latter provision applies to holders of telephone rights who, principally because of war damage, have been unable to take advantage of those rights.
^{}[]
Future recipients of telecommunications services and facilities are prohibited from reselling, subletting or renting such services and facilities. Owners of rights acquired before February 15, 1949, however, are allowed to transfer rights and services, provided that the right and the service are transferred simultaneously and to the same party; thus it is planned to curb the spread of the current practice of retaining telephone rights while renting services.

SCAPIN 1964

MEMORANDUM FOR: JAPANESE GOVERNMENT

SUBJECT: Instructions Pertaining to Treatment of Telephone
Rights and Prohibition of Resale of Public Tele-
communications Services

1. It is directed that on and after 15 February 1949 the Japanese
Government cease to recognize as valid the transfer between users, of
contracts, agreements, or registrations issued on or after that date
which cover telecommunications services or facilities furnished from
Government-owned telecommunications systems. The agreement entered
into on or after 15 February 1949 by agencies of the Government to
furnish telecommunications services or facilities to an individual or
organization will be binding only insofar as the original recipient is
concerned and will not be recognized nor honored if transferred from
the original recipient to a second party by agreement between the two
parties. This paragraph does not apply to such documents issued prior
to 15 February 1949, or to their subsequent transfer or registration,
or to telecommunications services furnished thereunder.

2. On and after 15 February 1949 the Japanese Government will not
approve a greater number of applications for telecommunications service
than can reasonably be expected to be fulfilled within three months
after the date of approval of the application by the Japanese Government.

3. On and after 15 February 1949 telecommunications services
furnished by the Japanese Government and any document evidencing or calling
for the same, other than those referred to in the last sentence of
paragraph one above, shall have no value, under Japanese law, as real
or personal property.

4. All presently unused and unreserved telecommunications facilities
of the Japanese Government will immediately be made available to provide
telecommunications service to those making proper application for
immediate service under the terms of current laws and regulations of
the Japanese Government. The same procedure will be followed for tele-
communications facilities which become available in the future. Regulations
of the Japanese Government covering installation priorities
will be strictly adhered to.

5. All telecommunications facilities presently reserved by the
Japanese Government to fulfill eventual demands for telecommunications
service to the holders of telephone rights acquired prior to 15 February
1949, will be held available for such service to such right holders only

DIRECT JAPANESE TO ABOLISH PRIVATE SPECULATION IN TELEPHONES
^{}[]
In a detailed directive issued yesterday, SCAP instructed the Japanese Government to cease to recognize as valid the transfer, between users, of contracts, agreements or registrations issued on or after Feb. 15, 1949, which cover telecommunication services or facilities furnished from government-owned systems which are not covered by telephone rights existing before that date.
^{}[]
In effect, the directive eliminates after Feb. 15 the so-called "telephone right" from any document issued or transaction entered into which covers the initial procurement after that date of such government services except when such procurement is obtained under a "right" in effect at that time.
^{}[]
According to SCAP's Civil Communications Section, the undemocratic practice of selling "telephone rights" dates back to the very beginning of the telephone in Japan. Once having obtained telephone service through the payment of registration and installation fees, a subscriber was entitled to telephone service indefinitely. Thus, the registration became recognized as a permanent right of the purchaser which could be disposed of as he saw fit. Due to scarcity of telephones and the great value placed thereon, the intangible "telephone right" became much the same as a stock certificate, with values determined by supply and demand.
^{}[]
It is reported that these "rights" have sold for as high as 55,000 yen in the Tokyo area. In most cases the trading in rights is very profitable and although the services being sold are furnished by the government, none of the profits are ever paid to the government except as taxes paid by the trader.
^{}[]
The concept of a "telephone right" having real value is not as illogical as it might appear. Registration and installation fees in the past were extremely high, and funds from these charges were used indirectly to finance construction and installation of telephone facilities. In Tokyo, the two charges combined were about equal to the annual income of an average citizen. The price paid was an appreciable part of the cost for buying and installing all materials necessary to provide and serve a telephone installation. The high cost denied the average Japanese any chance of obtaining telephone services.

In order to eliminate this unfair situation, the installation and registration charges were frozen in 1945 and were not advanced in proportion to increases in the monthly rates for telephone service due to economic conditions. If such increases had been made these fees would now be between 45,000 yen and 50,000 yen.

At the present time registration and installation fees, which total 1,800 yen, represent the same type of charge that is assessed for a telephone installation in the United States. Since these charges no longer represent any appreciable portion of the actual cost of equipment and materials necessary to furnish telephone service there is no justification for assigning them a real value.

In the future, it will not be necessary for a subscriber to purchase a "right" from a broker or another individual in order to obtain a telephone connection. As telephones become available, they will be assigned to applicants for service on a priority basis somewhat similar to the system used in the United States during the war. Upon acceptance of his application, the subscriber will pay the present relatively low installation fee, and, as a means for assisting in the provision of additional plant, will be required to purchase government bonds to the amount of 36,000 yen. These bonds are redeemable and interest bearing and have limited negotiability.

However the telephone service itself not covered by a "right" outstanding before Feb. 15, is no longer negotiable. The subscriber has the privilege of moving the telephone from place to place to fill his own needs, but cannot sell his right for service to another party.

Another undesirable practice the directive prohibits is the resale of service; the case of the holder leasing the use of a telephone to another party but retaining ownership of the "telephone right". Cases have been reported where the rental charge is as high as ten and twelve times the legal service rate, leaving the "right" owner a source of income from one telephone over and above that of workers in the upper average wage bracket. Although there may be a basis for the "telephone right" to entitle the owner to trade his right for service as he sees fit, the right does not entitle him to resell the actual service at rates different than those set by law.

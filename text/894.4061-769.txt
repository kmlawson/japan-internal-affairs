MEMORANDUM.
I saw the moving picture DAIGOREI (The Great Order)
yesterday (June 27th). It is a patriotic propaganda picture,
made with the cooperation of the Army and Navy, under the
auspices of the Osaka MAINICHI and Tokyo NICHINICHI. The
great theme is the crisis of 1935-36, but so many side issues
are introduced that all continuity is lost. The film is
largely made up of shots from various sources patched together.
The reasons for the approaching crisis are given as the
naval conference, the mandate island issue, the Russian
preparations for war, Japan's withdrawal from the League,
and trade rivalries throughout the world. Most of these
subjects are just mentioned, and then allowed to drop without
development, but the Russian menace is pictured at some length,
with pictures of Red cavalry charges, industrial progress,
marching troops, tanks, airplanes, etc., in sufficient numbers
to make even the calmest Japanese blood freeze. The voice
of the picture stated that Manchuria was menaced by these
Russian activities, and that the "poisoned fangs" of the
Soviets were already fastening on Mongolia and parts of China.
There was some propaganda directed against the United
States, mainly in the matter of naval affairs, but this was
relatively and surprisingly little.
A great deal of attention is given to trade rivalry.
The picture shows how trade rivalries brought on the Great
War, and then points to the world's jealousy in the face of
Japan's rapidly expanding trade. The pressure of population
in Japan is pictured as making overseas trade necessary and
inevitable.
There

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-
There is a great deal about United Asia; about the
brotherhood of the Asiatic races, and about Japan's role
as leader of the Orient. One of the most interesting parts
to me was a map showing the world divided into "blocs", with
Japan shut off from trade. Then another map pictures the
Far East, with a chain which circles around to enclose all
of the East, including China, India, Philippines, East Indies.
This chain fastens with a lock which carries a Japanese flag.
Cenerals Hayashi and Araki were pictured in close-ups,
explaining Japan's position in the world today; the justice
of her actions in China and Manchuria; the destiny of the
Yamato race; the necessity for girding up the national
loins to face any possible emergency. Matsuoka was pictured
explaining Japan's attitude vis-a-vis the League and the
necessity for Japan's withdrawal.
The picture to me was really puerile, as propaganda
pictures usually are. There was a very slim crowd (Mr. Hughes
tells me that he couldn't get seats in a nearby theatre where
an American movie was showing), and there was little enthu-
siasm shown.
It is noted that according to Fengo, Pravda objects to
the Film DAIGOREI as propaganda against Russia. There is
cause for objection. I should resent remarks made about my
"poisoned fangs".
W. T. Turner
June 28, 1934.

embargo.
In line with this idea, they say that the total im-
portation of foreign pictures might be limited to one hundred pic-
tures per year, on a quarterly license basis of twenty-five pic-
tures. This total would have to be distributed among the fifteen
importers of foreign films and we could be certain that on account
of political reasons Italian and German pictures would receive a
much higher percentage of the total quota than they warrant. Of
the 283 foreign pictures released in Tokio during 1937, all except
55 were American pictures, but it is quite unlikely that this pro-
portion would be maintained in any quota system initiated in Japan.
It is, of course, known that film production in Italy and
Germany is almost a negligible quantity now, but they would be given
relative preference regardless because of the political alliances
existing between those countries and Japan, and some provision would
have to be made for British and French pictures. American pictures
may, therefore, be given a total quota of seventy pictures per annum
or even less. A few pictures from that number would be alloted to
the Japanese companies importing independent American product, and
the balance would have to be split up between the eight major distri-
butors represented here. In that case, it might be well for these
eight companies to stand together and refuse to accept such a quota.
That would require united action by the New York orrices as no such
agreement could be made here. However, it has been decided by the

Eight representatives of this group called on the
Ambassador by appointment at l0 o'clock this morning to
lay before the Embassy the difficulties which they were
encountering in obtaining fulfillment of an arrangement
entered into between the American Motion Picture Associa-
tion of Japan and the Japanese Government.
Mr. Bergher, spokesman of the group, stated their
problem as follows: He said that an informal arrangement
had been entered into between his group and the Japanese
Foreign Office under which the American group were to be
permitted to import up to $30,000 worth of American films
and to remit the proceeds in an amount up to Yen 3,000,000.
This agreement was to have expired December 31, l938.
Mr. Bergher stated that no difficulties had arisen up
to the present with regard to the remittance of the proceeds
from the films already imported but that the Association had
so far been able to import only some $20,000 worth of
films; that the $10,000 worth of films remaining under
the agreement had been imported prior to December 31,
1938 and were still in the Custom House at Yokohama,
the Japanese Government having refused entry. The
spokesman

release the l0,Oo0 worth of films now in the Customs

2. THE NEW MOTION PICTURE LAW.
At the end of September the Home Ministry official-
ly notified the American Motion Picture Association of
the provisions of the new Motion Picture Law. The Law
will be enforced from Jenuary lst, 1940. Under a pro-
vision of this Law, the members of the American Motion
Picture Association will be placed on a quota basis for
the distribution of pictures in Japan. All indications
are that the quota for each individual distributor will
be extremely small as compared to the average number of
pictures imported and distributed by the members of this
Association heretofore.
The Home Ministry, however, in discussing the appli-
cation of the New Motion Picture Law with various members
of the Industry have indicated that they intend to take
into consideration the diplomatic and economic relation-
ship existing between Japan and various other countries.
Of the total number of foreign pictures distributed in
Japan in normal times, the American pictures occupied
the major portion. Even since the restrictions were put
into effect, the Amerioan Motion Picture Distributors
represented by this Association, have been importing and
releasing approximately 80% of the total foreign produot.

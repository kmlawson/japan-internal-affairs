# The Food Problem In Japan

## L—Quantity: “There Is No Food Shortage In Japan.”

By Egerton Charles Grey, M. M., D. Sc., F. I. C., M. R. C. S. etc., Who Has Been In Japan Investigating Food Problems for the League of Nations.

Special to The Japan Advertiser

That there is a food problem in Japan there can be no doubt, but what precisely is the nature of the problem, probably few realize. The Japanese Government has certainly the matter in mind, for the question of maintaining an adequate food supply has a very obvious importance in a country where the population is on the increase. In the past, as is well known fears of insufficiency of food, have led to drastic measures for birth control, and a certain alarm in regard to food supply has scarcely ever been absent. Naturally the problem is preeminently one of concern to the Japanese people, but none the less it cannot be entirely disregarded by other countries, for it has a most important bearing on emigration, colonization, foreign trade and foreign policy, and on many other matters which will be obvious to the reader who is at all familiar with the conditions of Japan. There is moreover the humanitarian consideration to which it is to be hoped none is indifferent, and it is particularly in this regard that the problem of the relation of food to health in Japan, comes to the attention of the medical world, and for this reason that the Health Section of the League of Nations, has thought it possible to offer some help. Under the auspices of this section the writer was sent to Japan to study the food problem about a year ago, and has remained here during this time, studying the food materials themselves and eiling the mass of somewhat heterogeneous information relating to the food supply which is contained in the statistical and other reports available in the country. The work is now in a certain respect completed and the results have been gathered into a report which will appear in due course. Pending the appearance of this report however, it will be of help to many interested in the problems, to have a general summary of the conclusions reached and of the facts upon which they have been based, and this is what the present communication offers. The matter will be dealt with in three articles, written for The Japan Advertiser, dealing with the following three aspects of the problem; The Quantity of Food in Japan; The Quality of Food in Japan; The means of improving the conditions, particularly in regard to the health of the people.

For many years past Japanese publicists and lecturers and writers of all nationalities who have not made a close study of the situation, have conveyed the impression to readers and hearers abroad that Japan lives on the borderline of starvation. One hears constantly about the "necessity of obtaining food supplies for the teeming millions", or "the rapidly-increasing population" of Japan, and about the alleged fact that "every inch of land is cultivated." Especially since the recent Japanese military activities in Manchuria and China Proper there have been numerous references in official and unofficial utterances to the allegation that Manchuria is Japan's "life-line", or that "Manchuria is vital to the existence of the Japanese nation", or that partial or entire control of Manchuria is a "matter of life or death" to Japan. The impression is undoubtedly created that Japan, perhaps more than any other country in the world, is in a precarious condition as far as foodstuffs are concerned, and that emigration to less densely populated countries or, as an alternative, expansion on the mainland of Asia, is an indispensable condition to the continued existence of the nation.

The idea which has thus been created abroad regarding Japan's dependence on foreign supplies of foodstuffs is almost entirely erroneous. Japan is dependent on imported foodstuffs to a less extent than are any of the larger nations of Western Europe. For the purpose of the comparison, the net per capita imports of foodstuffs (i.e., the gross imports of foodstuffs, less the gross exports, divided by the population) has been used. Such a country would have an excess of imports of foodstuffs but could not be said to be dependent on foreign foodstuffs to any vital extent. Vice versa, a country which imports large quantities of basic foodstuffs but more than pays for them by its exports of high-priced luxury foodstuffs, as does Holland, would have an excess of exports of foodstuffs, but would still be dependent to some extent on imported foodstuffs. However, in general the net per capita imports of foodstuffs may be considered as reasonably indicative of any one nation's degree of dependency on other lands for its food.

This reiteration has undoubtedly created the worldwide impression that Japan, perhaps more than any other country in the world, is in a precarious position as far as foodstuffs are concerned, that poor crops will cause famines such as those from which China and India suffer, and that emigration of Japanese to less populous countries or, as an alternative, expansion on the mainland of Asia, are indispensable conditions to the continued existence of the Japanese nation. There have been several painstaking investigations of the food resources of Japan, but the publication of the findings has made little or no impression on the habit of mind created by the constant reiteration of the proposition that Japan lives on the borderline of starvation.

Moreover, a study of food resources alone is not conclusive. To determine the vulnerability of a people to starvation, the food resources should be studied in relation to the dietetic habits of the people. One people might wax fat on what would be a starvation diet to another. A better index of the dependency of a nation on foodstuffs brought from abroad would appear to consist of the per capita net imports of foodstuffs - the amount of foodstuffs purchased from abroad for each member of the population, less the amount sold to foreign countries. In other words, the gross imports of foodstuffs less the gross exports, divided by the population. It is true that this index has some faults, chief among which

=== Page 19 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

which is the variation in standards of living in the different countries. In the United States, where the standard of living is very high and the people consume great quantities of comparatively expensive foodstuffs, such as coffee and bananas, it might reasonably be expected that the per capita purchases of imported foodstuffs would be much greater than the per capita purchases of the Chinese, where the standard of living is very low and where only cheap, basic foodstuffs can be purchased and consumed by the masses. Also, a nation whose imports consist largely of luxuries cannot be said to be dependent on foreign supplies of foodstuffs, however large a per capita importation it may have, while another country, whose importations of foodstuffs consist of necessities, undoubtedly is to that extent dependent on foreign supplies of foods. However, in general the net per capita imports of foodstuffs may be considered as reasonably indicative of any one nation's degree of dependency on other lands for its food.

There appears to be no doubt, therefore, that most of the nations of western Europe are dependent upon other countries for foodstuffs to a much greater extent than is Japan. Switzerland has to buy from other countries twenty-five times more foodstuffs per capita than does Japan. Yet one does not hear that, because Switzerland is unable to provide food for its population, it should be allowed to take and occupy New Zealand in order to secure adequate food supplies. In the Netherlands 77 per cent of the land is cultivated as fields, pastures, forests, etc.; in Japan only 24.9 per cent is so cultivated.

miners was promulgated in Japan. Artiale 5 of that law

the China incident, for Japan's "wartime" economy. The
ordinances came into force on October 20, 1939, and by

Japan's "wartime" price control legislation entered
its first stage with the passage of the Law Concerning
the Regulation of Excess Profits in August, 1937. The
enactment in July of the following year of Regulations
Concerning the Control of Commodity Prices, which set an
upper limit for prices and established "official" prices,
may be said to mark the second stage. The plan for the
control of commodity prices (compare my despatch No. 3869
of May 8, 1939) adopted by the Central Price Committee
in April, l939, althouch productive of no results, fore-
told the early appearance of the third stage, an appear-
ance which was only hastened by the impact of the European
war in September, 1939, upon Japan's economy. It might
well be said that price control legislation had up to
this time trailed behind price advances and that the
Government's policy had been to "go slow".
The promulgation on October 16, 1939, of ordinances
pegging prices and wages at the level which had obtained
on September 18, 1939, marked in definite fashion the third
phase of price control in Japan. However, there were
important exceptions in the new legislation, for example,
prices on commodity exchanges; prices of land and buildings;
prices of negotiable instruments, rights of enterprises,
rights in intangibles; and prices of commodities which might
be used in export trade, or raw materials for export com-
modities, such as comestibles, raw silk, cotton, and

of the commodities normally entering the Japanese marts
of trade have been marked out for control. It is worth
noting, however, that the system of "official" prices
which had existed since July, 1938, for certain commodi-
ties was combined with one of "agreed" prices, that is,
prices which had been agreed upon as a result of confer-
ences between government and business. The thread which
runs plainly through all these measures is the emphasis
by the Government upon the importance of cooperation on
the part of the people. The manner of approach to the
problem is still that of a free capitalism.
Under the authority of the ordinances promulgated
on October 16, l939, the Government proceeded, despite
the appearance of danger signals pointing towards infla-
tion, in leisurely fashion with the tremendous task which
lay before it, literally that of convincing a people that
a state of fixed low prices for commodities the supply of
which could not be assured was better than being abundantly
supplied with commodities at speculative prices. The
Government itself soon went against the spirit of the
price-pegging ordinances by raising the price of rice,
charcoal, tobacco, and other commodities, but told its
oritics that these steps did not indicate that the low-
price policy had been abandoned. The result was one whioh
might be expected: widespread speculation occurred, prices
continued to rise, and stooks of many commodities dis-

and temporary, and their forecast was soon borne out, for
wholesale prices in Tokyo, according to the same index,

"The Government is endeavoring to determine

been stabilized, but this admittedly is a most dif-

It must not be supposed, however, from the foregoing
discussion that there is any acute shortage of essential
commodities. Japan is in the throes of a price orgy. The
price of many commodities has skyrocketed almost out of
sight. Everyone wishes to buy and no one wants to sell.
The rice situation, with respect to which the Embassy has

there is undoubtedly some shortage of this commodity, the

told with respect to numerous other commodities: stocks
of most goods are ample, but no one wishes to part with
them. There is no sign of actual want. There is, to be
sure, considerable grumbling against the Government for
the dilatory manner in which it has attacked the price
problem, but no indication of acute social unrest. On
the contrary, the fact that the Japanese people have become
used to certain privations and restrictions would appear
to have better prepared them for the situation they are now

one of relaxing its price control policy with the result
that wealth will become concentrated in a speculative

"The Government is preoccupied with the speedy

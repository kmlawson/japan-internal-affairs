The Japanese Ambassador presents his compliments to the Honorable the Secretary of State and has the honor to transmit the following information, relative to the measurement of tonnage of vessels regarding which the two countries have been according reciprocal recognition by virtue of their respective laws, that the Imperial Government has amended the law No. 34 of March 30, 1914, by the law No. 6 of the sixth year of Showa (1931), and that the amendment went into effect on the 20th of June, 1932, and has the honor to enclose herewith two copies of the English translation of the new law and the regulations thereunder which have been prepared by the Japanese authorities concerned.

In this connection, the Ambassador has the honor to express a desire, in behalf of his government, for the continuance of the reciprocal recognition of certificates of ship-measurement. It should be mentioned that the new rules are substantially identical with those which were in force before the

The amendment

1. changes the words "register tonnage" adopted in the former law to the words "net tonnage",
2. and necessitates the tonnage of all vessels to be expressed in tons of 1000/353 cubic metres which are substantially equivalent to tons of one hundred cubic feet adopted in the former system,
3. and furnishes simpler regulations for the measurement of the capacity of ships under twenty metres in length.

In the transient stage, however, the regulations now superseded will continue to apply to such vessels as have not yet undergone the re-determination of tonnage. In these circumstances, it should be noted that there will be, for some time, presumably up to the 30th of September, 1934, two classes of Japanese vessels: those with tonnage of the new law measurement, and those with that of the old law measurement.

# THE SHIP MEASUREMENT LAW.

*Law No. 34 of March 30th, 1914.*
*Amended by the Law No. 6 of the 6th year of Showa (1931).*

**ARTICLE I.**—The capacity of ships shall be ascertained by measuring their inside volumes, the unit of which shall be one cubic metre.

**ARTICLE II.**—The tonnage deck is the upper deck in ships which have one or two decks, and the second deck from below in ships which have three or more decks.

**ARTICLE III.**—The gross capacity is the sum of the capacity below the tonnage deck and the capacity of the closed-in spaces above the tonnage deck in ships with one or two decks; the sum of the capacity below the tonnage deck, the capacity of the spaces between decks and the capacity of the closed-in spaces above the upper deck in ships with three or more decks; provided that the capacity of the following spaces, when situated above the upper deck, shall not be included in the gross capacity.

1. The spaces used for the working of the helm, the capstan and the anchor gear; the spaces occupied by the donkey boiler and engine, if not connected with the main machinery.
2. Machinery spaces, wheel-houses, Cook-houses and companion houses.
3. Light and air spaces and water-closets.
4. Any space deemed by the competent Minister of State to be similar to the above mentioned spaces as regards the safety, sanitation or the use of the spaces of the ship.

On the request of the owner, the whole or a part of the capacity of the machinery spaces above the upper deck may be added to the gross capacity, if the competent Minister of State approves it.

1

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
In open ships, the gross capacity is the sum of the capacity below the gunwale and the capacity of the closed-in spaces above the gunwale.

ARTICLE IV.—In ascertaining the net capacity, the capacity of the following spaces shall be deducted from the gross capacity, but no deduction shall be allowed in respect of any space which has not been included in the gross capacity:—

1. Crew spaces and chart rooms.
2. Water ballast tanks.
3. Machinery spaces.
4. The spaces used for the working of the helm, the capstan and the anchor gear; the spaces occupied by the donkey boiler and engine, if connected with the main pumps of the ship.
5. Boatswain’s stores.
6. Sail rooms of the sailing ship.
7. Any space deemed by the competent Minister of State to be similar to the above mentioned spaces as regards the safety, sanitation or the use of the spaces of the ship.

ARTICLE V.—The extent and limit of the spaces prescribed in the preceding two articles shall be determined by the rules which shall be made by the competent Minister of State.

ARTICLE VI.—In ascertaining the net capacity, the capacity to be deducted from the gross capacity as the capacity of the space occupied by the propelling power, shall be determined by the following proportions:—

1. As regards ships propelled by screws, in which the capacity of the actual machinery spaces is above thirteen per cent. and under twenty per cent. of the gross capacity, the deduction shall be thirty-two one-hundredths of the gross capacity; and in ships propelled by paddle wheels, in which the capacity of the actual machinery spaces is above twenty per cent. and under thirty per cent. of the gross capacity, the deduction shall be thirty-seven one-hundredths of the gross capacity.

=== Page 8 === (confidence 0.99)
2. In cases which do not conform to the preceding section, the deduction shall consist of the capacity of the actual machinery spaces, with the addition in the case of ships propelled by screws of three-fourths, and in the case of ships propelled by paddle wheels of one-half of the capacity of the spaces; but on the request of the owner, if the competent Minister of State approves it, the deduction may be estimated in the same manner as prescribed in the preceding section.

The deduction for the machinery spaces shall not in any case exceed fifty-five per cent. of that portion of the capacity of the ship which remains after deducting from the gross capacity all deductions allowed in this Law except the machinery spaces.

ARTICLE VII.—The deduction for the capacity of the sail rooms shall not in any case exceed two and a half per cent. of the gross capacity.

ARTICLE VIII.—The gross capacity or net capacity of ships expressed in Tons (100 cubic metres) shall be respectively called the gross tonnage or net tonnage.

ARTICLE IX.—The regulations for measuring the capacity of ships shall be made by the competent Minister of State.

ARTICLE IX-2.—For measuring the capacity of ships under twenty metres in length, the competent Minister of State may establish special regulations notwithstanding the provisions of Art. II to Art. VII of this Law.

## CHAPTER I.

### General Regulations.

**Article I.**—The measurement of the capacity of ships 20 metres and above in length shall be carried out in accordance with these Regulations.

The length prescribed above means the horizontal distance from the fore side of the stern to the aft side of the stern post, measured on the range of the upper deck beams in decked ships and on the top surface of the upper most strake of the outside plating or planking in open ships.

**Article 1-2.**—The unit for the measurement of the length, breadth, depth, height and thickness shall be one metre and decimals shall be taken to two places by increasing the second decimal by one if the third is 5 or more.

In calculating the common intervals of division of the length or depth, the one-third of them, the area, volume and capacity, decimals shall be taken to three places by increasing the third decimal by one if the fourth is 5 or more; provided that in calculating the gross tonnage or net tonnage, decimals shall be taken to two places by increasing the second decimal by one if the third is 5 or more.

**Article II.**—The length of the tonnage deck is as follows:—

=== Page 15 === (confidence 0.99)
1. In decked ships, the length measured in a straight line along the upper side of the tonnage deck, at the middle line of the ship, from the inside of the inner plank at the bow to the inside of the inner plank at the stern, deducting from this length what is due to the rake of the stern in the thickness of the deck, and what is due to the rake of the stern framing in the thickness of the deck and also what is due to the rake of the stern framing in one-third of the round of the beam.

2. In open ships, the length measured on the top surface of the upper strake of the outside plating or planking at the middle line of the ship from the inside of the inner plank at the bow to the inside of the inner plank at the stern.

ARTICLE III.—The points of division of the length are the points which divide the length of the tonnage deck into equal parts according to the following table, as well as the points at the fore and aft ends of the length.

|  Length of the tonnage deck | Number of divisions  |

1. In decked ships, the depth measured at the middle line of the ship, from the under side of the tonnage deck to the upper side of inner bottom plating of double bottom, floor plate or floor timber, deducting therefrom the average thickness of the bottom ceiling and one-third of the round of the beam.

2. When the inner bottom of the ship of the preceding section is convex, the mean of the depths measured at the middle line of the ship from the under side of the tonnage deck to the upper

When the thickness of the ceiling is not uniform, the average thickness shall be deemed to be the thickness of the ceiling.

The shelter deck shall not be taken into account of the number of decks prescribed in Art. 2 of the Ship Measurement Law.

# CHAPTER II.

The capacity below the tonnage deck and the capacity below the gunwale.

Article XII.—The transverse area at each point of division of the length shall be estimated in accordance with the following provisions:

1. If the depth is divided into four or six equal parts, number the points of division of the depth from above; multiply the breaths at the points due to even number by four and the breadths at the points due to odd number by two, except the first and the last; add these products together, and to the sum add the breadths at the first and the last points; multiply the quantity thus obtained by one-third of the common interval between the points of division of the depth.

2. If the depth is divided into five equal parts, number the points of division of the depth from above; for the part above the fifth

ARTICLE XIII.—The capacity below the tonnage deck shall be ascertained in the following manner, except where otherwise expressly stated: number the transverse areas at the points of division of the length from the bow, and multiply every even-numbered areas by four, and every odd-numbered areas except the first and the last by two; add these products together, and to the sum add the first and the last; multiply the quantity thus obtained by one-third of the common interval between the points of division of the length.

# CHAPTER III.

## The capacity above the tonnage deck and the capacity above gunwale.

Article XVII.—The capacity of the spaces between decks above the tonnage deck shall be ascertained in the following manner: measure the length, at the middle of the height between decks in the centre line of the ship, from the inside of the inner plank at the bow to the inside of the inner plank at the stern, and divide the length into the same number of equal parts into which the length of the tonnage deck is divided; measure at the middle of the height between decks the inside breadth of the space at each point of division; number them from the bow, multiply the even-numbered breadths by four and the odd-numbered breadths except the first and the last by two, and to the sum of these products add the first and the last breadths; multiply the whole sum by one-third of the common interval between the points of division of the length and the mean height between decks.

Article XVIII.—The capacity of the closed-in spaces above the upper deck or above the gunwale shall be ascertained in the following manner:

1. For the erections, deck houses or other closed-in spaces which do not exceed in length the one-half of the length of the tonnage deck, measure at the middle height of the space the inside

=== Page 20 === (confidence 0.99)
breadths at both ends and the middle of the length; to four times of the middle breadth add the end breadths, multiply the sum by one-sixth of the mean length and the mean height of the space.

2. For the erections, deck houses or other closed-in spaces which exceed in length the one-half of the length of the tonnage deck, divide the length into four equal parts and similarly apply the provisions of the preceding Art.

ARTICLE XIX.—On the request of the owner in accordance with the provisions of the second paragraph of Art. 3 of the Ship Measurement Law, the addition of the whole or a part of the machinery spaces above the upper deck to the gross capacity shall be granted only in the case to get the result of decreasing the net capacity.

The capacity of the machinery spaces prescribed in the preceding paragraph shall include the capacity of the machinery casings above the upper deck and the closed-in spaces annexed to the casings.

A part of the capacity of the machinery spaces prescribed in the first paragraph of this Art. means the capacity of the spaces which is the part of the machinery spaces, enclosed by decks or their prolonged planes and casings.

**ARTICLE I.**—The measurement of the capacity of ships under 20 metres in length shall be carried out in accordance with these regulations.

**ARTICLE II.**—The length of a ship is the horizontal distance from the fore side of the stem to the aft side of the stern post measured on the range of the upper deck beams.

The breadth of a ship is the horizontal distance to the outside of the frame at the broadest part of the ship.

The depth of a ship is the vertical distance from the top of the upper deck beam at side to the top of the keel measured at the middle of the length.

**ARTICLE III.**—In open ships, the top surface of the upper most stroke of the outside plating or planking shall be taken as the top surface of the upper deck beam.

In way of the sunken forecastle deck, raised quarter deck or other similar decks, an imaginary surface continuous to the upper deck and parallel to the above mentioned decks, shall be taken as the upper deck.

In ships with the upper deck in part only, the continuation of the upper deck assumed parallel to the gunwale in way of portions without the upper deck shall be taken as the upper deck.

In ships with the other special construction, the length, breadth and depth of the ships shall be determined by applying the preceding article with necessary modifications according to the construction.

**ARTICLE IV.**—The unit for the measurement of the length, breadth, depth and height shall be one metre and decimals shall be taken to two places by increasing the second decimal by one if the third is 5 or more.

In calculating the volume and capacity, decimals shall be taken to three places by increasing the third decimal by one if the fourth is 5 or more; provided that in calculating the gross tonnage and net tonnage, decimals shall be taken to two places by increasing the second decimal by one if the third is 5 or more.

ARTICLE V.—In decked ships, the gross capacity is the sum of the capacity below the upper deck and the capacity of the closed-in spaces above the upper deck; provided that the capacity of the following spaces, when situated above the upper deck, shall not be included in the gross capacity.

1. Machinery spaces, wheel houses, cook-houses and companion houses.
2. Light and air spaces and water-closets.
3. Exposed hatchways under one metre in length or in breadth.
4. Any space deemed by the competent Minister of State to be similar to the above mentioned spaces as regards the safety, sanitation or the use of the spaces of the ship.

In open ships, the gross capacity is the sum of the capacity below the gunwale and the capacity of the closed-in spaces above the gunwale.

ARTICLE VI.—When erections, deck houses and other closed-in spaces above the upper deck have at side or end an opening of 91 centimetres or above in breadth, 122 centimetres or above in height and not fitted with permanent means of closing, they shall not be considered as the closed-in spaces in the meaning of the preceding article, unless such spaces are used for passengers’ accommodation.

ARTICLE VII.—The capacity below the upper deck or the gunwale shall be ascertained by multiplying the product of the ships’ length, breadth and depth by the following coefficient according to the material of ships.

|  Steel ships | 0.62  |
| --- | --- |
|  Wooden ships | 0.55  |

The capacity of the closed-in spaces above the upper deck or gunwale shall be ascertained by adding each volume obtained by multiplying the mean length, breadth and height measured to the insides of the spaces.

# SUPLEMENTARY RULES.

Article VIII.—In ascertaining the net capacity, for ships without propelling machinery, 20 per cent. of the gross capacity shall be deducted from the gross capacity and for ships with propelling machinery, 20 per cent. of the gross capacity and 1.75 times the capacity of the machinery space or 44 per cent. of the gross capacity, whichever is less, shall be deducted from the gross capacity.

The capacity of the machinery space shall be ascertained by multiplying the mean length, breadth and depth measured to the insides of the machinery space below the upper deck or gunwale, excluding the shaft spaces.

Article IX.—The capacity of spaces with complex form may be ascertained by dividing the spaces into two or more parts and adding their respective volumes, or by a similar method.

These regulations shall come into force on and after the date of enforcement of the Law No. 6 of the 6th year of Showa (1931).

The measurement of the capacity of ships due to application before the coming into force of these regulations may follow the Ship Measurement Regulations, Ordinance No. 16 of the Department of State for Communications, July, 1914.

MARACAIBO, VENEZUELA.
November 4, 1931.

Ben C. Matthews, Esquire,
American Vice Consul,
La Guaira, Venezuela.

Dear Matthews:

I am enclosing a translation of an article that appeared in one of the local papers lately regarding the activities of Japanese fishermen in the vicinity of La Guaira.

The small item gives me the impression that boats under the Japanese flag may be engaged in this occupation but this is so at variance with the policy of the government as practised in this section at least that I should appreciate your comments. The thought occurred to me too that large boats of this size and equipped with special apparatus would have little difficulty in making a very good survey of the depth of the waters along Venezuela's coast.

Very sincerely yours,
(signed) Gerald A. Mokma.
American Vice Consul.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to Legation's despatch No. 638 of November 12, 1931, concerning the arrival of twelve Japanese fishermen at La Guaira on August 28th last, coming from Panama, and to report that the Japanese in their launches proceeded up the Venezuelan coast as far as Higuerote and returned to La Guaira on November 18th.

While the Japanese are ostensibly fishing, it is certain that they are also taking soundings of all the

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.97)
-2-

the bays along the coast. It is quite possible that these soundings are taken in connection with their fishing venture. I have it, however, from a friend, Mr. Carlos Henny, who owns a fishing yacht in these waters, that the activities of the Japanese aroused his suspicions and made him believe that they were up to something besides fishing. Nevertheless, they have been fairly successful insofar as their fishing is concerned for the local fishermen at La Guaira have protested against their competition.

On Thursday, December 3rd, the Legation was informed that the Japanese had been arrested. I telephoned Vice Consul Matthews at La Guaira who having talked to the Chief of Police reported that no such arrest had taken place but that the local officials were keeping a careful eye on the Japanese and were very suspicious concerning their purpose in coming to the Venezuelan coast.

MEMORANDUM

January 11, 1932

Captain Ellis, Director of Naval Intelligence, came to see me on January 7 in regard to the reports from the Legation at Caracas concerning the arrival of Japanese fishermen at La Guaira and their activities along the coast. Captain Ellis gave me a memorandum to read, which he was going to leave with the Mexican Division, regarding another group of Japanese who were reported to have landed at Guaymas, Mexico, and to be mapping roads in the State of Sonora. He said that the Navy Department was considerably exercised over this and felt it most important to know just what these Japanese were doing, what appearance the men had, whether they were equipped with scientific instruments, etc. He asked that we instruct the Legation at Caracas to have the Consuls on the coast observe the activities of the group of fishermen and particularly to try to find out discreetly what the last port of call was of the steamer which brought them to Venezuela and whether it traversed the Canal. Captain Ellis also asked that we instruct the Legation at Bogota to have the Consuls look out for the appearance of these fishermen on the Colombia coast,

Subject: Japs in Venezuela.

Seven Japanese arrived in La Guira last October and took out fishing licenses. They lived on an island off this port and later ten more arrived. Their boat was a very fine one and not built in Venezuela. The leader talked good Spanish as did some of the others. He had plenty of money in American Express Co. checks of $50. each. He and some of his crew gave a champagne dinner to members of the customs at La Guira.

They have all gone but one man who is in Caracas and is trying to get a concession from the Government for a rice plantation and admittance for 100 men. The "Old Man" is opposed to this and a decree will soon be made prohibiting the immigration or admittance of both Japanese and Chinese. This Japanese has bought a Nash car, and is spending money lavishly.

I have been unable to find out where these Japanese came from or where they have gone. They did not visit Puerto Cabello, Maracaibo, Curacao, or Trinidad.

Mr. Henry Shumacher, who is a protoge of the "Old Man", has promised me details from the Venezuela secret service, but I have not received this as yet. Should I receive this I will for and it at once.

Respectfully submitted,

Murray Olsen,

Mr. Cordell Hull
Secretary of State
Washington, D.C.

Dear Sir:

The following may be of interest. I would appreciate your people keeping my letter entirely confidential.

I learn from a reliable source that the Japanese Embassy in Caracas has been filing coded telegrams to Tokio with the Servicio Radiotelegráfico of Venezuela amounting to about Bs 18,000 a month ($6,000) tariffs. Needless to say, with no Japanese living in this country and with no appreciable amount of Japanese imports, the answer is one of two things: Either the Embassy is trying to make itself important in the eyes of their home office in Tokio, or else they are retransmitting messages which are rive from elsewhere, $6000 a month is a lot of money for them to be spending when they are hard up for foreign exchange.

If the messages are being originated outside of Venezuela, it is my guess they may be coming over here from Panamá, since there are two planes a week and the air mail gets here the same day it is sent. Everyone knows Panama City and Colón is full of Japanese "barbers" and since all telegraph and radio services from Panama and the Canal Zone are more or less under U.S. Gov't control, maybe they mail their dispatches over here for sending from this point. I was also informed that the government of Venezuela has no-one capable of decoding these messages, so they have no idea of what is being transmitted.

Since the messages leave Venezuela, they are transmitted to Riverhead L.I. and come into the 66 Broad St. office of RCA Communications, New York, and it would be comparatively easy for your people to obtain copies of them and dedipher them. RCAC, I believe, retransmits them from the West Coast to Japan.

I was given this tip at least three weeks ago, so you should go four or five weeks back in RCAC's files.

For references as to the writer you may refer to US Passport #7960 issued by the Dept. of State, New York Agency. I am at present in this city as representative for one of the large USA radio companies.

Very truly yours,

R. H. Axtell

P/S In case you wish to communicate with me further, suggest you write c/o Box 222, Gatún, Canal Zone, as I may be leaving here in two or three weeks.

The Secretary of State transmits for the information and files of the Embassy, a copy of a letter from R. H. Axtell addressed to the Secretary of State.

The Embassy is instructed, if no objection is perceived, to acknowledge receipt of Mr. Axtell's letter, thanking him for the information contained therein.

Enclosure:
From Mr. Axtell,
August 24, 1939.

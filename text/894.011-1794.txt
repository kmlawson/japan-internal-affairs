SUBJECT: Review of the Japanese Constitution

DATE: February 28, 1949

I. Review of situation with respect to new constitution by Japanese Diet

In view of the attached cable from SCAP (C 68048 of February 24, 1949) it is recommended that the US member, FEC, be instructed to take the position should further questions be raised as to the review of the situation with respect to the constitution by the Japanese Diet that it is the view of the US Government that there has been substantial fulfillment by the Japanese Government of the FEC policy requirements on the review of the constitution. Should queries be raised as to reports in newspapers that the Diet is considering amending the constitution, the US member should point out that the constitution is under constant scrutiny by the Japanese people in all segments of official and private life, and by its very terms is susceptible to formal change at any time by a two-thirds vote of the Diet with the approval of the Electorate.

II. Review of Constitution by FEC

Attached are the comments submitted by the UK member on the provisions of the Constitution which deal with the position of aliens in Japan. In submitting these comments the UK member called to the attention of the Commission the fact that the problem of the review of the Japanese constitution was still before Committee III and that it was still the duty of the Commission to see if the constitution fulfilled the terms of the Potsdam Declaration and FEC policy decisions. He suggested that an attempt be made to point out the defects of the constitution but not to prescribe detailed remedies. He added that the discussions of Committee III should be sent to SCAP for his comments.

As to the substance of the UK comments, it is clear that the UK considers as identical "fundamental civil rights" of Japanese and "fundamental civil rights" of persons within Japanese jurisdiction as used in the FEC policy decision

on Basic Principles for a new Japanese Constitution. It is further claimed that one of these "fundamental civil rights" is the right to sue the state or a public entity. The Japanese term for "person" as used in Article 17 of the Japanese constitution which provides that: "Every person may sue for redress as provided by law from the State or a public entity in case he has suffered damage through illegal acts of any public official" could be translated as not necessarily excluding foreign nationals. In implementation of Article 17 the Japanese Government passed the State Redress Law which provided that foreigners who might suffer damage through the illegal act of any Japanese public official could only sue for redress against the Japanese Government or a Japanese public body if their own Government granted reciprocal rights to Japanese. It is the view of the British that if Article 17 is interpreted as not applicable to aliens it is contrary to the FEC policy decision; if it is interpreted as applying to aliens, the State Redress Law is unconstitutional. I think it is unnecessary to pursue this metaphysical discussion further at this time. It is to be noted, however, that "fundamental civil rights" may or may not be identical for nationals of a country and aliens residing within its jurisdiction and moreover that the right to sue the Government is not generally regarded as a "fundamental civil right". In the US a person is only permitted to sue the Government by permission of the legislature.

It is my view that the provision in the FEC policy decision that the constitution should be so framed as to provide for "the guarantee of fundamental civil rights to all Japanese and to all persons within Japanese jurisdiction" was not intended to grant to aliens in Japan privileges beyond those internationally recognized but rather to see that Japan lived up to those protected by customary international law.

The position might be taken by the British that Japan is an occupied country and therefore obliged to accord aliens certain preferential rights beyond those internationally recognized. STATEMENT OF U.S. MEMBER OF COMMITTEE 3 RELATIVE TO CHINESE COMMENTS ON THE CONSTITUTION

The Chinese member of Committee 3 desires an expression of the views of other members with reference to the Chinese comments on the Japanese Constitution contained in C3-326/13. The United States member submits the following comments:

1. Can the Emperor dissolve the Diet?

It is believed that the power of the Emperor to dissolve the House of Representatives, granted by Article 7 of the Constitution, is simply the power to proclaim formally a dissolution which has been ordered by the Cabinet, after the passage of a non-confidence resolution, or the rejection of a confidence resolution, as provided by Article 69. In such an event the Cabinet has two alternatives, either to resign en masse, or to dissolve the Diet within 10 days, and carry the issue to the people. The Emperor alone, without the advice and approval of the Cabinet, has no power to dissolve the House. His act of dissolution is simply one of those formalities which he must perform at the direction of the Cabinet. It is understood that this is the interpretation of the Constitution that prevails in SCAP Headquarters.

2. What is the intended meaning of the word kinrosha in Article 28?

The U.S. takes no position on this point, but would prefer to leave the issue to Japanese interpretation. The English word "workers", into which "kinrosha" has been translated, would imply manual laborers, rather than employees in general.

3. Should the Diet be vested with the power to repeal regulations enacted by local entities?

The Diet is already vested with supreme power, as the "highest organ of State power", and the "sole law-making organ of the State" (Art. 41). The only limitation on the power of legislation by the Diet, so far as concerns local public entities, is contained in Article 95, which provides that a special law which is applicable to only one

such

=== Page 6 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

such entity, cannot be enacted by the Diet without the consent of the majority of the voters in the entity concerned. This limitation would prevent the Diet from voting the repeal of specific regulations of a single local public entity without the consent of the voters thereof. If a single local public entity passes an unconstitutional municipal regulation, the proper remedy, as suggested by the Chinese member, is, of course, to test the constitutionality of the regulation in the courts, looking to a final decision by the Supreme Court if necessary. Specific repeal of the municipal regulation by an act of the Diet applicable only to the local entity would not be a normal remedy, and would, in fact, be practically prevented by Article 95. It is believed that this is a matter which will be worked out by the Japanese as they gradually gain experience in working under a constitution.

4. Should a time-limit and a specific voting procedure for the designation of a Premier in the House of Representatives be instituted?

Article 67 limits the power of the House of Councillors to hold up a decision by the House of Representatives on the designation of the Prime Minister to ten days. The only delay beyond that period would be that required for the deliberations of the House of Representatives itself, under its procedural rules. Present rules do not now require a two-thirds majority, but the designation may be voted by a simple majority (Art. 18, Chap. 2, Rules of the House of Representatives). It is believed that no difficulty would now exist to a sufficiently prompt designation.

5. Can the Emperor abdicate?

Under Article 2 the Imperial Throne shall be succeeded to in accordance with the Imperial House Law passed by the Diet. In other words, the Constitution leaves to the Diet the regulation of the succession to the Throne, which would include the succession in the event of abdication. The difficulty pointed out by the Chinese member appears to be a difficulty with the legislation rather than with the Constitution. It is believed that it is better to leave such details to legislation, where they can be quickly and easily modified to suit the occasion, than to embody them in the Constitution with its relative rigidity.

6. Is the bicameral system necessary?

The debate between the advocates of a bicameral and of a unicameral legislature have been age-long. Usually the outcome is a matter of historical accident. As pointed out by the Chinese member, a bicameral U.S. Congress was the natural consequence of the formation of a federal government. Nevertheless, 47 of the 48 State Governments in the United States have bicameral legislatures, although none of them are federal states.

Nebraska

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

Nebraska alone has a unicameral legislature. The United Kingdom has a bicameral Parliament, although the House of Lords is not based on the federal nature of the Kingdom.

The same reasons obtain for the institution of a bicameral legislature in Japan as exist in most of the American States. The upper house is a smaller body of older men elected for a longer and staggered term of office and hence is expected to be a more conservative and more consecutive body, and to exercise a restraining influence on the lower house. It is believed that the provisions of Article 54, which permit the Cabinet in times of national emergency to convoke the House of Councillors when the House of Representatives is dissolved, with power to take provisional measures which become null and void unless agreed to by the House of Representatives within 10 days after the opening of the next session of the Diet, is a sound measure which provides a useful popularly elected body to fill in any unavoidable interregnum caused by dissolution of the Diet.

Accordingly my Government would oppose suggesting to the Japanese people that they consider shifting from a bicameral to a unicameral legislature.

7. What is the meaning of "representative of all the people" in Article 43?

It is believed that this phrase means simply that elected members of the Diet represent all the people within their respective constituencies, and not any particular groups or classes of people.

8. Should the people be allowed to initiate Amendments?

Under the U.S. Constitution, Congress alone can propose amendments to the Constitution, by a two-thirds vote of both Houses. Article 96 of the Japanese Constitution is based on the same principle. Any system of permitting the proposal of amendments by popular petition would be both difficult to administer and prolific of trouble. It is always simple to get huge numbers of people to sign petitions, no matter what the object, and it is impossible to ascertain the genuineness of signatures. Fraud is easy and the value of a petition as a real index of public opinion is uncertain. If a substantial number of people desire an amendment to the Constitution the desire will inevitably be reflected in the Diet. It is believed that the present provision for amendment contributes to the stability of the Japanese Constitution.

# REVIEW OF THE JAPANESE CONSTITUTION

The Far Eastern Commission desires to bring to the attention of the Supreme Commander the following points which have arisen in the course of its review of the Japanese Constitution undertaken in accordance with the Commission's policy decision, Provisions for the Review of a New Japanese Constitution (FEC-031/41):

## 1. Position of Aliens Under the Constitution

The use of the word "kokumin" (which can only mean Japanese citizens) in the title of Chapter III of the Constitution, "Rights and Duties of the People", seems to make ambiguous the meaning of the other phrase "nanibito mo" (variously translated as "every person", "all", "all persons", "any person" and, with the negative, as "no person") in Articles 16, 17, 18, 20, 22, 31 - 35, and 38 - 40. The latter phrase ("nanibito mo") could be taken to cover all persons within Japanese jurisdiction, that is, both Japanese citizens and foreigners in Japan. It would appear, however, for the following reasons, that the Japanese Government considered that the word "kokumin" in the chapter heading must be taken to govern the language of the whole chapter and that "nanibito mo" is restricted to "every person" within the category of persons defined by the word "kokumin":

a. Article 16, in which "nanibito mo" is used, refers to some rights, such as that of petition for removal of public officials, which would appear to be appropriately given only to Japanese.

b. In drafting the State Redress Law (Law No. 125 of 1947) to implement Article 17, the Japanese Government apparently considered it constitutional to provide, in Article 6, that foreigners who might suffer damage through the illegal act of any Japanese public official, could only sue for redress against the Japanese Government or a Japanese public body if their own Government granted reciprocal rights to Japanese.

Thus it is not clear how the Constitution guarantees fundamental civil rights to all persons within Japanese jurisdiction (as distinguished from Japanese citizens), as provided in the Commission's policy decision, Basic Principles for a New Japanese Constitution (FEC-031/19).

2. Power of Supreme Court in Constitutional Matters

In Article 81 the Supreme Court is described as the "court of last resort with power to determine the constitutionality of any law, order, regulation or official act". Presumably it was the intention that the courts in general, and the Supreme Court in the last resort, should be the only bodies with power to decide the constitutionality of such laws, orders, et cetera. However, in the cases of the municipal regulations of Osaka, Fukui, and Mito, where questions were raised as to the constitutionality of such prohibitions as those against parades and meetings, there was apparently no effort made to obtain a decision through the courts. This would seem to provide some evidence that the Constitution may not be sufficiently clear on this point or that the Japanese may not fully understand the function of the courts in this respect.

3. Dissolution of the House of Representatives

Difficulties appear to have arisen in Japan in regard to the interpretation of Articles 7 and 69 of the Constitution which deal with the dissolution of the House of Representatives. Article 7 provides that "The Emperor, with the advice and approval of the Cabinet, shall perform the following acts in matters of state on behalf of the people: ...Dissolution of the House of Representatives". Article 69 provides that "If the House of Representatives passes a non-confidence resolution, or rejects a confidence resolution, the Cabinet shall resign en masse, unless the House of Representatives is dissolved within ten (10) days."

Article 7 does not in itself place any limitation on the power of the Cabinet to advise the Emperor to dissolve the Diet. On the other hand, it appears to have been held that Article 69 must come into operation before the Cabinet may advise a dissolution. Furthermore, Article 69, as it has been interpreted, does not appear to give the Prime Minister discretion to designate any vote as a vote of confidence, and instead appears to require a specific resolution of confidence or non-confidence. If, therefore, Article 69 is to govern in this matter, a situation of deadlock might well arise wherein the opposition, if able to command a majority in the House, could refuse to pass government legislation, but could, at the same time, refuse to pass a non-confidence resolution.

DEPARTMENT OF STATE

Gentlemen:

I have received a request from Mr. James R. Roach of the Department of Government, University of Texas, Austin 12, Texas for 50 copies of the new Japanese constitution. Mr. Roach intends to use this material in a course he is teaching on the Far East.

If it is possible to do so I would appreciate your complying with Mr. Roach's request.

Sincerely,

Lyndon B. Johnson

Department of State
Washington, D. C.

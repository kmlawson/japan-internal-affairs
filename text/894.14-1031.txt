JAPAN (IN MILATION NO SOCIAL CONDITIONS)

Subject: Philanthropic Organizations.
Military Relief (Governmental).

1. a. With the tremendous increase in the numbers of men in the service as a result of the war in China, the Military Relief Law, which was first promulgated in 1904 has been found to be inadequate to conditions prevailing at the present time. Under the peace time conditions for which the law and its various amendments were designed, it was the experience that the families of about one man out of ten were in need of aid upon the induction of the soldier into the service. However, with the mobilization of far larger numbers of men to meet the present situation, experience thus far has shown that the families of about three men in ten are in need of aid in one form or another. As a result of recently enacted revisions the provisions of this law, which applies to the families of non-commissioned officers and privates, are generally as described in the following paragraphs. In the interests of clarity and brevity, only the major provisions of the law are set forth below, all the details of each case and numbers of minor provisions having been omitted as having no bearing on its broader applications.

b. The law as amended, covers the subject of relief from a number of different aspects, and is divided roughly into the following broad sections.

(a) Relief for dependents of men called to the colors.

(b) Relief of dependents of men permanently disabled.

(c) Relief for dependents of men killed in battle or otherwise dead in the service.

(d) Relief of men wounded in battle or sick while in the service.

(e) Reestablishment of wounded men.

(f) Reestablishment of men discharged without wounds or illness.

2. Relief for dependents of men called to the colors.

a. Grants for living expenses.

A basic grant of $0.35 per person per day which

sum is manipulated in accordance with a scale which takes into account such factors as the conditions under which the dependents live and their number, the location, whether rural or urban, and a number of similar considerations. For example, a single dependent, living alone in one of the six large cities, would receive ¥0.60 a day or a total of ¥18.00 per month, while a family of four members under the same conditions would receive ¥47.00 per month or about ¥0.37 per person per day. On the other hand a single dependent in a rural community would receive ¥0.35 per day or ¥10.50 per month, and the family of four would receive ¥25.00 per month which amounts to about ¥0.20 per person. The above two conditions represent the extreme high and low payments in this class and apply only when the dependents have no other income. In cases where there is some other income, the difference between the income and the above amounts is paid when the income is the smaller of the two amounts.

b. Medical Aid.

Dependents who require hospital treatment receive aid varying between ¥1.00 and ¥2.50 per day. The same amounts are given to pregnant dependents who require treatment in maternity hospitals.

c. Occupational Aid.

For dependents who can improve their condition by establishing themselves in some business, sums varying from ¥50.00 to ¥150.00 are granted, while those who can be aided through vocational training receive from ¥0.10 to ¥0.30 per head per day if this training is received at home, and from ¥0.50 to ¥0.75 per head per day if the training be given at an institution.

d. Others.

Dependent families suffering from floods, fires, or other disasters, may receive up to ¥12.00 per family, while in the case of deceased dependents the sum of ¥12.00 is granted to the family concerned.

3. Relief of dependents of men permanently disabled.

Though not stated specifically in the law, it is known that dependent families of soldiers who have been permanently incapacitated will receive certain payments, the amount of which is not known, and the law also provided for payments to dependents of soldiers who are found to be in need of long periods of hospitalization before becoming fitted to support themselves and families upon discharge.

4. Relief of dependents of men killed in battle or died in the service.

a. The dependent family of a non-commissioned officer killed in battle or who was died from other causes while in the service, will receive an annual allowance of $324.00 while that of a private will receive $260.00. These amounts may be increased if the soldier has been in the army for more than twelve years, and all men killed or died in the service are credited with exactly twelve years service even though their actual service be less. In short, the above sums are the minimum. Increases over the above sums are given in cases of dependent families having more than three members so that, generally speaking, these allowances are usually from 25 to 45 per cent higher than the figures stated above.

b. Death Gratuity and Burial expenses.

In the case of a superior private, the death gratuity and burial expenses paid to the family are $180.00 and $37.50 respectively. These amounts are somewhat higher in the cases of non-commissioned officers.

c. Special Gratuity.

Under this heading are included such special payments as those which are attached to awards of the Order of the Golden Kite, special awards to air service personnel, and so forth.

d. Special Privileges.

In addition to the direct monetary benefits mentioned above, the dependents of deceased soldiers enjoy certain privileges such as reference in appointment as sellers of tobacco and stamps, exemption of their children from admission and tuition fees as government primary and middle schools, income tax exemptions equal to the amount of the benefits already mentioned, the right to a badge which gives them preferred treatment at festivals at the Yasukuni Shrine, Imperial visits, and other occasions.

5. Who may receive such benefits.

At the time of his entry into the service the soldier must register the names of his dependents in accordance with regulations describing the relationships which fall under that category. In general the term dependent includes parents, children, and in some cases brothers and sisters, but excludes uncles, aunts, cousins, in-laws, and a host of other relationships, even though some of these may have been entirely or partially dependent upon the soldier at the time of his induction into the service.

This organization maintains and operates asylums for mentally deranged patients, tuberculosis hospitals, watering places, and other facilities for the treatment of those non requiring care for long periods.

7. Reestablishment of wounded and sick.
In order to provide for the fitting of wounded and sick men for normal occupations after a cure has been effected, there has been established what is known as a Vocational Training Institute. This organization attends to matters connected with the vocational training, employment, and extending aid to men endeavoring to reestablish themselves. The work is done largely on a prefectural basis in order to place the men, as far as possible, in normal surroundings in their home communities.

8. Privileges for wounded soldiers.
In order to make easier the resumption of a normal life, various privileges have been made available. These include aid in educating their children, free treatment in army hospitals in case of the recurrence of bad effects

10. a. In considering the figures given in the above extracts of the law on this subject, it must be remembered that they refer only to those aids provided by the government. In addition, there are a large number of benevolent and philanthropical societies of all sorts which supplement the work of the government, with the result that need, when usually receive larger amounts of aid than indicated in the foregoing paragraphs. This aid takes many forms such as help in procuring work, donations of clothing, and assistance in all the many respects in which a needy family is likely to find itself in need.

b. While the amounts shown in the preceding paragraphs do not appear large, and in fact are not designed to be very much more than aids, from a practical standpoint they will usually suffice in providing food for the recipients, and thus definitely do away with the spectre of hunger. Other needs are filled by private charity as mentioned above, many of which charities are government aided, and the workings of the well recognized Japanese trait of cooperation within the family by which the group rallies to the support of the more needy members.

Subject: Philanthropic Organizations.
Private Relief.

1. The following tables show the activities of the more important of the private charitable institutions referred to in Par. 10 a, Report No. 9415. The amount of the sums contributed by the government are not known, and cannot be determined at the present time when the subject of governmental expenditures is largely secret. An inspection of the budget shows no definite item under which these expenditures might be made, though they are very likely carried under some of the very general and indefinite headings which go to make up that document.

4. Comments.

a. From the broad scope of the activities carried out by the foregoing charitable organizations it is evident that they not only reach into places which the more formalized government relief agencies do not extend, but also due to their national scope and the wide distribution of their branches, they touch the lives of needy persons very intimately.

b. The wide diversification of the activities of these organizations enables them to do a great deal toward supplementing the governmental gratuities of various forms, and their work both in aiding the needy soldier and his family and looking after their interests, is one which fills a real need and is very much appreciated by all the people. Under present conditions the work of these organizations has increased to national proportions, and the scope and variety of their efforts have resulted in a widespread popularity which takes a practical form in the readiness with which contributions are made to them.

Source: Official Publications, Conversations with Japanese.

Dear Mr. Secretary:
I have received the inclosed cable, dated 4 January 1946, from
the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, recuesting that an
education mission of no less than eighteen nor more than twenty-one
distinguished American educators be fomed for the purpose of advis-
ing his Staff, the Japanese Education Ministry, and others on techni-
cal matters relating to the Occupation Education Program. He desires
that the mission depart for Tokyo at the earliest possible date and re-
main there for an estimated period of thirty days.
As obably know, State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee
Paper 162/2, dated 8 January 1946, Subject: Reorientation of the
Japanese, now in process for final approval, concludes that the long-
range character of reorientation and reeducation of the Japanese is
an appropriate responsibility of the Department of State and that
the Department of State should immediately undertake the development
of such a program to be implemented by SCAP. It is, therefore, con-
sidered that the recruitment and final selection of this mission is
an appropriate responsibility of the Department of State.
It is rquested that you form this mission from among the educa-
tors named or such substitutes as you may feel desirable, and that
the collateral considerations of including Catholic and other sectarian
advisors who are not considered in the cable be given weight in the
formation of the mission. It would be highly desirable if the mission
could be selected and depart not later than 15 February 1946. When
selected, the War Department will provide all necessary transportation
and ex enses incident to the needs of the mission.
Sincerely yours,

SUBJECT: "Primer of Democracy".
This volume represents a real achievement, the result of eighteen
months of work on the part of Dr. Howard Bell of CIE, GHQ, SCAP,
Professor Odaka of Tokyo National University and twelve Japanese scholars
in the fields of education, journalism, economics and political science.
As the despatch from Tokyo indicates, the first 770, Oo0 copies are for
use in the tenth grade (the first year of the new Japanese "shinsei koto-
gakko" -- upper secondary school - grades 10, 11 and 12), but the demand
has been so great that it is to be made availabla to all teachers, adult
education groups, citizens public halls, and all groups interested in
sither political or social education.
It is of interest to note that the Japanese sdition contains many
cartoons which convey clearly some of the concepts described.
The book opens with Bell's excellent (in my opinion) essay "The
Essence of Democracy" in wlich he stresses that democracy is more than
a political system but is the spirit of respect for man.
The section "The Story of Democracy" opens with a discussion of the over-
use of the term and its meaning, and traces the evolution of democracy from
ancient times to England, the United States and France. The section "The
Structures of Democracy" examines anti-democratic structures to determine
in what way democracy differs. The British, American and Swiss democratic
structures are compared and evaluated. "The Suffrage" investigates all
aspocts of that subject from the methods of ele ction to the reasons for
voting. "Majority Rule", the following section, discusses possible snarls
in a democratic government, and fully explains the priceless bounty of
freedom of speech. In the section "Enlightened Electorate" propaganda
per se is defined and a method of scientific study of information is
suggested.
The section "Politics and People" has a first paragraph which begins:
"Politics is everybody's business" and strikes a keynote of the necessity
of applying the practical to the theoretical. Sections following are
concerned with democracy in social life, economic life and labor unions,
the latter particularly pertinent for Japan today.
The final chapter entitled "Democracy and Dictatorship places these
two enemies side by side and evaluates them. Particularly well done is the
analysis of the development of communism from socialism to the dictatorship
of the proletariat.

I believe that Mr. Benton should reply to this letter from
Dr. Stevens. My views are as follows:
1) Private educational institutions, especially those which
have had American sponsorship in the past, should survive. If this
FW 894.42A/4-1546
entails the shipment of American money from previous supporters,
I believe this should be permitted because of the ultimate gain to
our national policy.
I would see no general reason for forbidding Japanese
nationals themselves to give of their funds to help private
institutions.
2) I concur with Dr. Stevens that American printed matter of
an educational sort should go forward to Japan. One practical, but
small, step is to secure permission for the scientific and literary
materials held by the International Exchange Service of the
Smithsonian Institution to go forward to the Japanese recipients
without censorship. I would be pleased to take any action on this
when the policy agreement has been reached.
This arrangement implies that similar Japanese materials
would be forwarded to recipients in this country via Smithsonian
Exchange. The recipients are mostly libraries, professional asso-
ciations and editors of professional periodicals. I believe the
recipients are now eager to receive this material.
3) I believe it highly desirable that American materials should
get into translation with some professional control on the integrity
of translations and under agreements suitable to American publishers.
4) I believe a consensus has been reached already concerning
the desirability of procuring a resident educational commissioner

fall definitely within the field of implementation which
is General MacArthur's prerogative. If General MacArthur
feels that the time is ripe for these steps to be taken,
I believe that there would be universal agreement in
M
Washington that he should go ahead.
2. The shipment to Japan of information materials
894.42A/4-1546
of all kinds has been going on for some time. This pro-
blem, as are all others, is primarily affected by the
economic factor.
3. A system of exchanging scientific publications
between Japan and the United States was set up some
months ago and is functioning satisfactorily. Magazines
and publications are sent to OIC for forwarding to the
Civil Information and Education Section through War De-
partment channels for distribution in Japan and the same
channel is used in reverse. It is understood that Gen-
eral MacArthur feels that for the time being exchange
should be confined to this channel as it obviates the
necessity of censorship. It would be very cumbersome,
so long as censorship must be maintained, to do it in
any other way.
4. Tokyo's telegram War no. C 60203 says that a
civilian education adviser is not wanted.
5. I have talked with a number of people who have
talked with Dr. Stevens and I think the important thing
FW 894.42
to emphasize is the role we play in this thing. We are
not charged with implementation. We can and want to
convey to General MacArthur broad policy guidance on
matters falling within our province. However, specific
suggestions as to implementation come better from the
individual to General MacArthur than from us.

SOME MISSION RECOMMENDATIONS
The United States Education Mission, after one month's intensive study of the situation in
Japan, and with the purpose of our Mission in mind--of assisting in planning an educational
program that would help in the process of demilitarization and the reorientation of the
Japanese educational system-made several recommendations. Some of the most important
ones follow:
THE AILS AND CONTENT OF JAPANESE EDUCATION. The highly centralized educational syatem of
Japan was caught in a net of ultra nationalism and militarism. Decentralization of the
educational system is necessary in order that teachers may be free to develop professionally
under guidance without regimentation and that they in turn may do their part in the develop-
ment of free Japanese citizens. To effect this change knowledge must be acquired that is
broader than any available in a single prescribed textbook or manual and deeper than can be
tested by stereotyped examinations. A curriculum consists not merely of an accepted body
of knowledge but of the pupils' physical and mental activities; it takes into account their
differing backgrounds and abilities. It should, therefore, be set up thru cooperative
action involving teachers, counting on their experience and releasing their creative talents.
The official courses in morals, in geography, and in history had been eliminated from the
schools by military directive. This was done because these three courses were used for de-
veloping ultra nationalism, militarism, and Shintoism. Morals should be developed and prac-
ticed in the varied pro ram and activities of the democratic school. Books in the field of
geography and history wil? have to be rewritten to recogmize mythology for what it is and to
embody a more objective viewpoint in texthooks and reference materials. In the lower grades
more use should be made of the community and local resources. At the higher levels competent
scholarship and research should he encouraged. The program in health instruction and phy-
sical education is basic to the educational pro,ram as a whole. Medical examinations, in-
struction in nutrition and public health, the extension of the physical education and re-
creation program to the university level, and the replacement of the equipment as rapidly
as possible were recommended. Vocational education should be emphasized. A variety of
vocational experiences is needed under well-trained staff members.
LANGUAGE REFORM. The need for a single and efficient medium of written communication is
well recopnized, and the time for tak np this momentous step is perhaps more favorable now
than it will be for many years to come. Lanpuaze should be a highway and not a barrier.
Within Japan itself and across national borders this highway should be open for the trans-
mission of knowledre and ideas in the interests of a better world understanding. The
Mission recommended that some form of Romaji be adopted, replacing thousands of Chinese
characters with the Roman alphabet. It proposed that a language Commission made up of
Japanese scholars, educational leaders, and statesmen be formed promptly in order that a
comprehensive pro.ram may be announced within a reasonable period.
ORGANIZATION AND ADMINISTRATION OF EDUCAPION. The Mission accepted the principle that for
the purposes of democratic education, control of the schools should be widely dispersed
rather than highly centralized as at present in Japan. The lission regarded the obser-
vance of ceremonies in the reading of the Imperial Rescript and obeisance to the Imperial
Portrait in the schools as undesirable. The Ministry of Education, under the proposals of
the Education Mission, would have important duties to perform in providinp technical aid
and professional counsel to the schools but its direct control of local schools would be
greatly curtailed. In order to provide for greater participation by the people at local
and prefectural levels and remove the schools from adm nistrative control by representatives
of the Minister of Home Affairs at the local level, it is proposed to create educational
agencies elected by popular vote at both local and prefectural levels. Such agencies would
be granted considerable power in the improvement of schools, the licensing of teachers,
selection of textbooks--all of which are now centralized in the Ministry of Education.
The Mission proposed an upward revision of compulsory education in schools to he taxsupported,
coeducational, and tuitionfree-such education to cover 9 years of schooling, or until the
boy or girl reaches the are of 16. The Mission proposed that the first 6 years be spent in
primary school as at present and the last 3 years in a "lower secondary school." These

ety of community activities, adult education might be fostered. It was recommended that cen-
tral public libraries with branches be established in the larger cities and that appropriate
arrangements be made for library service thruout Japan. In addition, organizations of all
kinds, including community and professional societies, labor unions, and political groups,

Universities and other higher institutions of learninp should develop facilities for advanced
study on the part of teachers and administrators; they should promote research and exert ed-
ucational leadership.
HIGHER EDUCATION. Higher education now has the opportunity of setting a standard of free
thought, hold inquiry, and hopeful action for the people. To fulfill these purposes higher
education should become an opportunity for the many, not a privilege for the few. In order
to increase the opportunities for liberal education at higher levels, it would be desirable
to liheralize to a considerable extent the curricula of the preparatory schools leading to
the universities so that a general college training would become more widely available. In
addition to providing colleges, it is proposed that more universities be established according
to a considered plan. These institutions should be entirely free in all respects to pursue
their objectives in a manner which they themselves deem best. Establishment of economic and
academic freedom for faculties in institutions of hi her education is of primary importance.
For the student, the freedom which should be guaranteed is freedom of access, on the basis
of merit, to all levels of higher studies. Financial help should be given in order that
further education may be positively assured for talented men and women unable to study on
their owm resources. Freedom of access to higher education should be provided immediately
for all women now prepared for advanced study. The extension of libraries and research
facilities is recommended. Attention needs to be given to the improvement of professional
education in fields such as medicine, school administration, journalism, labor relations,
and public administration.
ADUILT EDUCATION. The schools are but one agency for adult education, but thru parent-teacher
activities, evening and extension classes for adults and the openinp of buildings for a vari-
ety of community activities, adult education might be fostered. It was recommended that cen-
tral public libraries with branches be established in the larger cities and that appropriate
arrangements be made for library service thruout Japan. In addition, organizations of all
kinds, including community and professional societies, labor unions, and political groups,
should be helped to use effectively the technics of forum and discussion.

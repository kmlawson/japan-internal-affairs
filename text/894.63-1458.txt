The Board of Economic Warfare and the War Department
on several occasions lately have asked officers of this
and other divisions recently returned from Japan for
information concerning the light metals industry of Japan
and Manchuria, stating that our intelligence services had
very little information on this subject.

In this connection, an officer of this Division submits
the following suggestion as to a likely source of information
concerning the light metals industry and production
of the Japanese Empire for possible submission to MID and
ONI -- this Division's liaison officer with BEW will bring
the matter directly to the attention of that agency:

It is noted from an article in the Washington Evening
Star of April 24, 1942, entitled "Spectacular Process Produces
Magnesium at 22 Cents a Pound, Hansgirg, Austrian,
Now in Detention Camp, Guides Plant by Telephone", that
Dr. (Fritz) Hansgirg, the Austrian genius of the great
Permanente magnesium plant in California, is now in California
in a detention camp.

Dr. Hansgirg from 1934 to 1937 was employed by
Noguchi, Japan's leading electro-chemical industrialist,
as consultant in electro-chemical processes, with particular
reference to light metal extraction, at the Noguchi nitrogen
fixation plant at Konan, on the northeastern coast of Korea,
reputed to be the second largest nitrogen fixation plant in
the world. In his capacity as Noguchi's consultant,
Dr. Hansgirg also had access to the great chemical fertilizer
and explosives plant owned by the Noguchi interests at
Nobeoka and elsewhere in Japan. From 1938 to 1941,
Dr. Hansgirg was employed as technical consultant by the
South Manchuria Railway Company for the Company's enormous
chemical plant at Kanchingtze (Kanseishi) across the harbor
from Dairen. By reason of this employment, which could not
have failed to bring him into close contact with the electro-
-chemical world of Japan, particularly with his own speciality

of light metal production, it is believed that Dr. Hansgirg has an intimate knowledge of the light metal situation in Japan.

The officer of this Division in question met Dr. Hansgirg frequently in 1934/36 in Korea, when Dr. Hansgirg used to come to the Consulate General at Keijo from Konan to make affidavits as to his chemical processes and other inventions for filing with the United States Patent Office. In the summer of 1935 the officer was the guest of Dr. Hansgirg at the latter's home in Konan, and was told by Dr. Hansgirg all about the production of chemicals, explosives, magnesium, et cetera, of the vast plant below Dr. Hansgirg's home -- visitors, of course, were barred from the plant itself. Dr. Hansgirg also volunteered the information that if an enemy wished to end the plant's usefulness, all he would have to do was to drop a bomb or two on the conduits leading out of the Lake Choshin dam to the first level of the plant's hydro-electric system. At that time these conduits were not in any way concealed, protected or camouflaged according to Dr. Hansgirg.

As Austria was an independent state and as German National Socialism was not yet a world issue when the officer knew Dr. Hansgirg, the latter at that time did not express any political views which might have indicated where his loyalties would lie following the absorption of his country by Nazi Germany. He gave the officer the impression, however, that he was a great admirer of the United States and its institutions.

The Star article in question states that Dr. Hansgirg bears "no ill-will (because of his internment). He holds himself ready to give all aid possible (in the operation of the Permanente plant)." In view of this attitude, it is not unlikely that Dr. Hansgirg may willingly provide our intelligence services with such information as he may possess concerning the light metals and electro-chemical industries of Japan.

RECEIVING STAR, WASHINGTON, D. C., FRIDAY, APRIL 24, 1942

# Federal Process Produces Magnesium At 22 Cents a Pound

## Hansgirg, Austrian, Now In Detention Camp, Guides Plant by Telephone

By a Staff Correspondent of The Star.

SAN FRANCISCO, April 24 — A few miles from here a mountain literally has been transformed into a factory of Titans.

Far away in a detention camp an Austrian engineer stays close by a telephone in case an emergency arises in the operations of the great war industry which is his brain child.

The plant is that of the Permanente Metals Corp., which is extracting magnesium, the magic, silvery metal, from ocean and mountain. Perhaps no other industrial operation in America is invested with such a supernatural atmosphere as this made-over mountain, on whose slopes deer still run wild, with its gigantic retorts and its blue-hot furnaces whose interiors look like the atmospheres of other stars. It promises to increase manyfold the country's supply of one of the most essential ingredients of war.

## Requires Sudden Chilling.

From the first the Permanente plant has been a place of mystery from which have come many fantastic rumors. It is engaged in the so-called Hansgirg process of magnesium extraction, which involves a chilling of a supposedly explosive mixture of magnesium, oxygen and carbon from 2,000 to 300 degrees C. In less than a thousandth of a second. The plant is equipped to produce about 24,000 tons a year by this method.

The starting point of the process of magnesium hydroxide, essentially the same thing as milk of magnesia, which is obtained from sea water — perhaps the richest of all sources of magnesium — and from a limestone-rich rock, dolomite, of which there are extensive deposits in both California and Nevada.

These are processed to form magnesia, or a combination of magnesium and oxygen, and shipped as raw materials to this plant at Permanente. The big job is to get out the oxygen and thus produce approximately 90 per cent pure magnesium — a metal about one-fifth the weight of steel, volume for volume, and, in certain alloys, just as strong as the finest steel.

## Mixed With Carbon.

In the Hansgirg process the magnesium oxide is mixed with carbon and the mixture ground to an extremely fine powder. At extreme temperatures carbon has a strong affinity for oxygen. The powder, made up in the form of tiny pellets, is dropped into an electric furnace, where, at about 2,000 degrees, everything is changed into gas.

The carbon in this gas unites with the oxygen to form the deadly carbon monoxide of automobile exhausts and the pure magnesium is set free in the form of a vapor. But the gas will go back to its original form again almost instantaneously as soon as the temperature goes down unless the two separated ingredients can be "frozen" faster than the normal chemical reaction can take place.

This "chilling" is the essential element in the Hansgirg process. The Austrian engineer invented a cone-shaped nozzle provided with 36,000 tiny holes through which large volumes of cold natural gas are forced at high velocity. This gas mixes instantaneously with the furnace vapors and reduces their temperature to 200 degrees centigrade. Thus the magnesium vapor is condensed to form solid particles of magnesium dust before there has been an opportunity for the back reaction to proceed to an appreciable extent. From this dust is obtained the solid metal.

## One of Cheapest Methods.

By this process, engineers of the Permanente Metals Corp. say, they are producing magnesium for a little over 22 cents a pound — the cheapest at which it can be produced except as a by-product to the extraction of something else, like chlorine, from sea water.

The whole process is extremely spectacular and, unless it is carefully controlled, likely to be very dangerous. The first plant set up by the inventor in Austria exploded with considerable loss of life. He superintended the erection of plants in South Wales, Japan and Korea before coming to the United States last year. Because of the danger, there was considerable reluctance to put the process in operation here — although Dr. Hansgirg himself was confident that he had overcome the difficulties.

The plant was set up at Permanente by the Kaiser Corp., which also operates the Todd-California and several other shipbuilding plants. It is probably the world's largest engineering contractor and has in the same location the largest cement-producing works in the world. Up to the outbreak of the war Dr. Hansgirg himself acted as consultant on the ground in the construction of the plant and the installation of the machinery. With the outbreak of the war being a German citizen, he was placed in a detention camp. He recognized his former associates say, the inevitability of this action and bore no ill-will. He holds himself ready to give all aid possible.

## Plant Being Expanded.

The plant still is far from full production, but several units are in operation, while others are under construction. There have been, the engineers claim, only some minor accidents to date, and these have been due to carelessness on the part of workmen. However, they assert, they have been greatly magnified by rumor.

Every step of the process now is sufficiently under control, they say, to make explosions extremely unlikely in the future. And, all assertions to the contrary, the plant actually is turning out magnesium. One can see great quantities of all already produced.

This plant actually was constructed on Government funds, but so great was the gamble considered that the Kaiser interests guaranteed their shipbuilding profits to make good the Federal expenditure.

They now are setting up units to extract magnesium by still another process — the so-called ferro-silicate reduction process — which is considerably more expensive but will increase the supply of the metal vitally needed for war purposes. Because of its combined strength and lightness it is essential for various parts of airplane engines and it is the chief ingredient in certain types of incendiary bombs.

After the war, the Permanente Corp. officials believe, it will find a wide usage in civilian industry.

Japanese Firms Operating in Indo-China (7) 15/43

Japanese firms are actively engaged in mining, in shipbuilding, sawmills, forestry, in processing agricultural products, particularly rice and rubber. They have not engaged in coal mining (Indo-China's largest mining industry) and have gone in for plantation agriculture only on a very small scale. They have not taken over French mines, factories or plantations - but in a number of cases have developed their own parallel enterprises while demanding the whole or a fixed proportion of the output of French enterprises.

## Mining

Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Dai Nippon Rinko, Nantaku, Taitaku, and Iwasaki all deal in phosphates.

Ataka, Mitsui and Mitsubishi deal in zinc.

Sanko, Mitsubishi, Taitaku and Cioei dealing in manganese and bauxite.

Kishumoto Shoten of Osaka has manganese mine near Vinh.

Ataka wanted to exploit an antimony mine near Vinh.

Showa Tsusho buying copper. Taiwantaku Shoku operating copper mine. Mitsubishi instructed to refine copper and lead.

Taiwan Development Co. also planned to have lead smelter and operate large iron mines along Red River.

Indo-Chinese Commercial and Industrial Company (Jap-owned) operates iron mine at the Thai Nguyen, Tuyen Quang and Thanh Hoa. Also operates a wolfram and one coal mine.

Kanematsu - exploring for mica in Hanoi area.

Tin, mercury and molybdenum mines also Japanese-operated but names of particular firms are not known.

## Shipbuilding and related enterprises

Timber Association includes Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Taitaku (Taiwan Takushoka), Ataka, Sanko and Nakamura.

Benthuy sawmill reported owned by Mitsui.

Hitachi desires to manufacture engines for wooden ships.

Indo-China Wooden Ship Association includes Mitsui.

Mitsubishi, Ataka, Sanko and Iwai (and possibly also includes Dainan Koshi and Nakamura).

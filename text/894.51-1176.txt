## II.
### Acquisition of Gold During 1939

Unfortunately there has been a complete statistical blackout in Japan in so far as gold production or acquisitions of old gold are concerned. The meager data which is available, however, leads one to believe that it may conservatively be estimated that Japanese acquisitions of newly mined and old gold during 1939 were probably somewhat in excess of gold acquisitions in 1938.

In a report under date of May 5, 1939 the Commercial Attaché at Tokyo expressed the opinion that the total output of gold during 1938 did not greatly exceed ¥200,000,000. At the official buying price of gold of ¥3.85 per gram, this would amount to 51,948 kilograms of gold, which at $35.00 per ounce would amount to approximately $58,500,000. This is very close to EA's estimate of $60,000,000 for new gold acquired by Japan during 1938.* The Commercial Attaché also remarked that he thought that gold production for 1939 would "run something like" ¥250,000,000, and that in view of the strenuous efforts being made by the Japanese Government to acquire all gold holdings in the country, perhaps ¥50,000,000 of exportable gold would be obtained from this source. It may be noted that the EA memorandum referred to above estimated that during the first few months of 1939 newly mined gold was being acquired at the rate of $6,000,000 per month, and that old gold was being acquired at the rate of $1,000,000 per month. If gold acquisitions continued throughout the year at these rates, $72,000,000 of newly mined gold would have been purchased by the end of the year, and $12,000,000 of old gold, or a total of $84,000,000.

It is of course possible that newly mined gold acquired by Japan last year amounted to less than $72,000,000. Thus an article in the *Far Eastern Survey* of November 22, 1939 states, with regard to the hope of the Japanese Government for an increased production of gold, that

"The absence of published data does not permit us to say definitely how far these hopes have been realized but there is evidence that production has not come up to expectation."

"No accurate estimates can be made, of course, as official figures on gold are now withheld, but from available data it may be calculated that Japan has left at the present time little if any gold outside of the ¥500 million reserve of the Bank of Japan. Similar views are expressed in a report entitled "Japan's Gold Situation", which was prepared by the American Consul at Kobe under date of September 30, 1939. While this report does not give any statistical estimate of Japanese gold production for 1939, it states that while the present policy of secrecy hides the true gold production situation, statements that get out in spite of the censors show that the output of newly mined gold is still disappointing. The report also states that gold production during the first six months of 1939 has been described as "rather poor" and at a "low level", and that it has also been said that "the situation in the field does not make it possible to realize the production goal" and that "results are unsatisfactory under the prevailing conditions". Factors accountable for this situation are said to be a shortage of labor, power and heavy machinery and adverse weather conditions.

It is also probable that mining costs are rising, and in this connection it may be noted that although the official buying price of gold has remained at ¥3.85 per gram since May 2, 1938, the Japanese Government announced on November 11, 1939 that a premium of ¥1 per gram will be paid on gold sold to the Government above fixed standard quantities, the premium being calculated on a half-yearly basis

This measure, however, was probably in part necessitated by the fact that the yen has been pegged at 23-7/16 cents since October 1939, whereas the official price of ¥3.85 per gram is the equivalent of $35.00 per ounce at the exchange rate of ¥1 = 29 cents.* The Japanese Government has found by experience in Chosen that an attempt to purchase gold at substantially below its world price, with consequent encouragement of smuggling, is not a good way to stimulate its acquisition..

In spite of the rather pessimistic (from the Japanese point of view) tone of the report from the Consulate at Kobe, and the articles cited in the Far Eastern Survey, I am not convinced that the estimates of the Commercial Attaché at Tokyo, which are close to those hitherto used by EA, should be revised downward materially. I have not seen anywhere a statement that gold production in 1939 was less than in 1938. The report from the Consulate at Kobe is written primarily from the point of view of demonstrating that gold production has not been sufficient to cover current gold exports, and has fallen below the production goal set by the Japanese. However, actual gold production in 1939 could fall far short of the hoped-for or planned increase

IV.

Estimate of Japanese Gold Reserves
at the end of 1939

The table on page 9 shows estimated Japanese gold reserves and the leading factors of change affecting them at the end of each month during 1939. Summary data for 1937 and 1938 are included for reference. Monthly data for 1937 and 1938 may be found in the table in Appendix II of EA's memorandum of May 15, 1939 on "Japanese Gold and Foreign Exchange Resources".

As shown in the table on page 9, estimated official gold stocks of Japan at the end of 1939 are $153,000,000 of which $37,700,000 are represented by gold in transit to the United States. Since at the present writing (January 23, 1940) there is no gold under earmark, the heavy gold arrivals of the first part of January 1940 have by now been converted into dollar balances, and in whole or in part expended, so that remaining Japanese gold stocks may be estimated at approximately $115,000,000 plus such gold as may have been acquired since the beginning of the year. In other words, Japanese gold stocks at the present time appear to be represented almost entirely by the Bank of Japan's gold reserve, which in turn appears to have been reduced below ¥501,000,000 if the gold in that reserve continues to be valued at the new legal gold value of the yen of 290 milligrams per yen established in August of 1937.

It is possible, however, that perhaps extra-legally the Japanese Government is valuing the Bank of Japan's gold reserve at the current exchange value of the yen or 23-7/16 cents per yen, United States currency, instead of 32.63 cents per yen, which is the gold parity of the yen and the dollar.* It may be noted that ¥501,000,000 at 23-7/16 cents per yen amounts to approximately $117,000,000 as against the estimate of remaining Bank of Japan gold reserves of $115,000,000. VI.

Comparison of Probable Deficit on Current International Account with Gold Exports

The estimated net debit on current international account with Non-Yen-Bloc countries is substantially below gold exports during 1939. Even taking the larger estimate of ¥400,000,000, the net debit at the average dollar value of the yen during 1939 would amount to only $104,000,000. As shown in the table on page 9, however, gold exports during 1939 amounted to $171,000,000, and in addition there was a loss of $12,000,000 of gold arising from a decrease in this amount of gold in transit at the end of 1939 as compared to gold in transit or under earmark at the end of 1938.

It is, of course, possible that the net deficit on Japanese current account with Non-Yen-Bloc countries may be greatly in excess of ¥400,000,000. I believe that to a considerable degree, however, the discrepancy may be accounted for by such factors as further reduction in foreign commercial credits to Japanese, and an increased tendency on the part of exporters to demand cash with order on exports to Japan.

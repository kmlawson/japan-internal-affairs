PART I
INTRODUCTORY
Jepan plunged into the scramble for whale oil in
the Antaretic in 1934-3:. 1: that year. Nippon ogei
Kabushiki Kaisha purchased from Norway and sent out the
factory ship Antarctic, renamed the Tonan Maru, and
three chasers. The catch of this first season resulted
in 2, 006 tons of oil, or .5% of the world's production.
By the 1937-38 season her p. oduction had reached 65,000
tons of oil, or ll.9% of the world total. The present
season will see a further increase in oil, plus an
effort to develop the output of other products from
whales.
While this report will deel with the newly developed
factory ship whaling industry of Japan, it is believed
that a few words of a general historical nature, plus
a brief mention of the coastal and northern waters
whaling industry may be appropriate introductory
material.
It is probable that whales were caught in the
very early days of Japanese history, but the first
definite mention of it was made during the Kamakura
Period, some 750 years ago. About 350 years ago the
spears and harpoons used in catching whales had been
improved sufficiently to bring whaling to an elementary
commercial stage. Toward the end of the seventeenth
century a method of catching whales by the use of nets
had been evolved. The industry now advanced rapidly
due to the new method and to encouragement by the
Tokugawa Shogunate and the individual clans.

Thirty-nine whaling grounds were developed, many of which
could boast a yearly catch of over lo0 whales.
During the second quarter of the nineteenth century
an influx of Dutcn, end especially American whelers into
nearby waters greatly reduced the number or whales
caught by the Japanese and the industry suffered severely.
About 1890 the explosive harpoon used by the American
whalers was introduced, but the Norwegian system of whal-
ing replaced it in 1899. The success of the Russians who
in 1895 began to use this method in the Japan Sea and who
brought a good deal of their production to Nagasaki
brought on the change.
The Russo-Japanese War ended the Russian venture
in this area, and soon the Japanese fleet increased
from ten to thirty ships, at which point expansion was
stopped by the Whaling Ordinance of 1909. In 1928,
ships operating outside an area roughly within 100
miles of the main and colonial shores (200 to 520 30'
N.L., and 1180 to 1590 E.L.) were exempted. The limi-
tation of thirty ships was reduced to twenty-five in
1934.
While the greater efficiency of the factory ship
method plus a growing scarcity of whales in nearby
waters will mean a decreasing importance in coastal
whaling, the catch in which the sperm whale predominates
is still of considerable value as indicated by the

Japanese fisheries products imports hes been found to

History
The Fisheries Training School was established in
Tokyo in 1889 by the Dai-Nippon Suisan-kai (Fisheries
Society of Japan) for the purpose of training men to
assume responsible positions in fisheries companies.
In 1897 the Fisheries Training Sohool was taken over
by the Imperial Government. At the same time, the
school was assigned the additional function of investi-
gation and experimentation both f or the industry as a
whole and for private individuals and companies.
There were thus three departments, educational,
experimental, and marine investigation.
This organiza-
tion remained unchanged until 1929.
In l929 the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, which
directs the Fisheries Institute, split up the work of
the Fisheries Institute. To the Fisheries Institute
was left the educational work, and to the Fisheries
Experimental Station, which was created at that time,
was given the experimental and investigational work
formerly performed by the Fisheries Institute. For the
seope of this work, reference is made to Section VI of
this report.

Amalgamation of Small Fishing Firms Projected
A number of small fishing firms are giving serious onsideration
to a plan for the establishment of a new company to be nowm as the
"Japan Deep-Sea Bonito and Tunny Fishing Company", according to informa-
tion received from press reports and other sources. The Cabinet
Planning Board and the Merine Products Bureau of the Ministry of
Agriculture ard Forestry are said to be conferring with the Japan
Bonito and Tunny Fishing Association with reference to the new project,
which would consist of a merger of the 600 association members into
one large firm.
The proposal contemplates the creation of a Yen 35,000,000
concern which will operate in Japanese waters and the South Seas. The
total tonnage of the Association will reportedly be divided into two
fleets, one of which will remain permanently in southern waters. The
main office of the new firm is to be set up in Tokyo, and ten fishing
bases are to be established in Japan Proper and the Japanese Mandated
Islands. Another base is supposed to be created at Batavia, which
will control subsidiary locatiens at Menade, Sourabaya and other points
in the Celebes, New Guinea and Sumatra.
Further information on this subject was seoured from an
official of the Japan Bonite and Tunny Fishing Association, who stated
that the Direoters of the Association held a meeting on December 7th
at which the merger was discussed. but that no final agreement was
reached, and the subjeot was accerdingly tabled until December 23rd
when a second meeting on the question will be held. The movement
toward amalgamation has undoubtedly been strengthened by the difficulties
which the smaller firme have enoountered in securing gasoline, heavy
oil, nets and other essentials. It is believed that a single large
company would experience less trouble in ebtaiming such materiale.

1940 salmon catch was below average. This condition is said to have
applied particularly to red salmon, which is normally the mnst important
type used in the export trade. The effect of the reduced catch,
however, is largely offset by the fect that exports have fallen off
considerably this year, owing to the European war. According to a re-
port on the financial condition of the Taiheiyo Fishery Company, canned
red salmon. whioh was formerly shipped to Gret Britain, is now being

accumulate for some time in hopes of eventually disposing of them in

Recently there has been some mention in the press
of a shortage of fish in Japan, fish being one of the
main items in the Japanese diet. The shortage was attri-
buted to (1) the drafting of Japanese fishermen (who have
seafaring experience) for naval and related war duties,
and (2) shortage of gasoline. It is thought that the
894.628/223
second item is not important as it relates to fishing
because fishermen can use sail vessels.
The Japanese will not be able to do their usual fish-
ing off the Alaskan coast this year and it is probable
that their whaling activities will be stopped or greatly
curtailed.
There occurs to the writer of this memorandum the
question whether it might be worthwhile for the Navy,
in collaboration with the Bureau of Fisheries, to make a
survey of Japanese fishing resources and practices with
the thought in mind of giving special attention by means
of submarines to Japanese fishing fleets. In other words,
would it be worthwhile to equip and train a submarine
"task force" for the purpose of further curtailing Japanese
fishing activities? If so, some of the Alaskan fishermen
with knowledge of Japanese fishing habits and a healthy
personal animus toward the Japanese might be of use.
PS/SGB
The writer has done some trout fishing in the Hsingan
mountains of North Manchuria and encountered there a
Japanese practice of dynamiting streams resulting in the
wholesale destruction of fish of all sizes and kinds.
It seems to the writer quite possible that the detonation
of mines or torpedoes in Japanese fishing grounds in the
open sea would not be effective in any practicable mea-
sure in killing or driving away fish. The writer has no

remote possibility of interest.

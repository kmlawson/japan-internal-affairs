Tokyo's despatch of April 15, 1942,
gives an outline of the existing foodstuffs
situation in Japan.
Rice. Because of poor harvests in Japan
and the colonies the avaflable supplies of
rice for the Japanese Empire this year will
be 96.6 million koku (one koku equals 5.12
bushels) as compared with a minimum national
demand of l00 million koku. Although propa-
ganda for enemy governments describes the
food situation as excellent, the people of
Japan are advised by their government that
they must be prepared for further cuts in
their rations. It is anticipated that the
1942 crop will be poor because of the great
shortage of fertilizers.
Canned goods. Owing to the cessation
of exports there is a good stock pile of
canned goods in the country, which will be
placed on the local markets.
Fisheries. There is a shortage of fish
owing to curtailed fishing activities resulting
from a shortage of fuel oil for deep-sea
fishing vessels, from the requisitioning of
many sampans for military landing operations,
and from the refusal of fishermen to sell
their catches to city markets because of the
low official prices fixed there. At one time
only 100 to 200 tons of fresh fish were de-
livered to Tokyo daily, against a normal

GENERAL RESUME OF THE FOODSTUFFS
SITUATION IN JAPAN PROPER
DECEMBER 1941 - APRIL 1942
It is scarcely necessary to state that any
effort to comment on the present foodstuffs situation in
Japan is bound to be rather speculative under existing
circumstances. Personal observation and press reports,
which cannot be verified and are often intentionally mis-
leading, are practically the only sources of information.
Nevertheless some rough conclusions can be drawn from even
this scanty material.
The four months which nave passed since the out-
break of war with the United States and Great Britain have
been insufficient to bring out very clearly the effect of
the hostilities on the nation's foodstuffs resources. As
yet the Japaneee have anparently not been able to secure
sufficient shi ping facilities to transport any substantial
amount of food from Southeastern Asia. Conseguently
there has been a considerable tightening and extending of
the ration system. As has been shown in previous reports,
Japan was suffering from a  eneral shorta e of foodstuffs
before the beginning of the war. For several years the
rice crop has been sub-normal, and other crops have also
suffered from the lack of fertilizer and labor. The
fisheries inaustry has been hampered by the shortage of oil.
On the one hand, domestic supplies have been
augmented by the fact that since the summer of l94l exports
of canned goods have been almost entirely discontinued.
This has resulted in a fairly substantial accumulation of
stocks, most of which have been withheld from the market
by the authorities against an emergency. This advantage
has been slightly offset by the cessation of imports of
provisions

purchased from the United States, Canada, Australia and
the Argentine by Japan, China and Manchuria has not been
very large, it has been a definite factor in the attempts
to balance the cereal requirements in this area.
The rice problem is even more important. As
has been frequently pointed out in the local press, rice
production in Southeastern Asia, particularly in
Tnailand and Frencn Indo-China, is considerably in excess
of local requirements. According to an article in the
Diamond magazine, Ja an expects in the near future to
secure about 10,000,000 koku (one koku equals 5.12
bushels) from these areas for her own use, and to send an
additional 10,000,000 koku to China, and 3,000,000 koku
to Malaya. Tne success or failure of this scheme will
depend largely on the efficiency of our submarine warfare.
The same situation exists with re ard to sugar in a lesser
degree.
As has already been pointed out, the shorta e of
oil is affecting Japan's fishing industry. In this field
as vell, suomarine warfare may play an important part in
restricting the activities of offshore operations.
Whale meat, which the authorities have been promoting as
a substitute for beef, should disappear from the market
when existing supplies are exhausted, since it is assumed
that any whaling operations by the Japanese in Antarctic
waters are now out of the question.
In summary, then, it can be stated that Japan
is now suffering from a moderate food shortage, although
it should not be assumed from the statements in this

THE 1941 RICE CROP
According to a report released by the Ministry
of Agriculture and Forestry on March 13th, the actual
1941 rice harvest for Japan Proper totalled 55,080,000
koku (one koku equals 5.12 bushels), a sli ght decline
from the previous estimate of 55,462,000 koku. This un-
usually poor crop was officially represented as being the
result of low temperatures and excessive rainfall during
the growing period. The actual harvest for Chosen amounted
to 24,885,000 koku, and that of Taiwan (first and second
harvests) aggregated 8,410,000 koku. In acdition to this,
a carry-over into the new rice year (commenced November 1,
1941) of 8,310,000 koku was reported. Thus the total
available suoplies of rice for the Jacanese Empire for the
present rice year, exclusive of possible arrivals from
Southeastern Asia, amount to 96,685,000 koku, as compared
with a minimum demand of rough1 100,000,000 koku.
Articles appearing in tne Japan Times and
Advertiser emphasize the increased carry-over, which is
due exclusively to heavy arrivals from French Indo-China,
and stress the fact that further imports are expected to
augment existing supplies. The eneral situatiôn is re-
ported to be excellent.
An entirely different picture, however, was
painted by the Minister of Agriculture and Forestry in
his address to the Conference of Prefectural Governors
on March 7th. A report of this address appearing in the
Asahi

THE FISHERIES SITUATION
The fisheries industry in Japan was confronted

allotment of oil to the industry for the January-March
period, and further allotments are reportedly contemplated.
It is believed, howe ver, that this allowance has not been
very large.
The requirements of the Military have also un-
uestionably affected the number of vessels available.
This applies to both offshore fishing boats and to the
small motor-driven "sampans" used for coastal fishing.
Concrete evidence exists to show that te latter are ex-
tensively used for landing operations by the armed forces.

Subject: Article on Price Control and Commodity Shortages in Japan.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington, D. C.

Sir:

I have the honor to enclose a copy of an article entitled "Japanese People Live Life of Rationing Cards", published October 8, 1943 in the China Information Committee Daily Bulletin, issued by the Ministry of Information. The author is stated to be Seisaku Shieni, a Japanese employed by General Wang Peng-sheng, General Chiang Kai-shek's adviser on Japanese affairs. The article, assumably based on information from Japanese publications received at Chungking and Chinese Intelligence reports, is believed to contain a credible appraisal of the rationing system in Japan.

Summary: The commodity price situation in Japan is not as serious as reported abroad, for the Japanese Government several years ago adopted a strict system of price control and rationing of consumer goods. The fixed prices are adjusted from time to time but adjustments in private incomes, especially among the salaried classes, have, since the beginning of the Pacific war, lagged far behind the rise in prices. Rationing in Japan was introduced in May 1938 and has since been extended to include all daily necessities. The real trouble in Japan's economic position is the acute shortage of materials and consumer goods, lack of materials and manpower having brought about a great gap between supply and demand. The situation is further deteriorating with the increase of war strain. The Japanese Government has issued warnings of a food shortage this winter. The conversion of all "non-essential" factories into war plants will further curtail production of consumer goods. The situation will be further worsened if the Allies can begin a large scale bombing offensive against Japanese industrial centers and dislocate the nation's production and transportation.

Respectfully yours,

C. E. Gauss

China Information Committee
Daily Bulletin -- No. 773
October 8, 1943

JAPANESE PEOPLE LIVE LIFE OF RATIONING CARDS

By Seisaku Shioni

Although I have been in China five years, I have been able to keep in touch with conditions in Japan throughout these war years, thanks to our underground channels of information and the Chinese secret service. The Japanese people live a life of rationing cards under the strain of war. The control is so strict that it is commonly whispered around that life itself is rationed in Japan.

The commodity price situation in Japan is not as serious as it has been reported abroad, for the Japanese government has long adopted a strict system of price control through the use of price ceilings. The ceilings are readjusted from time to time, so that the rise in commodity prices is gradual, although the rate has been much faster since the outbreak of the Pacific War. However, readjustments in private incomes, especially among the salaried class, have ever since the beginning lagged far behind the rise of prices. Thus the salaried class suffer the most.

The real trouble in Japan's economic setup is the acute shortage in materials and consumer goods. The lack of materials and manpower has brought about a great gap between supply and demand. This gap will be further aggravated by the Japanese government's decision to suspend "non-essential" manufacturing in favor of war production.

The Japanese solution for the shortage of goods is rationing. The system was first introduced in May, 1938, when the purchase of mineral oil in Japan had to be done by rationing cards. The first consumer's goods put under the rationing system were sugar and matches. They were first rationed in June, 1940. Since then, the list has been constantly enlarged so that it now comprises all daily necessities of Japanese life.

The goods are distributed through "neighborhood associations" every month or ten days. Each association has about ten families and is headed by a president, appointed by the police. The goods are bought with cash, on the basis of rationing cards. Goods can also be bought in the black markets. The black market prices are sky-high although the available goods sold in such markets are rather limited.

Each

Each adult in Japan gets only seven sheng (each sheng is one hundredth of a picul and is about one catty in weight) of rice a month. As rice is the staple food in Japan, the food situation of the Japanese people is rather difficult. This explains the cry for more food production in Japan and occupied China. As each adult needs about 20 sheng (workmen need more) of rice a month, the amount allowed by the Japanese government is far from sufficient. The deficiency is usually made up by potatoes, millet, and other cereals. This also explains why the Japanese government recently ordered the increased production and use of potatoes for a rice-consuming people.

For those who have to eat away from home, meal tickets are issued by the authorities. Only people with meal tickets are allowed to eat in restaurants. It is now a common practice for Japanese people to carry their own rice when they go visiting. Even farmers do not have enough rice for their own consumption, for all the crop is bought by the government at arbitrary prices. Last year's poor harvest in Korea (10,000,000 piculs less than average) and the shortage of shipping for the transportation of rice produced in Japanese occupied areas in southeastern Asia further aggravate this year's food shortage in Japan.

Sugar, salt, sauce, and other seasoning materials are rationed. Each person can only get half a catty of sugar, half a catty of bean curd, half a catty of biscuits or cakes, and one pint of wine each month. The amount may be reduced as Japan's all-out munition production program will further curtail the manufacture of cakes and the shortage in rice production will give the nation still less wine.

For families with more than ten persons, ten "special packages" of matches (each with 230 matches) are allowed each month. Six packages are issued to families over six. For families with five persons, ten "ordinary packages" (70 matches each) are allowed each month. Two packages each month are issued to families with less than four persons. Thus, each person averages one package of matches a month, not sufficient for smoking purposes.

Each person is allowed a cake of toilet soap and a cake of laundry soap each month. They are hardly enough for the family washing.

Besides, there are other goods which are rationed only when a stock is available. Among them are beer, soda water, paper, and eggs. Each person gets one or two eggs a month. And it takes one or two months before the second stock is accumulated and rationed. Those who suffer most under the shortage of eggs are sick people and children. Besides, the shortage of eggs can hardly be made up by keeping chickens as there is not enough chicken feed for the purpose. What was formerly chicken feed is now earmarked as food for the people. The Japanese government is now encouraging the raising of rabbits to supplement the nation's meat supply.

Even

-3-

Even the supply of fish, the second most important staple food for the Japanese people, is reduced under the strain of war. A large number of fishing boats have been commandeered to serve as patrol boats. Thus, the capacity of the Japanese fishing fleet has been greatly reduced. The drafting of young men from coastal villages has also created a manpower shortage in the fishing industry.

The production of vegetables is reduced as most of the larger vegetable gardens are devoted to the production of rice. Japanese gardeners are ordered to grow vegetables instead of flowers. House wives have to go to the market every morning at three o'clock and stand in long queues for the day's shopping.

The rationing of clothes started in February, 1942. It includes nearly every item of garment. Each year, a person residing in any of the big cities is allowed 100 points while one living in a village is allowed 80 points. But since the points required for each garment in 1943 have been increased about 25 per cent, this year's cards get about one fourth less than those of 1942. For 1943, each suit requires 63 points, an overcoat 50 points, a "people's uniform" 40 points, a raincoat 38 points, a woman's dress 35 points, a shirt 15 points, a suit of summer underwear 16 points, socks three points, a towel three points, and a handkerchief two points.

Most of the clothes are made of synthetic cotton, as raw cotton is mainly diverted to war production. Clothes made of synthetic cotton become shreds after two or three washings.

Besides encouraging the use of geta, Japanese wooden slippers, Japan's wartime footwear is mainly made of pig skin or fish skin instead of leather. These pig or fish skin shoes lose their shape or crack after getting wet. And Japan is a rainy country.

The shortage of commodity goods in Japan is already acute. Yet the situation is further deteriorating with the increase of war strain on the nation. The Japanese government has issued warnings of a food shortage this winter. The conversion of all "non-essential" factories into war plants will further curtail the production of consumer goods. The situation will be further worsened if the Allies can begin a large scale bombing offensive against Japanese industrial cities and dislocate the nation's production and transportation.

(Compaia)

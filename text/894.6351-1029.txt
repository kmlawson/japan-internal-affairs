16 June 38
JOHN A. SAVAGE
FROM ORE

The Nissan of Tokyo, headed by Mr. Aikawa, in which Mitsubishi has a large interest, has been given the task by the Japanese Government of developing the steel industry in Manchukuo; that is, at the Showa Works in Manchukuo. The present capacity of this plant is roughly 800,000 tons of steel ingots, and during the past winter a contract totaling 33,000,000 Yen was given to Otto Wolff of Cologne, Germany, largely a barter of new steel plant and rolling mill equipment to be made in Germany against soya beans to be imported into Germany. This is supposed to increase the capacity to 1,800,000 tons but the Germans are finding it very difficult to furnish as much equipment for the above sum as the Japanese wish. At this moment, engineers from H. A. Brassert &amp; Co. of Chicago are in Japan and Manchukuo drawing plans which would increase the capacity of these plants to 3,500,000 tons.

The great problem is iron ore. Foster Bain of the Columbia School of Mines, New York City, who has done a lot of work in Asia, has reported a new iron ore property in Manchukuo in an area recently freed from bandit control. Mr. Bain advises that this ore is of a better grade and much more plentiful than he had expected and it could be made the basis of considerable increase in pig iron production. It is this ore that it is planned to use in the new Showa capacity.

Mr. Hirose, a metallurgical engineer of Mitsubishi Company, was in Duluth last week to see the ore docks and the Hibbing mines. He said that the Japanese are now developing a mountain of iron ore, known as the Mozan Mine near Seishin in Korea, estimated to contain 1,800,000,000 tons of ore analyzing 40% iron and 40% silica. They are planning concentration and the building of a large ocean port iron ore dock, with an output of 5,000,000 tons concentrates per year.

The Japanese, until recently, were receiving hematite ore running 63% iron from two places in British Malaya, total annual shipment approximately 1,800,000 tons to 2,000,000 tons. On account of refusal to work by Chinese coolies in these two mines, production is greatly reduced. For several years, Broken Hill in Australia has been shipping from Whyalla to Japan approximately 300,000 tons of manganiferous iron ore per year, 50% natural iron, 16% natural manganese, a good deal of which was used as open hearth lump. Due to a recent determination by the Federal Government in Australia, all export of iron ore has been stopped, including this extremely valuable manganiferous ore. This decision was based upon a study of iron ore reserves and an opinion by the State Geologist in Australia that Australia had no ore to spare for other countries. This prohibition has also stopped the development of the London owned Yampi Sound deposits, from which Japan expected to receive 1,000,000 tons of 65.00 plus iron ore, much of it 67.00 plus open hearth dump, per year beginning in 1946.

The Han Yang iron mine, 60% miles up the Yangtze River, is of course not now shipping its regular 1,000,000 tons of over 60% iron per year to Japan.

An iron mine in the Philippines, owned by Americans in
Waalla, is shipping about 600,000 tons per year of 63% natural iron
at Japan. This deposit is believed by the Japanese to contain not
over 5,000,000 tons. Recently, Japanese interests tried to deal
with the Philippine Government regarding the Surigao lateritic iron
are deposit, a very large one, but were turned down.

Using Japanese ships, the Japanese are delivering these
iron ores at Japan for about 5¢ per unit based on one yen equaling
30¢. The Whyalla manganiferous ore costs the Japanese maybe 35.00 -
35.50 delivered Japan, basis 66.00 combined natural iron and manganese.
The opportunity exists for the British and the United States to do
some effective economic and military work in curtailing the above
supplies, or in doubling their cost to Japan, at which doubled prices
they would be cheap, compared to ore prices in Europe and America.
There is no real competition in selling ore to anyone in Japan.

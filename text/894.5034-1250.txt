TELEGRAM RECEIVED APR 13 1941 DEPARTMENT OF STATE
Dated March 10, 1941
Rec'd 9:13 a.m.

Secretary of State,
Washington.

383, March 10, 5 p.m. (SECTION ONE)

In view of recently enacted legislation extending rigid government control over all industrial production, American owned corporations engaged in manufacturing are extremely apprehensive over their investments in Japan. Such legislation prescribes a license system for the manufacture of designated important machines and machine tools and for raw materials. It provides for expropriation of lands, materials, labor, plants and equipment, for compulsory amalgamation of factories and changes in types of machinery produced. It further provides for inspection and supervision of business offices, factories, warehouses and accounting books and for the fixing of prices. Moreover, it is anticipated that under the broad provisions of the revised general mobilization and foreign exchange control laws the enforcing regulations will stipulate a Japanese majority of share holdings and membership of boards of directors together with complete Japanese control of management.

383, March 10, 5 p.m. (SECTION TWO)

This matter is of extreme importance at the moment when extensive rationalization of industrial production and more rigid foreign exchange control are contemplated under the new legislation. Many Americans have recently approached me and members of my staff for advice and assistance. They feel that the situation has gone far beyond the state of "embarrassment" from having their funds tied up and that they are now faced with the strong probability of losing the entire or better part of these investments and cash holdings from being squeezed out of business and the likelihood of heavy exchange depreciation.

The same situation has existed to a lesser extent for the past three years and representations have been made to the Foreign Office on specific cases in compliance with the Department's instructions No. 1345, October 4, 1937 and No. 1359, October 29, 1937, but without satisfactory results.

March 13, 1941

J. Weis:

The Japanese provisions for licensing manufacture and for compulsory amalgamation of factories and changes in types of machinery produced may go further than current British practice and be on more permanent lines, but the British are just now moving to centralize manufacturing in certain factories while closing other producers in the same line of goods, and in other ways organizing industries as efficient units rather than as a collection of competing units. In other words, the Japanese regulations are not unprecedented in type and so far are merely unimplemented legislation.

The suggestion that the United States should liquidate foreign holdings in this country and use them to pay Americans whose holdings in the other country are liquidated is also not novel. It would be a good prelude to hermetic isolation. Just how it would work with any given country would of course depend upon the balance of investment and other claims between the two countries. Equity would appear to require the cashing of all the available foreign assets and a careful allocation among American claimants on some defensible basis other than first-come-first-served. Whether this would ever be done by peacetime legislation in the United States may be a question.

Enclosure no. 1 to despatch no. 2891, dated February 21, 1944, from the American Legation, Stockholm, Sweden, entitled: "Status and Operations of Nippon Yusen Kaisha, Leading Japanese Shipping Firm".

Source: Deutsche Zeitung in Norwegen, December 23, 1943.

TRANSLATION FROM GERMAN.

Title: Favorable Development of Nippon Yusen Kaisha.

Hamburg, December 22.

During the general meeting of the N.Y.K., the president of this leading Japanese shipping company communicated remarkable results relative to the activities and business expansion of the firm during the war. His exposition of the organization of Japanese shipping, still being effected, was particularly interesting. Ships not requisitioned were taken over more than a year ago by the "Ship-control Corporation". This corporation assigns the ships to various associations, the N.Y.K. being a member of one of them. At the expense of the Control Corporation, the ships are then assigned for various purposes. In addition, the shipping companies receive fixed compensation for certain work performed by themselves. In July of this year, another reform was effected by the Control Corporation. In accordance with this reform, the N.Y.K. not only manages the ships assigned to it but also ships of other shipping companies, so that ships of ten separate owners now are managed by the N.Y.K. The favorable position of the firm, even during the war, is emphasized by the information that a dividend of 8 percent again could be distributed. Depreciation was written off as usual and the reserve fund was increased. The construction of wooden vessels undertaken by the company has proven to be sound. From the preceding year, 3.7 million yen was carried over as profit, while the 1943 profit comprised 6.7 million yen, making a total of 10.4 million yen available. Out of this amount, 2.5 million yen was set aside for expansion, plant and operation, 1.5 million for special reserves, 3.7 million for dividends, and 3.7 million yen carried over for the next half-year period.

Tokyo's despatch 4713, May 15, 1940,
"Labor Problems in Japan"

Another of the Embassy's series of preliminary sketches with regard to economic and social conditions in Japan. Two previous despatches of this series are attached.

The Embassy reports that statistical data on labor conditions have been suppressed and that the despatch is based largely on observation, hearsay, and deduction.

The Embassy finds a shortage of both industrial and agricultural labor in Japan, with shortages most serious in technicians and skilled laborers. Real wages, excepting for highly skilled work, are declining as a result of a rise in prices more rapid than in wage scales. It is noteworthy that the Home Ministry has decided to prohibit the formation of a new labor party which has been projected by the Abe faction of the Social Mass Party. Such a group might implement labor unrest, which is at present inarticulate.

Comment: There are available statistical data on labor wages, prices, and living costs in Japan. These, of course, require a good deal of interpretation, but it would seem that there is a larger factual basis for labor reporting than is apparent from this despatch.

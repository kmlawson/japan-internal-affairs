Washington, D.C.

Sir:

I have the honor to report there has been a rumor at Valparaiso for several days to the effect that some convoyed Japanese merchant vessels would arrive there in the near future, and that investigations have been made and no confirmation has been found.

The basis story was that space at Valparaiso docks for three Japanese merchant ships had been reserved by a telephone call for some time between March 23 and April 2. The ships were supposed to have been inscribed in the confidential book kept at the Valparaiso customs house of ships of belligerent nations calling at that port. Not only are the names of the Japanese ships not in the confidential book, which has been checked, but the man who received the telephone call said that the person who made it and who spoke in English refused to give his name and then hung up. Also, the reservation of dock space in this manner

manner would be a very unusual proceeding, if true, for an expedition of this kind because (1) there could be no surer way of making the rumor public, and (2) it is inconceivable that even if the request were bona fide they would expect the Valparaiso authorities to hold dock space for three ships empty for ten days. The Naval Attache of this Mission has reported in a despatch to the Navy Department at Washington that no confirmation of the rumor has been obtained.

The American Consul at Valparaiso has received some information which might tend to substantiate the rumor. The following is the text of a memorandum enclosed with his letter of March 23, 1942, to the Embassy:

"Contact who is an employee of a large bank and has a small business of his own, says that a Japanese with a name something like Kanamori and who represents Itoh y Cia. went to see him in the bank this morning to inquire if his import permit for about $2,000 of worsted to be imported from Japan was still valid; contact told him that it was and gave the Japanese the impression that he was an Axis sympathizer in order to get all possible information out of him.

"The Japanese told contact that the convoy would arrive in a short time. He told contact that of course Japan had no other interest in selling the goods which could be sold in Asia as well as in many other parts of the world, but to destroy the morale of the United States and prove to Latin Americans that the United States did not dominate the Pacific. Contact asked the Japanese if he did not think that the United States Navy would interfere and he said that the United States had only small patrol ships down in these waters but that the convoy would be protected by a fleet of heavy ships."

The Consul has been asked to report to the Embassy any further information on this subject which may come to his attention.

Respectfully yours,
Claude G. Bowers

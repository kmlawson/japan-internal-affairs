I have the honor to review and summarize general conditions affecting the rice supply in Japan which has become an important problem facing the Government at the present time. It is widely reported that the outlook for the present "rice year" which began on November 1, 1939, is the most unfavorable since the year of the rice riots of 1918. The price of rice is reported to be the highest in the last thirty years. The Government has undertaken

The present difficulty follows a severe drought during the past summer in southwestern Japan, and owing to the prevalence at the same time of similar conditions in southern Korea, there resulted a drastic reduction of the amount of Korean rice shipped to Japan during the past season. It is reported that the authorities in Korea have undertaken stringent measures, that domestic consumption in Korea will be curtailed, and that the deficiency in food stocks will be made up by the importation of millet from Manchuria and of other grains from Japan. From careful study of the information available and from discussion with various informed individuals, it is believed that while Japan faces a serious problem in rice distribution at the present time, owing to the reluctance of present holders of stocks to place their grain on the market, a serious crop failure in Chosen, decreased imports from Taiwan and increased local demand for rice, there is nevertheless no danger of a serious shortage during the coming rice year. The Government has announced the curtailment of rice for sake brewing by 2,000,000 koku. It is estimated that the programs for voluntary economy by the people, and the 70 percent polishing of rice, will represent a further saving of at least 1,000,000 koku. The rice problem in Japan at the present time is, however, potentially very dangerous and for this reason the Government and the people evidence considerable apprehension. A similarly curtailed crop next year would undoubtedly represent a disaster of major proportions. Production of rice in Japan requires intensive cultivation and liberal application of fertilizer. The quantity of fertilizer available to the farmers in the last year has decreased and if this commodity is not supplied to the farmers in sufficient quantity at the time of rice planting next year there will undoubtedly follow a decrease in the rice production, even if other conditions are favorable. While this problem is not one which should be acute at the present time considering the amount of rice actually available, the conditions are aggravated

The political effects of a disturbance in the distribution of rice are obvious. The Cabinet has not shown a high degree of acumen in meeting the problem. Although professing to follow a general "low price policy", the Cabinet nevertheless twice increased the price of rice. Resentment followed among the consumers in the cities and among the farmers who had sold their stocks prior to the increases. At the present time there is in the cities apprehension of further increases in the cost of rice, which leads to hoarding; in the country there is hope of further increases in the price of rice, which also leads to hoarding. In both sections there is full knowledge of the danger of a partial failure next year. To offset these factors the Government must stabilize the price of rice and must assure an adequate supply of fertilizer and other artificial factors necessary to a good crop. # RICE SUPPLY SITUATION

## Crop Failure in Chosen Causes Shortage
## But It Is Only Temporary Phenomenon

The Oriental Economist, December 1939

Until very recently it was frequently asserted, and with an understandable touch of national pride, that Japan would never suffer from a shortage of rice for food. This observation is no longer to be heard, and since the great failure of the crop in Chosen, comment has taken on something of an alarming one. However, it is still

clear that if both the Government and people can face the situation squarely and work wisely and perseveringly to overcome it, there is no basic cause for alarmist views. At bottom the food shortage is mainly due to the drought that occurred in the last crop season, which after all is a temporary phenomenon.

The Oriental Economist learns from members of its own staff who have surveyed the situation in Chosen, that the conditions caused there by the drought are more disastrous than could possibly have been imagined. It was the worst visitation of nature in 90 years, according to old native farmers who in their first forecasts had estimated the crop at 14 million koku, which was a fairly close approximation of 14.5 million koku as calculated by The Oriental Economist by its own method. The drought effected an area that would be enclosed within a circle with its center in the middle of the Genkai Sea, including several prefectures in Japan with Shimonoseki as the center, and in Chosen the provinces comprising the southern end of the peninsula where the worst damage was suffered.

The total paddy-field area in Chosen is more than 1.6 million chubu of which only about 600,000 chubu is irrigated while the remainder depends mostly on rain. Under this condition of farming, which is the reverse of that in Japan, there are good crops in seasons of heavy rainfall and poor crops in seasons when rain is scarce. The rainfall during the current crop season was only 48 per cent of the average throughout the whole peninsula and only 35 per cent in the southern part. The area north of Keijo in the harvested average crops on the whole, but the farmers in the area to the south suffered heavy damage.

## TIMELY STEPS TAKEN

Faced by this situation, the authorities in Chosen took timely steps. Four months ago they began enforcing economies in rice consumption with the object of maintaining the exports to Japan as far as possible, while also supplying food for the men serving at the front and thus performing Chosen's role

as a base of military supply. As practical steps, arrangements were made to import 2.1 million koku of millet from Manchuria, while the polishing of rice by more than 70 per cent and the use of more highly polished rice for food were prohibited by law. Steps are also being taken to help the drought victims, to assist landowners in securing a smooth supply of food, and to restore public confidence in the general situation. Despite the extent of the crop failure, there is no fear whatever, that Chosen's own food supply will run short. The question is solely whether or not the peninsula will have a surplus to export to Japan, and if not, what the effect will be in Japan.

The first forecast of this year's rice crop in Japan proper was 64.7 million koku. With no imports from Chosen at all and with 5 million koku of imports from Taiwan, the total supply would be 69.7 million koku, excluding the stocks to be carried forward from the previous season. On the other hand, the consumption of rice amounted to 79 million koku in 1937 and to 80 million in 1938. The requirement for this year is estimated at around 78 million koku. Without imports from Chosen it is clear that there will be a considerable shortage. This is the reason why there has been talk of economizing on rice, curtailing sake brewing and even importing rice from Thailand. However, in the opinion of The Oriental Economist the situation, if properly met, will not require the importation of Thai rice for reason that will be explained later.

The production of rice is, of course, largely determined by conditions of nature. For instance, in 1933 Japan harvested a record crop of 71 million koku and carried over an almost phenomenal surplus stock of 17 million koku to the following rice year. As a result the market slumped so drastically that the Government had to begin buying when the price touched a bottom level of 715 per koku, and by the time the market had regained its stability the Government had it

By no means all of the crop in Japan was affected by the drought. The section north of the Osaka-Kyoto area will reap a harvest so rich as to be unusual in its history, and it is said that in the prefecture of Niigata alone the increase will be 1 million koku or nearly 30 per cent. Earlier in the season these conditions led some optimists in the northern part of the country to predict that the national crop for the current season would be as much as 73 million koku, even surpassing the record crop of 1933, and even the weight of trade opinion in the Tokyo and Osaka markets seemed to be that the figure would be at least 65 million koku and possibly 60 million koku. But this was not to be. From the Kansai and districts west of Osaka came the first complaints about a lack of water, and then followed a period of rising heat without rain. Alarm began to be voiced about the growing conditions of drought, and later reports told of worsening conditions. The first crop forecast of the Government fell below even the conservative popular estimate of 65 million koku.

Of course, it is extremely difficult to achieve accuracy in estimating the crop, but it has been a rule in Japan that seasons marked by drought bring richer crops than expected, as the actual yield always exceeds the forecasts. It would not be surprising if the same thing could be said of Fukuoka, Saga Hiroshima, Shimane, Tottori and Kagawa, the other prefectures which suffered. When allowance is made for the above-mentioned rule that a season of drought brings rice crops after all, and when allowance is also made for the good weather conditions that prevailed through the ripening season in the Autumn, the crop estimate of 68 million koku made by The Oriental Economist seems, if anything, to err on the side of conservatism.

With regard to the rice crop in Chosen, it was assumed at the outset that there would be no surplus to export if the yield is 14 million koku as given in the first forecast. The Oriental Economist, however, ventures to say that the colony will be able to export to Japan some 2 million koku in the coming year. An explanation of the following considerations on which its estimate is based, requires some idea of consumption in the peninsula. According to the new method of investigation, the consumption there amounted to 15.8 million koku in 1938 and something like that amount will probably be consumed this year. However, according to the former method of investigation, the average consumption for the five years prior to 1938 was 9.3 million koku per year. The first forecast for the current year, computed on the old basis, will amount to approximately 11 million koku. This means that there will be a surplus of at least one million koku against the average consumption of 9.3 million koku, even allowing for a reasonable increase resulting from the improved financial conditions and higher standards of living, and setting the total consumption at 10 million koku. Moreover, 2.1 million koku of millet is certain to be imported from Manchuria; and substitute crops raised to offset the failure of rice include an area of 170,000 chobu sown to buckwheat which should yield at least half a million koku. The uncommonly favorable weather during the Autumn months should increase the rice crop by at least 200,000 koku and possibly by half a million koku over the estimate. The new rule against polluting rice by more than 70 per cent and the curtailment of rice brewing together should save something like 300,000 koku. The farms where the crop was recorded as a complete failure will actually yield from 20 to 30 per cent of seed-rice at least and these farms constitute a considerable acreage. In view of all these items, the surplus available for export to Japan has been estimated at 2 million koku, apart from any question of exports to Manchuria or China. The above calculation was made on the assumption that the imports from Taiwan will be the same as in the average year.

While we have been unable to obtain complete or accurate figures concerning the importation of foreign rice, it is believed by many competent observers that the volume of rice imported will necessarily exceed that suggested in my despatch under reference (¥200,000,000). To supplement rice importations and to facilitate distribution of rice in this country, the Government has embarked upon a program of purchasing domestic rice in the amount of 10,500,000 koku (1 koku = 5 bushels). From the various meagre press reports, which are disappointingly lacking in details, it is obvious that the government has met with considerable difficulty in effecting these purchases. The Minister of Agriculture is reported to have urged the cooperation and assistance of the various prefectural governors and to have expressed expectation that the program of purchasing domestic rice would be successfully completed by the end of this month. The government undoubtedly desires to acquire these holdings in order to assure the availability of a minimum amount of rice necessary for the carry-over period prior to harvesting of this year's crop. Compulsory sale has been practiced in several areas. It has been reported that hoarding and reluctance on the part of farmers to sell their holdings have interfered materially with the Government's program. The press is unanimous in its censure of the secrecy practiced

Many prefectural governors are reported to have urged that a careful accounting be made of rice stocks in Japan and of imported stocks and that the people be informed of the amounts available. The Government has said that it will give careful consideration to the proposals; but in the meantime has confined its efforts to attempts to induce the people further to restrict and reduce consumption.

There have been reported numerous complaints from agricultural districts concerning the shortage of rice bran and other livestock feeds. It has also been suggested in the press that the areas devoted to rice cultivation next year will be restricted in serious measure owing to a combination of unfavorable circumstances such as the shortage and high cost of fertilizers, the inadequate number of agricultural laborers and the shortage of agricultural equipment. The Government is said to be considering "adequate measures" to deal with the situation.

The problem is still largely one of distribution, but there are increasing evidences that the Japanese consumers as well as the producers of rice are not entirely confident of the Government's ability to continue to meet the demand for rice nor of the adequacy of rice supplies, and there has been therefore an intensification of the tendency to hoard this essential commodity. With regard to the long-range view of the problem, it is again to be emphasized that there are many indications that the quantity of fertilizer available to farmers, even at increased prices, will be decreased from the amount supplied last year, the supply of agricultural laborers will probably continue to decrease and there is little prospect of increasing the amount of equipment and materials available to farmers. JAPAN'S RICE SUPPLY

For several years previous to the rice crop year of 1939-1940 (the rice crop year extends from November of one year through October of the next), imports of rice into Japan from outside the Empire constituted only a negligible portion of Japan's rice consumption. For example, the average annual imports of foreign rice into Japan during the five-year period extending from November 1932 through October 1937 were only 122 million pounds as compared with an annual consumption of rice in Japan during that period of 23,355 million pounds. During that period imports of rice constituted only a small fraction of one percent of consumption in Japan. During the two succeeding crop years imports were even smaller: in 1937-1938 imports amounted to 47 million pounds as compared with a consumption of 25,123 million pounds, and in 1938-1939 imports amounted to 49 million pounds as compared with a consumption of 24,918 million pounds. Most of these small imports came from Thailand (except that in 1939 almost half of imports came from China), only very small quantities coming from Indochina and Burma.

During

=== Page 26 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

During the crop year 1939-1940 there were extremely unfavorable conditions for the growing of rice in the Japanese Empire. There was an unprecedented drought which drastically reduced production in Chosen while the unfavorable weather conditions also affected sharply production both in Japan and in Taiwan. The Department of Agriculture estimates that during that year production in Japan was reduced from 20,693 million pounds to 19,477 million pounds and shipments from the colonies to Japan were reduced from 3,023 million pounds to 1,099 million pounds. It was therefore necessary not only to decrease consumption at home by various arbitrary measures but to import large quantities of rice from abroad. It is estimated that approximately 3,500 million pounds of foreign rice was imported into Japan during the year, or about fifteen percent of consumption in Japan. Of these foreign imports it is estimated that approximately 1,500 million pounds were taken out of China and approximately 2,000 million pounds were imported from Thailand, Burma and Indochina. (Exports from Thailand to Japan during the period January to July 1940 amounted to 376 million pounds and the exports from Burma to Japan during the period January - August 7, 1940, amounted to 716 million pounds. Export statistics for Indochina are not available, but

=== Page 27 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

but it is known that exports to Japan were sizeable, and it is estimated that exports from all three areas to Japan during the crop year 1939-1940 amounted to about 2,000 million pounds.)

Mr. Dawson, Agricultural Attaché at Shanghai, estimates that during the crop year 1940-1941 production in Japan will be even smaller, chiefly as a result of shortages of fertilizer, labor and animals, but that shipments from the colonies will be considerably increased from last year and that foreign imports will amount to approximately 2,200 million pounds. He estimates, however, that shipments from occupied areas of China are unlikely to equal shipments of last year. It seems then that, estimating that Japan will obtain 1,000 million pounds from China, Japan during the coming season will be obliged to import about 1,000 or 1,200 million pounds of rice from Thailand, Burma and Indochina, or four or five percent of Japan's sharply reduced estimated consumption of 23,000 million pounds (the estimate being reduced because of restrictions upon consumption).

There is a large exportable surplus of rice in French Indochina. During the three years 1935-1937, inclusive, French Indochina exported an annual average of 3,717 million pounds of rice, chiefly to France and China.

French

=== Page 28 === (confidence 1.00)
-4-

French Indochina could undoubtedly supply all of Japan's import requirements if need should arise. Rice is produced in Indochina, however, principally in the southern portion of Indochina, which is not yet occupied by the Japanese.

As a result principally of the shortage and other factors, the price of rice in Japan increased steadily from ¥36.45 per koku in July 1939 to ¥43.30 in December of that year, at which point the price was pegged by the Government. There has been no subsequent change in the official price of rice, and according to information received from Japan the price has been fairly well adhered to by the trade. Late in 1939 and throughout 1940 the Government took other steps to alleviate the keen rice shortage. The hoarding of rice was prohibited; the Government inaugurated a program of buying in surplus regions and selling in deficit areas; decrees were issued to reduce consumption, including the curtailing of rice wine brewing by forty percent, restricting the polishing of rice and requiring an extensive use of substitute cereals; and steps were taken to increase supplies by making unprecedented heavy purchases of foreign rice. Official control over the distribution of rice was extended by an ordinance effective September 10, 1940. All rice must now be distributed through

=== Page 29 === (confidence 1.00)
-5-

through the Government-controlled distribution agencies and even farmers residing in producing areas will be required to obtain rice supplies from officially recognized sales of commercial agencies. The regulations also provide for a complete ban on the barter of rice in the rural districts. In the cities, restrictions have been placed upon the amounts of rice which restaurants and hotels may serve. Campaigns are being inaugurated to restrict home consumption and a system of ration cards has been established in some of the heavy consuming centers.

In conclusion, the rice situation does not seem likely to be as stringent during the 1940-1941 crop year as it has been during the year 1939-1940, due to the fact that larger production in and shipments from the colonies are likely to more than compensate for decreased production in Japan and due to more effective government control of price and distribution. Substantially smaller supplies of foreign rice will be required and at least half of this supply may be obtained from China. It would be almost impossible to prevent supplies of rice from China from reaching Japan by naval measures. A strongly enforced blockade maintained from Hong Kong and Manila might be able to interfere somewhat with the shipment of rice from Indochina to Japan, but probably not to cut off

=== Page 30 === (confidence 0.93)
-6-

off that supply completely. Considering all factors, important among which are the internal measures of control in Japan, it does not seem likely that Japan's supplies of rice can be interfered with by foreign countries in such manner as to be a critical blow at Japan's economy.

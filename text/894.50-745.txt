DEPARTMENT OF OVERSEAS TRADE.
COMMERCIAL REPRESENTATION ABROAD.
British overseas trade, the Department has developed and
rwing Services of Commercial Intelligence Officers :—
1. IN THE EMPIRE.
The Trade Commissioner and Imperial Trade Correspondent Services.
At the present time there are 15 Trade Commissioners' offices. These
are situated, four in Camada (Montreal, Toronto, Vancouver, Winnipeg) ;
two in Australia (Sydney, Melbourne) ; South Africa (Capetown, Johannes-
burg); and India (Calcutta and Bombay—to cover also Ceylon) : and one
each in New Zealand (Wellington), Irish Free State (Dublin), British East
Africa (Nairobi), British West Indies (Trinidad) and Malaya (Singapore).
The Trade Commissioners in the Dominions have the assistance of Imperial
Trade Correspondents at a number of important centres.
In various parts of the Empire in which at present there are no com-
missioners, there are correspondents with whom the Department deals direct.
2. IN FOREIGN COUNTRIES.
(a) The Commercial Diplomatic Service attached to the British
Diplomatic Missions.
This service consists of 36 officers who are stationed in all the more
important foreign markets of the world. The members of the Commercial
Diplomatic Service are styled " Commercial Counsellors" in the highest
grade, and " Commercial Secretaries " in the two lower grades. They are
members of the staff of the British Embassy or Legation in which they serve.
The Commercial Diplomatic Officer has general supervision over the
commercial work of the consular officers in his area and, with the co-operation
of these two services, a complete network of Government commercial repre-
sentatives is thrown over foreign countries.
The Department is represented in Japan by Mr. G. B. Sansom, C.M.G.,
Commercial Counsellor, and Mr. H. A. Macrae, M.A., Commercial Secretary,
His Majesty's Embassy, Tokyo.
(b) The British Consular Service.

I. GENERAL ECONOMIC SITUATION.
This section of last year's report carried the record of events to
the month of June, 1929, at which point the Finance Minister had
just stated publicly that, although it was the Government's policy
to raise the gold embargo, this step could not be lightly taken in
the then condition of the money market. But during the last days
of June there came a Cabinet crisis and a change of Government.
The new Ministry announced immediately that it was their inten-
tion to prepare at once for the removal of the embargo, and to
remove it at the earliest possible date. The essential preparations
were to include retrenchment in Government expenditure and a
national thrift campaign. As soon as results of these measures
were reflected in interest rates and the exchange value of the yen
the free gold market would be restored.
Previous administrations had announced their desire to remove
the embargo and had stopped at that; but it was clear that this
new Ministry was in earnest. Public realisation of this fact con-
tributed to the success of their economy campaign. The Govern-
ment led the way by reducing the expenditure in the budget which
they inherited from their predecessors, and for a time at least even
private individuals, under the influence of official propaganda, cut
down their personal spending. Department stores began to suffer
and restaurateurs would inquire of diners whether they would have
a Government or an Opposition meal, the former being a very
frugal affair and the latter somewhat more lavish. There is no
doubt that the thrift campaign, though it could not, of course,
fundamentally affect economic conditions, did in a small measure
assist to create an atmosphere favourable to the removal of the
embargo. Meanwhile the prospect of an early return to free gold
exports naturally caused importers to hold off from foreign pur-
chases, because they anticipated a rise in the exchange value of
the yen. This attitude of caution was accompanied by a genuine
fall in the demand for foreign goods, since it was not the moment
to commence new enterprises or to expand old ones, while the
reduction of Government expenditure indirectly caused a reduction
in expenditure on private undertakings. By the time that the new
Ministry had been in office for three months the adverse balance
of trade had been substantially reduced, and in fact the excess of
imports was the smallest excess recorded for some years.

=== Page 24 === (confidence 0.99)
9
The chief danger, however, against which Mr. Inouye, the
Finance Minister, had to guard was an outflow of money into
foreign investments. There were still large sums of idle money
in the form of deposits of the leading banks, at no interest, with
the Bank of Japan. This was to a great extent the unabsorbed
balance of the special credits granted under emergency legisla-
tion to repair the damage of the financial crisis of 1927, and
amounted probably to at least 300 million yen. Fortunately for
Mr. Inouye at this juncture interest rates in Japan and in the
United States began to draw closer together, and the price of
Japanese bonds in New York had recently much improved. These
circumstances combined to discourage Japanese investors from
purchasing securities abroad. The exchange value of the yen
improved rapidly, and the collapse on the New York Stock
Exchange in the autumn of 1929 by bringing down rates of interest
in the United States reduced the danger of an outflow of gold after
the removal of the embargo. These factors were considered
favourable, and in November the Government announced that the
embargo would be raised on January 11th, 1930.
The programme was duly carried out. A complete description
of the operation, from the time when the decision was taken until
the present moment, is of great technical interest, but cannot be
given here. Certain general features, however, may suitably be
mentioned. In the first place, since the Government had definitely
decided to restore the currency to an effective gold standard, it was
necessary to guard against a sudden outflow of gold. The currency
was well protected and some deflation might be expected, but in the
transition period there might be an undue drain upon the reserve
in Japan. To ensure against this credits were, in November,
1929, opened in London and New York to the extent of 50 million
yen each ; but it is understood that so far no recourse has been had
to them. The Bank of England and the Federal Reserve Bank of
the United States gave an assurance of moral support. The Bank
of Japan appears also to have come to some unofficial understand-
ing with the leading Japanese banks by which the latter were to
refrain from sending large amounts abroad for investments, at
least during the critical period. Further, to reduce the export of
gold, the Government undertook an intensive campaign for the
reduction of imports and the use of domestic products in substitu-
tion. It is always questionable whether such campaigns have any
enduring results, but certainly some effect can be discerned in the
returns of foreign trade and in the complaints of importers, though
it is possible that general trade depression has been a more
powerful deterrent than mere propaganda. A number of the pur-
chasing departments, e.g. the Ministry of Railways, were fur-
nished with long detailed lists of articles which they must purchase
from domestic suppliers and much shorter lists of articles which
they might purchase abroad. Such a policy, even if not pursued
with extreme rigour, must at least temporarily diminish the export

immediate outlook for British exporters is therefore depressing,
time feared that by the return of the yen to par value importers
who were not amenable to Government control would be en-

domestic industries ; and certain members of the Tariff Commission
argued that an upward revision of the customs tariff should be
made in advance as a protective measure. But the responsible
ministers did not share this view, although there was some talk of
giving a general authority to the Cabinet to make tariff changes
by Ordinance and without reference to Parliament, should any
particular industry appear to be endangered. In the event no such
action was taken, and in fact the tariff revision sanctioned by the
Diet at its session in the spring of 1930 consisted principally of a
reduction in the rates of duty upon a few items.
When the embargo was removed the outflow of gold from
Japan did not, according to official announcements, exceed the
anticipations of the financial authorities, though they were,
strangely enough, taken aback by the number of applications for
small amounts of gold over the counter. But these arose from
mere curiosity, and soon subsided. In general the operation pro-
ceeded smoothly, though at first there were certain difficulties in
obtaining gold for shipment and the exchange market collapsed
for some days. Accusations were levelled in the press against
foreign banks operating in Japan that they were taking advantage
of a difficult situation by shipping gold as part of an exchange
gamble, but there seems to have been little or no truth in these
charges, and it was natural that foreign banks, being exchange
banks, should transfer funds to their headquarters on the most
favourable terms.
Though the operation of raising the embargo was facilitated by
the financial depression in the United States, the Japanese Govern-
ment were unfortunate in other respects, for at the very moment
when it was most desirable that their export trade should proceed
smoothly, so as to counteract the outflow of gold, they were con-
fronted by unusual difficulties in more than one quarter. Tariff
increases in India and Australia were a severe blow to them,
depression in America began to reduce the demand for silk from
Japan, their tariff negotiations with China were still pending and
—perhaps most serious of all—the price of silver fell heavily, so
that their earnings from export trade to China were reduced in
value by at least 20 per cent. Their chief markets were disturbed
or poverty-stricken.
In such difficult circumstances the Government were obliged
to redouble their efforts to reduce expenditure, to diminish imports
and to encourage exports. In these tasks they had at least the

melancholy satisfaction of feeling that very little persuasion was
needed to prevent both firms and individuals from embarking on
new expenditure, for acute financial depression prevailed in every
quarter. Measures were taken to assist small manufacturers and
traders by the loan of funds upon easy terms, and by the introduc-
tion of a system analogous to the English Export Credits scheme.
No doubt these measures will be of some benefit, but it seems that
the present condition of trade and industry is too fundamentally
depressed to recover by the mere application of local remedies.
Improvement of credit facilities may have a slight palliative effect,
but what both merchants and manufacturers want are orders. If
they can get business they can get credit, but unfortunately the
converse proposition does not hold good. Almost every industry
and trade in Japan at the time of writing (June, 1930), is in low
water, and newspaper articles on commercial topics bear such

Industries.-Industrial production during the period under
review makes a good statistical showing. In the latter half of 1929
there was a marked increase in the output of staples such
as raw silk, cotton yarn, cotton tissues, coal, &c., the pro-
duction curve rising well above the trend line showing expected
natural increase, although during all this period output restric-
tions were in force in almost all important industries. The com-
mercial results of this increased activity have, however, not been
so satisfactory, as will be seen from the accounts of various com-
modity markets given elsewhere in this report. Further, in the
first part of 1930 even the natural increase received a slight check,
and by the month of June a stagnant condition had been reached
in most branches of production. How much this is to be ascribed
to deflationary tendencies operating upon already depressed
markets cannot be discussed here. There has been much argument
both as to diagnosis and remedy, and the magic word " rationalisa-
tion " has afforded comfort to many. But when it comes to its
practical application, certain critically-minded industrialists are
inclined to suggest that it is only a new name for an old thing, and
at best a mere counsel of perfection which does not help them in
their troubles. In so far as " rationalisation " means the amalga-
mation of productive units, it brings obvious dangers in its train
if it is not cautiously adopted. Chief among these is unemploy-
ment, The figures of unemployment are already somewhat dis-
turbing and are drawing upon the Government a heavy fire of
criticism, their retrenchment policy in particular being strongly
attacked on the ground that it checks enterprise at a moment when
it is most needed. There is at present no adequate official record
of unemployment such as is obtainable in Great Britain, and it is
therefore difficult to gauge the extent to which it has increased in
Japan of late. But it seems clear that the total number of unem-
ployed persons has risen rapidly in the last six months. According
to the investigations of the Home Ministry, which probably do
not cover the whole ground, there has been an increase from
268,000 in September, 1929, to 372,000 in April, 1930.
Though the foregoing makes a gloomy picture of the industrial
position in Japan, it must not be interpreted as criticism of indi-

(1) The re-formation of the Teisan* for the purpose of withdrawing

(3) The curtailment of production by 20 per cent. in all filatures

the end of March, large shipments being made to India in anticipation of the

effort now being made by mill-owners to economise by introducing the high

and India a portion of her present grey goods trade and must turn more
more to the production of finished goods to supply not only her present
rkets but also to attempt to push afield into African, Near Eastern, and
outh American areas hitherto supplied mainly by Lancashire. The present
Government postponed the erection of the Cotton Trade Experimental
Institute at Nagoya, but no doubt the plan will be soon revived, while cotton
dustrialists both in Osaka and Nagoya are making independent studies to
prove their facilities for bleaching and finishing, especially in the case of
dhuties," in which line they see an opportunity to attack Lancashire's
dition.
Some reference was made in last year's report to the difficulties of the raw
cotton importers. Trade has been so bad this year that two of the " big
three" made severe losses, and one, the Nippon Menkwa Company, has had
to readjust its position by sacrificing all its reserves (Yen 22,000,000) and
cutting its capital by three-fifths. A proposal has also been put forward to
create a Cotton Arbitration Board in the case of demands by mills—reported
to be very frequent nowadays—for allowances on raw cotton deliveries.
Under the pressure of weavers there has been a downward revision of the
Japanese Tariff on cotton yarns, but it is doubtful if independent weavers will
benefit much, as the prices of Japanese yarns are so low to-day that despite
the cheaper cost of production in China, Chinese yarns cannot yet be intro-
duced at favourable rates. Should, however, prices advance, Japanese
spinners may feel the effect of this competition.
Reverting to the abolition of night work and the lifting of the gold embargo,
Japanese cotton industrialists planned to meet the resulting difficulties by
increasing their spindleage, and the number of automatic looms, and by
reducing costs other than labour. Before the abolition of night work the cost
of spinning was divided as to raw material from 75-76 per cent., the balance
being labour, administration, interest and taxes ; after its abolition, cost of
raw material has averaged from 71 per cent. or 72 per cent. to 75 per cent.
according to mills. The effect of the lifting of the gold embargo on the cotton
industry was :—
1. A fall of 10 per cent. in the value of stocks, which was much minimised
by hedging.
2. A weakening of ability to compete abroad unless costs other than
materials and overhead also fell.
3. Increased difficulty in maintaining the value of fixed assets.
4. Decreased domestic buying power brought about by the general depres-
sion caused by the lifting of the gold embargo.
But these difficulties have been much enhanced by unexpected events,
which could not have been foreseen, such as the slump of silver, the
raising of the tariff on cotton textiles in India, and the prolonged world-wide
economic depression.
By December it was found that the increased output of cotton yarns, which
reached 256,000 bales in that month, could not be consumed, and the Japan
Cotton Spinners' Association decided to reimpose restriction of output by a
net amount of from 12-13 per cent., so that the monthly output has now
fallen to from 220,000 to 225,000 bales. At the end of April the number
of working spindles was 5,962,965 and the average monthly output of yarn
231,678 bales. In March the Indian Government, for the protection of the
home cotton industry, raised the tariff on cotton grey goods from 11 to 20 per
cent. or to a specific duty of 3‡ annas per lb. whichever was the larger, and
on finished goods to 20 per cent., with a 5 per cent. preference to British
goods, except where the specific duty was leviable. This action caused both
consternation and indignation in Japanese cotton circles, as it hit their trade

There remains little to add to the foregoing account except to
say that in order to offset the prospective loss of trade with India
the Japan Cotton Spinners' Association and the Japan Cotton
Yarn and Cloth Export Merchants' Union have taken definite
steps to develop trade with Russia and with South and West
Africa. The Association and the Union are forming an export
combine of their members, to be called The Japan Cotton Cloth
Export Union, whose purpose is the investigation of the market
for cotton cloths in the countries named and the introduction of
Japanese cloths there. It is proposed that the Union shall first
despatch investigators and salesmen to the territories in question
and that, when business has begun, a new Export Association
shall be formed to deal particularly with the new territories. One
such mission, of six " inspectors," has already been despatched
by the city of Nagoya to South Africa, Zanzibar, Kenya, &c.
This method has been used with success by the Japanese cotton
industry in China and Manchuria and in British India.
It should also be added that the restriction of output of yarns

restriction of 10 per cent., effective from June 16th, 1930, to the end
of the year. This brings the total restriction of productive capacity
up to approximately 25 per cent. and reduces the monthly output
of yarn to about 200,000 bales.

A British exporting house with experience of the Japanese porce-
lain industry has kindly supplied the following information :—
" As regards exports of porcelain to America, it is certain that the increase
was due principally to an expected increase in the tariff. Early in 1929 it
was proposed that a heavy specific duty be placed on porcelain ware in addition
to the ruling ad valorem duty. This duty, it was believed, would be brought
into force some time in September and as a consequence there was large buying
in anticipation. All factories were working at top speed, as it was generally

=== Page 46 === (confidence 1.00)
31
expected in Japan that once the new rates of duty were in force there would be
a serious business depression for some months. Actually, the revised tariff
did not come into effect during the year. As regards the increased importation
by Great Britain, this was mainly due to improved manufacturing conditions
in Japan. Many entirely new items were brought out during the year—
copies of European merchandise, etc.—and it was found that Japan could
produce more cheaply than Europe in a large number of cases. For exports to
Great Britain the makers specialized in domestic articles, and in spite of the
duty of 28s. per cwt. on this class of commodity, exports increased.
Progress in Process of Manufacture.—
Due to the scarcity of money, very few factories have been in a position to
install up-to-date mechanism in their plants. A few of the larger factories,
however, have replaced the old style coal kilns with electrical apparatus,
thereby being in a position to improve their products. Under the old method
of firing it was difficult to obtain uniform temperature in the kiln, the result
being a very high percentage of rejections. With the new electric kiln it is
possible to obtain uniform heat, and to regulate it as required. This new
method not only effects an enormous saving as regards rejections, but also
does away with dust and dirt, and as a consequence, goods electrically fired
are far cleaner in every way than goods fired under the old process. The
decorating of porcelain and earthenware is still done largely by hand, and the
cost of this labour being small, it would be difficult to improve on this method.
Progress in Styles Produced.—
Enormous headway has been made recently, and Japan is to-day in a
position to cater satisfactorily for the requirements of foreign countries.
This advance in style is largely due to the number of Japanese who go abroad.
ascertain what other porcelain manufacturing countries are producing, and
return to Japan with samples, which they copy and improve upon. The
greatest progress seems to have been made in earthenware items—and here
Japan has advanced so rapidly that exports during 1929 were very consider-
able, and the future outlook is promising."
The foregoing may give the impression that the Japanese success
is largely due to imitation of foreign models, and this is true if
imitation means careful study of articles known to suit foreign
tastes. But it must not be forgotten that Japan has an ancient
tradition of pottery, and still produces wares which for beauty and
originality of design rival anything that is made in Europe.

From the steady decrease in imports which has accompanied the growth
of home production it is apparent that Japanese domestic products are at
present displacing imported goods.
The reasons for this change, according to the numerous authorities who
have been consulted, are as follows :—
(1) The declared policy of the authorities—which they follow irrespective
of changes of Government—to encourage home industries. The most obvious
translation of this policy into action is the maintenance of a high protective
tariff, and the inclusion in the 100 per cent. " Luxury Tariff " of certain high
quality fabrics. It must also be remembered that there is a government
clothing factory, and that contracts not only for the army and navy but also
for police, railway, tramway and similar services are naturally taken by
Japanese producers.
(2) Japanese mills can supply requirements much more quickly than
European mills. Orders for English or German cloths for the Spring season
must be given in April or May of the previous year, while orders for Japanese
Spring season goods are settled only in December of the previous year. For
Japanese makers this simplifies transactions, and lessens the element of un-
certainty. Furthermore, payment for imported goods must be definite and
immediate, whereas there is more latitude given to buyers by the Japanese
mills.
(3) Unfavourable economic conditions during the past few years have
reduced purchasing power, and thus contributed to the success of Japanese
goods, which are cheaper.
(4) The three preceding causes have contributed to the advance of
Japanese mills in technical skill and in workmanship.
For the four reasons above stated it is clear that in course of time Japanese
goods will gain increasing control of the domestic market. What margin will
remain for import cannot exactly be stated, since it depends upon questions of
fashion, price movements, etc., which are unpredictable. There is a conflict
of opinion upon this point, even in the best informed quarters, but most
observers seem to think that there will always be a market for imported goods
of high quality or special type. One authority predicts that the Japanese
mills will, if they progress at the present rate, entirely check the imports of
low and middle class worsteds within 2 or 3 years. He adds, however, that
high class worsteds will still be imported because the Japanese mills will not
spend money on making new designs in small quantities, and in the high class
fabrics only small sales are possible, the trade being a " one piece per design "
trade, or even smaller. Further, " it will never pay Japanese dealers to go

It will be noticed that one of the largest items in both the above
lists is ammonium sulphate. A difficult situation has arisen with
regard to this important fertilising agent. Japanese manufac-
turers have vastly increased their output in recent years, and
meanwhile the price of imported sulphate in Japan for a time fell
so low that Japanese producers appealed to the Government to
impose import duties under the anti-dumping legislation which
was enacted in 1920 but so far has never been applied. The case
raises in an acute form the issue between the demands of industry
and the needs of agriculture, and this is the essence of the problem
by which Japan is now confronted at all points.
There has been during the last decade a strong tendency for
chemical fertilisers to displace organic agents such as oil cake and
fish manure. At the expense of these latter the consumption of
artificial fertilisers has risen from 31 per cent. of the total in 1927
to nearly 50 per cent. in 1930. The principal artificial agents are
ammonium sulphate, superphosphate of lime and compound
fertilisers.
The ammonium sulphate manufactured in Japan is produced
almost entirely by synthetic nitrogen plants (Casale, Claude,
cyanamide and similar processes). It is difficult to estimate
exactly either present or future production, but the output in
Japan, Corea and Manchuria of synthetic nitrogen compounds
for 1929, in terms of tons of sulphate of ammonia, was probably
just under 300,000 tons, and will increase yearly as new plants come
into operation. It is stated on good authority that the total
capacity by 1935 will be at least 750,000 tons, and some press
reports say as much as 1,200,000. A new company, the Showa
Fertiliser Company, expects to start operations shortly with a
yearly capacity of 200,000 tons, and is to receive a subsidy in the
shape of exemption from income and business profits taxes for
three years. The Corean works of the Japan Nitrogen Company

Automobiles.—On the basis of recent estimates the number
of motor vehicles in use in Japan proper is now approximately
100,000, of which total about 60,000 are passenger cars and 40,000
trucks and other kinds of freight vehicles. The total number
estimated to be in use in June, 1928, was 57,309 and in October,
1929,73,630.
There seems no doubt that the number must further increase,
since the use of motor vehicles in useful services, as carriers of
goods and on omnibus routes is growing apace with the indus-
trialisation of the country and the growth of the population, which
has learned to appreciate quick transport. Motor transport of
goods and passengers is indeed now increasing so fast that the
Ministry of Railways is seeking power from Parliament to control
it in order to prevent undue competition with the Government-
owned lines and to develop motor services to act as feeders of the
railways. The Minister of Railways is one of the strongest sup-
porters of the national campaign in favour of the use of domestic
products and the exclusion of imported goods, and it seems likely
that the interest of the Ministry in motor transport may lead to an
increase in the manufacture of motor vehicles in purely Japanese
factories, as distinct from factories in Japan—e.g. those of the
Ford Company and General Motors—controlled by foreign cor-
porations. At present three Japanese companies manufacture
motor vehicles—'buses and trucks only—with the help of Govern-
ment subsidies ; but their output is very small. It is now suggested
that manufacture on a larger scale should be undertaken, also
under subsidy, by these companies in co-operation with some of
the Japanese dockyards, which, owing to the completion of the
recent shipbuilding programme and the results of the London
Naval Conference of 1930, are very short of work. But this pro-
ject, if it materialises, is not likely, for the present, to diminish the
market for foreign-made vehicles.
In Corea and Formosa, as well as in Japan proper, an increase
in motor transport of both goods and passengers is taking place.
Practically all motor vehicles in Japanese territory are
chauffeur-driven. The owner-driver class, among the Japanese,
has not yet developed to any appreciable extent, partly because
there are no surfaced country roads to make motor touring
pleasant. The Japanese chauffeur is not interested in getting the
best out of a small engine by skilful gear-changing. He prefers
a car that will go everywhere on top gear. Hence the general
demand is for high-powered cars.
Everywhere there has been a very great increase in the use of
taxicabs, which are now very superior vehicles, mostly of the Ford
and Chevrolet saloon class. As to motor trucks, the general
demand, for commercial purposes, is for light trucks carrying up
to 2 tons.

Roads.—Progress has been made in the widening and
straightening of streets and bridges in Tokyo, and the new
thoroughfares are being surfaced with stone, asphalt or concrete.
The surface drainage system and street cleansing have also been
improved. Similar progress, but to a smaller extent, has been
made in some other cities, but in the towns and villages and in the
country, except between Tokyo and Yokohama and between Osaka
and Kobe, narrow roads with rough earth or gravel surfaces are
still all that exist. The absence of good motoring roads in the
country in Japan prevents development of the tourist traffic, and,
as has been frequently remarked, is one of the reasons why there
are so few owner-drivers of motor cars.

ECONOMIC CONDITIONS.
Note.—In the text of this report the terms " import " and " export " are
to be taken as including trade between Japan and Corea. The term
" foreign," however, is to be understood as excluding Japan.
There being no regular services between Corea and Europe or America,
foreign goods to and from Corea are usually transhipped at a port in Japan.
For purposes of convenience they are frequently passed through the Customs
at the port of transhipment and consequently appear in the Customs returns
as part of the trade between Japan and Corea. Not only, therefore, is it
impossible to get statistics of the total foreign trade, but it is extremely
difficult to obtain accurate information as to the volume of British and other
foreign trade.
Trading conditions.—The past twelve months have proved a trying
time for traders. Corea is dependent on Japan for finance and reacts at once
to trade conditions in that country. Not only, therefore, has business suffered
from the effects of the sudden rise in exchange, the retrenchment policy, the
economy campaign and falling markets, but it has had to contend with a
banking policy of severely restricted credit.
As if this was not enough, the rice crop of 1929 was a poor one for the
second year in succession. Unfortunately for the Corean farmer, the price of

=== Page 95 === (confidence 1.00)
rios in Japan is low and exports fell off so that the farmer is hard put to it to
make ends meet and since 80 per cent. of the population are farmers, the pur-
chasing power of the country as a whole has been seriously affected.
At the moment of writing there are no signs of recovery. Prices are not
yet settled and may go lower. Whether they do or not, so long as the future
is uncertain there is little hope of trade picking up.
Lest the picture should be altogether unrelieved, it may be well to mention
what hopeful factors there are. In the first place the cut in the Corean budget
was, all things considered, a slight one and did little more than check the rate
of development of Corea's resources. The economy campaign was more
serious as it involved dealers in heavy losses on stocks, but though the cure
has been a drastic one a return to lower price levels must in the long run
stimulate demand. Exports, again, showed a healthy expansion of trade in
most items other than rice. Good harvests in 1930 would go far to counteract
other depressing conditions.

The consumption of kerosene continues to increase. It is probable that
saturation point for illuminating kerosenes has now been reached but there is a
heavier demand for engine grade kerosenes. The offtake of kerosene is affected
by the harvests. The last two rice crops have been poor and there was a
partial crop failure in the south. These conditions and the official policy of
retrenchment have resulted in a decrease in consumption and difficulties
in payment, a state of affairs which has become most noticeable since the
beginning of 1930.
Consumption of illuminating kerosenes is at its highest during the period
October/January. The two main uses for engine grade kerosenes are rice
milling and irrigation. The " season " for the former is during November.
December, January and to a lesser extent during February and March.
In normal years the irrigation demand is over the period May/June/July.
Although economic factors may result in reduced demand, the illuminating
kerosene season does not move. The demand for engine grade kerosene
depends so much, in the case of rice milling, on harvest conditions and the
price of rice, and, in the case of irrigation, so much on the incidence of rains,
that from year to year the demand fora given number of engines and the period
of highest consumption vary. A comparison with last year illustrates this
point. This year rice milling was delayed owing to farmers holding their
stocks in the hope of a rise in the price of rice. The recent heavy rains have
taken the place of irrigation. The consumption of engine grade kerosene in
the south is therefore actually less than that of last year.

Timber industry and trade in timber.—The timber industry is a State enter-
prise. The timber comes from the forests in the Yalu and Tumen basins, the
former being by far the more important. The Government operates a saw
mill at Shingishu near the mouth of the Yalu which in recent years has produced
rather more than 3,000,000 cubic feet of planks, box-boards, etc., annually.
At the same time the Forestry Department in 1928 sold about six and a half
million cubic feet of logs. The policy of the government is to increase the
amount felled year by year, but the market for timber has been depressed
for some time past and has been very hard hit by the recent fall in prices.
Competition from Saghalin and Hokkaido in Japan and the Russian
Maritime Provinces is severe, and timber from these parts can be laid down
more cheaply at such ports as Gensan, Fusan and Chemulpo than it can be
brought from Shingishu.
For big size timber for use in building (beams, etc.) Corea imports Douglas
fir and Oregon pine as Yalu timber averages only about 1-1} feet in diameter.
This market is depressed, but has held its own as filling a need that cannot be
filled elsewhere.

Taiwan Important Producer of Gasoline.

Taiwan is the principal source in the Japanese Empire for the domestic production of gasoline. As has been pointed out in previous reports from this office the Japan Oil Company Ltd., which is closely affiliated with the government, has exclusive rights in Taiwan for drilling prospecting and refining of oil, with the exception of certain large areas in the southern part of the island, which are reserved for the Japanese navy.

During the fiscal year 1931-1932 a government subsidy of yen 100,000 was granted to the Japan Oil Company for drilling operations in Taiwan, and the subsidy for the 1932-1933 fiscal year is set at yen 70,000.

The Japan Oil Company has tracts totalling some 22,000 acres in which development has been and is now going on. There are three well defined oil and/or gas fields in Taiwan, in which production has been proved. These are the Kinsui and Shukkoko fields in Shinkiku province and the Gyunikuki field in Tainan province. Production at Kinsui and Shukkoko is not petroleum but natural gas, while the Shukkoko field produces small amounts of crude petroleum.

Up to the present principal activity has been centered in the Kinsui field, and it is here that a gasoline topping plant has been erected. This plant was purchased in the United States. Up to the present this topping plant utilizing the natural gas from the Kinsui field has accounted for approximately 90 per cent of the gasoline produced in Taiwan. A small refinery operating on the cracking principle, located at the Shikkoko field produces the remaining 10 per cent.

## New Gas Well at Kinsui.

On February 27, 1932 well No. 12 at Kinsui came in, flowing at a rate of 200,000,000 cubic feet per day. This well, which had reached a depth of 1050 meters had been shut in and the sudden blowing off of gas, said to be from the sixth layer, has greatly increased the production at the Kinsui field. Wells Nos. 8 and 10 are also steady gas producers, and from these wells approximately 25,000 gallons of gasoline is produced daily. Well No. 12 after spouting at its initial rate for several days has now settled to a steady production of 40,000,000 to 50,000,000 cubic feet.

Additional equipment will shortly be erected, which will double the present capacity of the plant.

## Gyunikuki Field.

Operations at the Gyunikuki field in Tainan province have recently achieved importance. Three wells have been drilling there since early in 1931, and well No. 3, at which drilling started in July 1931, came in with a great rush of gas on April 9, 1932 at a depth of 1354 meters. Estimated flow of gas in the first 24 hours was 150,000,000 cubic feet. Spouting at this rate continued for several days, but on April 14th the well became clogged with mud and water and ceased to flow. The stoppage of this well was regarded as a fortunate circumstance by officials of the Japan Oil Company, as the gas was running too waste, and as yet there is no machinery to handle or take care of the gas.

Although the Gyunikuki field had been proved before, the coming in of well No. 3 in such quantity shows that this

**Gasoline Production Increased Threefold in 1931 Over 1930.**

Recent years have seen a steady increase in Taiwan's production of gasoline, but 1931 was by far the best year, and production in 1932 will probably exceed the 1931 figures.

**Ratio of Natural Gas to Gallon of Gasoline.**

From 2400 to 2500 cubic feet of natural gas is used in the manufacture of one American gallon of gasoline. A certain proportion of imported crude gasoline is mixed with the local gasoline in the process of manufacture.

**Carbon Black.**

The manufacture of carbon black from natural gas is assuming increasing importance in Taiwan. Carbon black is used in Japan in the rubber industry and in manufacturing paints and inks. It is stated that carbon black to the extent of yen 1,300,000 annually is imported into Japan for industrial purposes, most of which has come in the past from the United States.

In 1931 a plant for the manufacture of carbon black from natural gas was purchased in the United States, and was erected at the Kinsui field of the Japan Oil Company. An American engineer supervised the erection of the machinery.

**Development of Taiwan Oil Industry Will Affect Foreign Companies.**

At present besides the Japan Oil Company there are three foreign companies engaged in the oil business here. They are the Rising Sun Petroleum Company, a unit of the Royal Dutch Shell group, and two *American companies, one engaged in selling all classes of petroleum products, the other concentrating largely on lubricating oils and greases.

As the Japan Oil Company now produces more than enough gasoline to supply the local market, it is probable that the foreign companies will have an increasingly difficult time in meet this competition.

Reference is made to the report from this office dated April 20, 1932, entitled "Recent Developments in the Taiwan Petroleum Industry", file No. 863.6. From this report which showed a great increase in gasoline production in 1931 over the previous year, it was predicted that 1932 production would be still greater than in 1931, especially in view of the gas wells at the Kinsui field, which were said to be flowing at a phenomenal rate, according to glowing reports in the local press.

This prediction has not been fulfilled, and gasoline production for the first six months of 1932 by the Japan (Nippon) Oil Company, the sole producer in Formosa, is running behind the similar period for 1931. The following comparative table shows the results of production for the first half of the current year as compared to the first six months of 1931. Figures are in American gallons.

|   | January-June 1932 | January-June 1931  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  Crude Oil | 705,900 | 1,119,850  |
|  Kerosene | 194,210 | 439,000  |
|  Gasoline | 207,800 | 339,200  |
|  Gasoline - from natural gas | 3,737,000 | 4,003,500  |

The first three items in the table above, refer to production from the Shukkoko field, which produces both crude oil and natural gas, while the last item shows the production of gasoline from the Kinsui field, which is entirely from natural gas. It will be noted that no production is reported from the Gyunikuki field, there being as yet no refining equipment installed there.

The production figures in this report and in the previous report, to which reference is made, are believed to be quite accurate, within one to three per cent of actual

=== Page 22 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

actual production, and were obtained from the same source.

Instead of an increase in gasoline production for the first six months of the current year over the corresponding period in 1931, which was to be expected, from the reports of new natural gas gushers, in the Kinsui and Gyunikiki fileds given wide publicity in the local newspapers in February, March and April of 1932, gasoline production fell off by almost 400,000 gallons or 9.2 per cent.

Crude oil and kerosene from the Shukkoku field also suffered a severe decline, indicating that this field has past its production peak, and is declining.

**Published Reports Exaggerated.**

In view of the actual decline in production, in spite of the optimistic reports of new gushers, an impartial observer is led to the belief that the published reports were grossly exaggerated, and that the gushers soon played out, as nothing further has been heard of them. It is possible that the reports were published at the instance of the Japan Oil Company with a view to improving the market price of the shares before the annual meeting late in April. If this is so, the improvement did not take place, or, at least was of momentary duration, as the company's shares which stood at ¥48.90 on March 1, 1932 had declined to ¥44.60 on April 26, 1932. However this explanation is suggested as a possible reason for the reports, as it is understood that such methods are not unknown in Japan.

During the first six months of 1932, a total of 452,400 gallons of gasoline were imported from Japan for mixing

**Local Consumption Slightly Increased.**

Taiwan's consumption of gasoline increased about 9 per

are respectively 2,150,000 gallons as against 1,980,000

**Conclusion.**

It is apparent from the figures quoted that gasoline
production is not keeping pace with the year 1931, and
that, therefore, the estimates made in the previous report
as to production for 1932 will have to be revised downward.
Instead of 10,000,000 gallons as previously estimated it
is now probable that production will not exceed 8,000,000
gallons.

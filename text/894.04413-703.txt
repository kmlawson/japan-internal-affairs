person against an imminent and illegal entry shall not be punished*. As a result of the obscurity of the phraseology of this paragraph not only was one uncertain of what measures might be taken within the law against a trespasser endangering life, person, or chastity but in the event of the wounding or killing of the intruder one was liable to criminal legal procedure and to subsequent punishment unless it could be proved that at the time of the act an actual attack was in progress. The new law definitely legalizes the killing or injuring of an intruder if one's own or another person's life, person, or chastity is threatened in any of the following three instances:

1. In endeavoring to prevent the commission of theft or to recover stolen goods;
2. In endeavoring to prevent entry;
3. In endeavoring to repulse a person or persons who have trespassed without lawful excuse.*

The danger, furthermore, need be neither present nor actual provided that the killing or injuring of the trespasser is the result of fear, fright, excitement, or panic. The law also includes certain penalties with respect to punishment of habitual offenders in theft or burglary.

The significance of this law lies not so much in the actual provisions as in the fact that it is yet another indication of the persistent growth of the rights of the individual in Japan, rights which are not yet the equivalent of those of, say, a citizen of the United States.

*Details of these three points are included in the enclosed translation.

Since the Restoration (1868), which was shortly followed by the legal abolition of feudalism, there has occurred a steady increase of the rights of the individual at a sacrifice either of the rights of the State, as in this instance, or at a sacrifice of the rights of the family. Formerly the individual was no more than an unimportant subdivision of the family and either the family or the State (usually in feudal times the clan) acted in important matters for him. The introduction of Western legal procedure in the eighties of the last century enormously enhanced the rights of the individual, the inevitable result of the superimposition of a legal system borrowed from countries where the practice of individual responsibility was already highly developed. Similarly the disintegration of the family system, chiefly due to the development of industrialism, added to their number. The present law delegates to the individual even further rights of action which have heretofore been reserved to the State, namely, the punishment on one's own property of a dangerous trespasser. Nor has this gradual elimination of feudal vestiges come to an end. There is at present under discussion a revision of the civil code which, if effected, will further weaken the family system. At present the head of a family has the right to transmit his entire estate, with the exception of levies by the State, to his successor. The contemplated alteration would require by law the transmission of certain fixed proportions to certain other members of the family regardless of the will of the testator.

The chief argument advanced against the new

new criminal law is that it will result in abuse, especially to the detriment of labor in dispute with capital. In the case of labor or farmer-tenant disputes in Japan it is not infrequent that a delegation of malcontents forces its way into the premises of its employer to present its grievances. Under the new law "in endeavoring to repulse persons who have trespassed . . . without lawful excuse or those who do not withdraw . . . although requested to do so" the trespasser may be injured or killed without punishment to the perpetrator in case of fear, fright, excitement, or panic, even if there is "no present or actual danger to one's own or another person's life, person or chastity". Opponents claim that the law encourages capitalists to violence and protects them from legal consequences. This view is held not only by some members of the opposition parties but also by certain reputable lawyers. It has further been reliably reported that officials of the Social Bureau of the Department of Home Affairs, which is keenly interested in the welfare of laborers, hold a similar view and have expressed regret that the Department of Justice did not seek its opinion when drafting the bill. The inconclusive reply of the Minister of Justice, proponent of the law, to such claims was that the law applies, regardless of the origin of the affair, when "life, person, or chastity" is threatened. In short, the principal objection to the law seems to be that its scope may be wider than the framers of the law intended.

In addition to clarifying the right of defence against

against dangerous trespassers, the new law sets forth for the first time penalties for habitual offenders more severe than those for first offenders. Theft was (and is) punishable by imprisonment for not more than ten years: under the new law an habitual offender shall be imprisoned for not less than three years. Burglary was (and is) punishable by imprisonment of more than five years: under the new law an habitual offender shall be imprisoned for not less than seven years. (The term "habitual offender" is defined with some complexity in Article 3 of the enclosure to this despatch.) A burglar who injures a person or who violates a woman is subject to imprisonment of more than seven years: under the new law an habitual offender who commits either of these offences shall be sentenced to imprisonment for not less than ten years.

In an explanatory address, following the introduction of the bill to the House of Peers, the Minister of Justice urged the necessity of these more severe sentences because of the increase of theft and burglary by offenders who have already served prison terms for the same offences. Statistics published by the Department of Justice indicate that the authorities have reason to view the situation with some gravity. At the end of 1927* of the 37,990 persons serving sentences in prisons in Japan 21,188 (or 55.7 per cent.) had been committed for theft and 2,545 (6.7 per cent.) for burglary. Although there are no figures covering habitual offenders in theft and burglary, the number of persons committed to prison each year who have served previous sentences is steadily increasing.

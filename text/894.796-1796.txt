# Problem

To determine the course of action most likely to obtain greatest possible support in FEC for a policy that would permit SCAP to authorize such civil aviation services for Japan as may be required in the interests of the objectives of the occupation.

# Background

The basic Post-Surrender Policy for Japan provided: "Japan is not to have any * * * air force * * * or any civil aviation * *," and placed "a ban upon facilities for production or repair of * * all forms of aircraft." The Directive for Reduction of Japan's War Potential provides (paragraph 9A(1)): "The following should be prohibited in Japan: * * The development, manufacture or assembly of civil aircraft; and the participation by the Japanese Government or Japanese nationals in the ownership or airborne operation of civil aircraft."

In July 1947, the United States introduced in the FEC a series of papers based upon policy approved by ACC and SWNCC, in substance as follows:

1. Japanese nationals to be forbidden to engage in flight operations; but SCAP to be permitted to authorize civil air carriers to utilize Japanese nationals in ground facilities and services, subject to security screening of job and individual.

2. A separate internal air service within Japan operated by Japanese or by non-Japanese to be forbidden.

3. Civil air carriers of occupying nations to be permitted by SCAP to establish commercial service on through routes authorized by their governments, with SCAP being empowered to permit them to carry cabotage traffic within Japan, if SCAP determine that such would be

In the Spring of 1948, Commission 2 approved FEC 245/16, a modification of the US position, with France, the USSR, the Philippines and the US abstaining, the former three making a general reservation, and with China demanding tighter controls on Japanese participation. FEC 245/16 was in substance similar to the original US proposal.

After Steering Committee approval, and while the matter was in the Commission itself, with USSR amendments in line with the foregoing still pending, the US proposed a new policy, approved by ACC and SWNCC. This proposal embodied the following major changes:

1. Authorization of Japanese participation in non-Japanese flight operations in the interests of the objectives of the occupation, if no security threat exists.
2. Authority to SCAP to authorize internal air services on the sixty-day reference basis above referred to.
3. International services to be operated by any UK member state, consistent with security and within the limits of the availability of facilities.

4. SCAP to control all ground facilities and technical regulations.

This new proposal was referred back to Commission 2 in FEC. The Commonwealth countries raised a number of questions as to:
(a) Just what flight operations Japanese nationals might participate in; (b) What type of internal service was thought desirable by the US. New Zealand proposed that Japan should run an internal service; that services should be authorized on the basis of benefit to all UN member states rather than any particular state; and that no sixty-day rule was needed in view of SCAP's interim powers. Attempts at clarification have been made, but it has become apparent that specific details of the desired operation alone would meet the desires of the Commonwealth countries with respect to their request for clarification.

A query was therefore sent to SCAP as to his position on internal air services. His response is attached.

Discussion

It is believed desirable, in view of the fact that SCAP's interim powers are at present adequate to authorize basically essential international air services, not to proceed further in FEC until the US is able to clearly state its concept with respect to civil air services needed for Japan. Further discussion in FEC prior to the preparation of such material would appear to be potentially prejudicial.

Present FEC directives forbid the Japanese to have control of any civil aviation entity. To date, the US has not proposed a change in that policy. However, this matter should be thoroughly reviewed from the security point of view to see if such policy should be changed in light of the needs of the occupation.

A further detailed study as to the existence of actual need for internal air service within Japan on a more complete and documented basis than that in SCAP's referenced cable should be undertaken immediately. In view of the military character of the occupation, in view of the close relation to US civil aviation policy, and in view of the political questions inherent in this problem, primary interest appears to lie in LEE, CAB and the Department, although CAB is also interested. These agencies are all members of the ACC, which has previously studied the Japanese civil aviation question, and it therefore is desirable to further explore this point in ACC.

## Recommendations

The following action is recommended and will be pursued if you agree:

1. Inform the Commonwealth countries on an informal, oral and secret basis, that a preliminary study by SCAP has indicated that an internal air service in Japan would, of course, supplement present transportation facilities in Japan, would meet the need for improved mail, parcel post and light weight cargo movement, would provide speedy travel for those persons connected with businesses dealing with the development of export and production goods, would provide a means of air travel for tourists and Japanese businessmen, and would aid the development of foreign trade by letting foreign representatives have access to various areas inconveniently reached by surface travel; that a further and more detailed study is contemplated by the United States; that the pursuit of such a study would be facilitated greatly if the Commonwealth countries could informally and confidentially supply the U.S. with their thoughts with respect to this problem, and particularly the following issues:

a. The propriety of establishing an internal civil air service for Japan operated by (1) non-Japanese (2) Japanese.

b. The composition of the airline or airlines best designed to achieve this purpose.

c. Recommended methods for overcoming obstacles to the achievement of this purpose contained in present FEC policy.

2. Submit the problem to the Air Coordinating Committee for the pursuit of a study and development of a policy with respect to civil aviation in Japan, and specifically the following questions:

a. Precisely what internal air services for Japan are required to support the economic objectives of the occupation.

b. What character of airline or airlines should render services toward that end, taking into consideration the economics of such an operation, the economic incidence of such an operation upon the Japanese economy, and the financial support needed for any such service.

c. To what extent should there be Japanese participation in flight operations of any such entity or entities.

d. What considerations of strategy and national policy, other than economic, should influence a policy determination of whether or not Japan should be permitted internal civil aviation.

3. Make available to the Air Coordinating Committee the views of the Commonwealth countries as they may be received by the Department with a view to developing a United States position which would take into account the views of those interested countries.

SUBJECT: Civil Aviation Policy in Japan

This is a subject on which we need your advice as an
old aviation "expert".

The question of civil aviation in Japan is an old chestnut
to which you may have been exposed when the matter was
first considered several years ago. The item has been before
a FEC Subcommittee in various forms, and we should decide
whether we are going to push for a FEC policy on this matter
or let it drop. There appears to be no pressure from the
field for the establishment for an internal air service other
than the brief reply set forth in the attached cable from
SCAP which states in general that it would be a nice idea.

It is suggested that you read the discussion and recommendations
(page 4-6) of the attached memorandum prepared by
Mr. Wolf of AV. This memorandum points out all the factors
involved in establishing internal aviation service in Japan and
comes up a recommendation that after informally obtaining
the views of the Commonwealth countries toward the desirability
and type of civil air service which might be established in
Japan, the Department should submit the whole problem to the
Air Coordinating Committee for study and development of a
coordinated U.S. Government position which might then be
pressed in the FEC. Mr. Wolf's view is that our thinking has
changed since the matter was originally introduced in the FEC
two years ago, and that the logical place to coordinate the
governmental policy is the ACC. Mr. Amrine, Mr. Barnett, and
myself discussed this matter with Mr. Wolf several times this
summer and were in general agreement with this recommendation.

It seems to me that a new factor has been introduced by
the prospect of a Japanese conference and treaty. In view
of this possibility it is questionable whether it is worth the
fight which we would have to put up in the FEC to try to obtain
approval. Favorable action by the Commission is questionable
as it involves a reversal of existing policy to allow the Japanese
to engage in civil aviation. The Commonwealth countries are
opposing any move to give the Japanese more responsibility,
stating that these matters should be considered at a peace conference.
Mr. Hamilton is also of the opinion that we should
consider the imminence of a peace treaty in relation to this
question.

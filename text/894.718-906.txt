AMERICAN CONSULATE
Yokohama, Japan, May 7, 1936.

Subject: J.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State
Washington

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to my despatches Nos. 188 and 202 of March 19 and April 20, 1936, regarding the action of the Japanese authorities in delivering and censoring correspondence and printed letter arriving from abroad through the mails and to enclose herewith, as of possible interest, a copy of a letter dated May 2, 1936, to Reverend Harold Ochene of Yokohama which sets forth to some extent the attitude of the Japanese authorities. The letter is in answer to one written by Mr. Ochene on April 25, 1936, a copy of which was enclosed with my despatch No. 202 of April 28, 1936, mentioned above.

Very respectfully yours,

Richard F. Boyce,
American Consul.

Enclosure:

THE YOKOHAMA CITY OFFICE
ST. LOUIS, MO.

YOKOHAMA, the 2nd May, 1936.

Harold J. Schenck, Esq.,
Union Church,
66-8, Bluff,
Yokohama.

My dear Mr. Schenck:-

The receipt is acknowledged of your letter of the 25th of April last containing your well-meant criticism about our censoring of American magazines. In reply, I regret to have to say that I am not in a position to make the desired public statement of policy that will throw light on the matter of censoring and confiscating foreign mails in general. So your letter has been referred to the Director General of Posts, Tokio, for his subsequent disposal. In this connection, I think I can say this much.

1. In censoring magazines and news-papers, no distinction is made between the countries of their issue or between the languages used, all periodicals, domestic as well as foreign, being censored on absolutely equal principle.

2. The

2. The detention or confiscation of periodicals solely depends on their nature and contents, the nationality nor personality of the addressees not being taken into account, with the result of frequent seizure of magazines addressed to Imperial Government authorities.

3. It is hardly necessary to say that Japanese Government is not afraid of fair criticism based on the fact. That they fear, I presume, are articles founded on erroneous or uncertain information.

4. As you know very well, Japan is now going through the epoch when the government must do its utmost for the clarification and upholding of the national polity so that they can not take too severe and exacting measures in dealing with magazine or news-paper articles or pictures liable to lèse-majesté.

5. We can not help asking foreign residents for the same patience as the Japanese people in the matter of censoring of mails.

6. As to your reasonable suggestion to release publications after removing their objectionable pages, I am afraid it is not feasible from practical view point, as it imposes too much labour on the short-handed Direction General of Posts. It is, however, thought that the central authorities will adopt your view unless it entails too much

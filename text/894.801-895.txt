Definite information has recently become available as to the effective date of the Law for the Safety of Vessels, passed by the Imperial Japanese Diet as Law No. 11 of March 14, 1933 but not placed into operation until March 1, 1934. The law was evolved especially with a view to Japan's signature of the International Load Line Convention signed at London on July 5, 1930 and occasion was taken to revise generally the previous legislation affecting the inspection of vessels in the Japanese merchant marine. There are no special features of the new law which require lengthy explanation and the "unofficial" translation prepared by the Mercantile Marine Bureau of the Department of Communications is given as an enclosure to the report. Where appropriate, the following commentary has been made with a view to bringing out the special significance of certain provisions of the law.

Article 4.

The provisions of this article are materially weakened by the exception made in the last paragraph. It has become apparent that the Minister of Communications has not enforced the law as regards passenger vessels regularly trading on the Inland Sea, in spite of the example of the serious disaster which occurred last October when the Yashima Maru sank off Kobe with a loss of 65 lives. Reference is made to the Wireless Ship Act of the United States of June 24, 1910 and July 23, 1912.

## Article 8.
At the present time most of this inspection is being done by Lloyd's Register of Shipping, which is organized under the laws of Japan and is regarded as "one of the classification societies of Japan". The other classification society in Japan is the Teikoku Kaiji Kyokai or Imperial Marine Association, which is slowly encroaching upon the field in which Lloyd's was long supreme. Both societies classify Japanese ships according to the rules of the British Board of Trade. (1) The distinguished Japan Committee of Lloyd's Register of Shipping for the current year bears a pronounced Mitsubishi flavor, most of the members being either from Mitsubishi subsidiaries or companies controlled by the giant firm although ostensibly under independent management; while the rival Japanese classification society seems to be under the wing of the second great financial dynasty of Japan, the House of Mitsui.

It should be observed that the provisions of Article 8 do not apply to passenger vessels.

## Article 9.
Japan has ratified the International Load Line Convention and special regulations for the enforcement of the provisions of that treaty were made effective by ordinance as of March 1, 1934. The load line rules followed by the two Japanese classification societies are therefore uniform with those established by the Convention. (2)

## Article 13.
It is interesting to note the analogy between this provision and the similar statutes in the United States: R.S. 4556-45663. Under the Japanese law, however, in the case of a vessel with a crew of more than twenty the complaint need be presented by only ten men, whereas the American statute provides that it must be made by either the first and second officers under the master or by a majority of the crew. This provision of the Law for the Safety of Vessels is one for which the Japan Seamen's Union has long campaigned and will certainly be invoked under direction of the Union should the need arise.

## Article 14.
Thus, by paragraph 3 all American vessels in Japanese territorial waters are amenable to the provisions of the Law for the Safety of Vessels.

## Article 15.
The provisions of Article 15 are identical with those of R.S. 4400 of the Navigation Laws of the United States. Therefore the Federal Steamboat Inspection Certificates of American vessels are valid in Japanese waters.

## Article 16.
It is noteworthy that, contrary to American constitutional interpretation, a treaty is here given specific precedence over a statute, irrespective of the date of either.

The Law for the Safety of Vessels was promulgated by Imperial Ordinance No. 13 of January, 1934, effective from March 1, 1934. # Law for the Safety of Vessels

Art. 1. No Japanese vessel shall be employed in navigation unless such measures are taken on board in accordance with the present Law as are necessary for maintaining the vessel in a sea-worthy state and for ensuring the safety of human life.

Art. 2. With regard to matters falling under the following Heads, the necessary measures shall be taken on board each vessel in accordance with the provisions of the relevant Ordinance:

1. Hull.
2. Machinery and boilers.
3. Sails and rigging.
4. Draining equipment.
5. Steering, mooring and anchor gear.
6. Life-saving and fire-extinguishing appliances.
7. Accommodation.
8. Sanitary appliances.
9. Nautical instruments.
10. Arrangements for the stowage of dangerous goods and other special descriptions of cargo.
11. Appliances for loading and unloading and other kindred operations.
12. Electric installations.
13. In addition to those matters falling under the preceding Heads, such other matters as may be specially designated by the competent Minister of State.

The provisions of the preceding Paragraph shall not apply to the vessels mentioned below:

1. Vessels of less than 5 tons gross.

=== Page 10 === (confidence 0.99)
2. Vessels propelled by oars and also such vessels as may be specially designated by the competent Minister of State.

Art. 3. Vessels navigated in “Ocean-going Areas” and vessels of not less than 150 tons gross navigated in “Major Coasting Areas” shall be marked with their load-lines in accordance with the provisions of the relevant Ordinance: provided, however, that this provision shall not apply to vessels solely employed in fishing, towing, salvage or dredging, or for hydrographic purposes, nor to such vessels as the competent Minister of State may consider as exhibiting no particular necessity to have their load-lines marked.

Art. 4. The vessels mentioned below must be equipped with wireless telegraphic apparatus in accordance with the Wireless Telegraphy Law:

1. Vessels of not less than 1,600 tons gross navigated in “Ocean-going” or “Major Coasting” Areas.

2. Passenger vessels (viz., vessels the prescribed number of whose passengers exceeds 12) navigated in “Ocean-going” or “Major Coasting” Areas.

3. Fishing vessels of not less than 100 tons gross.

Even in the case of vessels which require to be equipped with wireless telegraphic apparatus in accordance with the provisions of the preceding Paragraph, such apparatus need not be installed, if, having regard to the object of the voyage or other circumstances, the competent Minister of State considers that this exemption from the said requirement is imperatively imposed by circumstances or that there is no necessity for the installation of the said apparatus.

Art. 5. All shipowners shall, in accordance with the provisions

=== Page 11 === (confidence 0.99)
of the relevant Ordinance, submit their vessels for inspection in accordance with the subjoined classification, in respect of matters falling under each Head of Paragraph 1 of Article 2 hereof in the case of vessels to which the provisions of the said Paragraph apply, in respect of load-lines in the case of the vessels mentioned in Article 3 hereof and in respect of wireless telegraphic apparatus in the case of the vessels mentioned in Article 4 hereof:

1. An inspection in close detail, which is effected before a vessel is for the first time employed in navigation and upon the expiration of the period of validity established in Article 10 hereof (hereinafter referred to as “Periodical Inspection”).

2. Simple inspection, which is effected between one periodical inspection and another, at a time fixed by the relevant Ordinance (hereinafter referred to as “Intermediate Inspection”).

3. That inspection which is effected as occasion requires before a vessel is employed for a special purpose (hereinafter referred to as “Inspection of Vessels Employed for Special Purposes”).

4. That inspection which is effected if and when it is considered specially necessary by the competent Minister of State, in cases other than those falling under the preceding Heads (hereinafter referred to as “Occasional Inspection”).

The competent Minister of State may, in accordance with the provisions of the relevant Ordinance, grant exemption from Intermediate Inspection.

I. Ship Building Subsidies
The Ship Construction Law effective
October 1, 1932, appropriated 11,000,000 yen
for the purpose of removing 400,000 gross
tons of obsolete ships from Japanese registry
and providing 200,000 gross tons of new
vessels. The new vessels are to be cargo
ships of not less than 4,000 gross tons and
13½ knots speed. The subsidy is ¥ 45 per ton
for ships of less than 14 knots, ¥ 46 per ton
for ships of 14 knots; it increases by ¥ 1
per additional one-half knot to ¥ 54 per ton
for 18 knots and over. The new law is
administered by the Ministry of Communica-
tions, with the assistance of a "Shipping
Standard Improvement Association", and in-
cludes representatives of the government,
the ship-owners and ship-builders. The quota
of tonnage to be constructed in 1932 and
1933 has been oversubscribed. The new ships
when completed will be among the fastest
freighters in the world.

IV. Future Subsidies
Japan is a firm believer in the value of ship subsidies as a means of maintaining an efficient merchant marine.

Appendix 1 is an editorial from the OSAKA MAINICHI SHIMBUN which shows that Japan cannot agree to the British proposals at the World Economic Conference for (1) abolition or reduction of subsidies and (2) a joint tie-up of rule ships at a uniform percentage of global tonnage of each nation.

Supervising Consul General Garrels, at Tokyo, appends the subsidies schedule for the years 1934 to 1938 which is to be submitted to the next session of the Imperial Diet. The amounts to be granted during the next few years do not materially differ from the subsidies granted at the present time.

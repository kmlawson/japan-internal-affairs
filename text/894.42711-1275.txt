I INTRODUCTION
Reference is respectfully invited to the reports
referred to upon the title page. The reports provide the
Department with much detail the basic facts concerning
the Americans of Japanese race living in Japan.
The present report is intended to present additional
data, with particular reference to the trend of the
social development of the "Nisei" community in Japan and
the increasing interest which the Japanese Government is
taking in the "Nisei" both in Japan and in the United
States as instruments of spreading Japanese propaganda,
arousing American sympathy for and combating American
sentiment against Japanese activities in Asia.
For purposes of convenience, the Americans of Japanese
race frequently will be referred to in this report as "Nisei".
The term is used by this group of Americans in describing
themselves, for lack of a better short description. Its
literal translation is simply "second generation", but its
meaning as applied to Americans of Japanese race is very
generally used.

II
STATISTICS
There is still no accurate census of American citizens
of Japanese race residing in Japan. The number registered
as known at the American Consulates in Japan is given as
4,107 on January 1, 1939. There is very little change in
the total number actually residing in Japan from year to
year, although constant arrivals and departures give a
transient character to one section of the group.
It is important to remember that the "Nisei" in Japan
are of two distinct groups. The larger group, of whom no
accurate estimate has ever been made, consists of those who
were brought to Japan at an early age, who have no American
education and are essentially Japanese in everything but
birth. They live mostly in the Kobe consular district and
have been estimated at from 25,000 to 100,000 in number.
They have not shown any marked tendency to return to the
United States. Unless and until they do return to the
United States in appreciable numbers they present no pro-
blem and can be ignored. They will be referred to as Group
A "Nisei".
The smaller group is estimated at about 1,500, and is
congregated mostly in Tokyo. The group consists of those
Americans of Japunese race who have lived in the United
States long enough to acquire through American schools and
American surroundings a cefinite American character. Most
of them have completed their grammar school education in
the United States. Many of them have completed American
high schools. Some have graduated from American colleges.
They will be referred to as Group B "Nisei".

III
REASONS FOR CONTINUED EXISTENCE
OF "NISEI" GROUPS IN JAPAN
Group A
This group depends largely upon its immediate
family relatives in Japan for economic support. The
parents have permanently abandoned their United States
domicile and wish the children to remain in Japan at
least until they complete their education. This results
in complete absorption within the Japanese community.
There seems to be little or no desire on the part of any
substantial portion of the individuals in Group A to return
to the United States. Even though they are American
citizens they do not seem to have inherited the desire
which took their parents to the United States a generation
ago. There is no indication that large numbers of them
will even make any attempt to retain their merican citizen-
ship. Anti-Japanese sentiment and the continued unemploy-
ment prevailing in the United States, coupled with family
ties and heavy industrial employment in Japan are believed
to account for the continued residence of Group A in Japan.
Group B
As indicated on pages 5 and 6 of the NISEI, Enclosure
#1 herewith, most of this group come to Japan for study.
The student section of Group B is a constantly changing
one, the average residence in Japan for those who do not
seek permanent residence here probably being under four
years. Many return to the United States in two years or
less.
There

- 5 -
There is a growing number of Group B "Nisei" who
are establishing permanent domicile in Japan. Most of
them came to Japan for purposes of study and have since
found satisfactory employment.
A common statement of Group B "Nisei" who are employ-
ed in Japan, in reference to their continued residence
here, is that due to the anti-Japanese prejudice in the
United States, the economic future for them in America is
too limited. When it is possible for them to obtain
employment in Japan, their promotion is limited only by
their ability. They are willing to work for lower salaries
than similar work would bring them in the United States,
and are willing to adjust themselves to a semi-Japanese
standard of living in return for escape from alleged anti-
Japanese dscrimination in the United States.
As a matter of fact, the present openings for Group B
"Nisei" in business firms and Government agencies that deal
with foreigners and provide good employment, at salaries
which are often ligher than for Japanese in similar positions,
are sufficient for only a limited number of persons. The
section entitled "Vocation", pages 16 to 28 of Enclosure
#1, describes this situation. It is doubtful if the
number of "Nisei" obtaining satisfactory employment in
Japan can increase rapidly or materially.
There is a
point in their adjustment to life in Japan beyond which
life in America, in spite of anti-Japanese handicaps, is
more attractive than the Japanese standard of living.
Incidentally on June 9, 1939, a "Nisei", Tosuke
Yamazaki, delivered an address on the regular short-wave
broadcast to the United States on "Nisei Opportunities in

IV
JAPANESE GOVERNMENT INTEREST
IN THE "NISEI"
1. Purpose of the Japanese Government's Interest
As Group A "Nisei" are absorbed in the Japanese com-
munity, the Japanese Government does not di tinguish
between them and Japanese.
As indicated on page 6 of Enclosure #1, most of the
Group B "Nisei" now in Japan arrived since 1933. Their
importance as a group was first noticed in 1934 by a few
American missionary teachers and by the America-Japan
Society in Tokyo. The first important effort of the
America-Japan Society on behalf of the Tokyo "Nisei" took
the form of a Lincoln's Birthday party on one floor of
Mitsukoshi Department Store on February 12, 1935. The
attention of the Japanese Foreign Office and otler Japa-
nese officials was effectively drawn to the "Nisei" by
that affair.
Since 1935 the Japanese authorities have been giving
more attention to cultivating the Group B "Nisei" with a
view to using them to spread pro-Japanese propaganda in
the United States. Their efforts have increased in
intensity since the outbreak of the hostilities in China.
The Japanese authorities are still suspicious of the
loyalty and "sincerity" of Group B "Nisei". Highly-placed
Japanese civilians in the America-Japan Society were at
first reluctant to associate with the sons and daughters
of low-born Japanese emigrants. On their side, the Group
B "Nisei" were homesick, uncomfortable and sensitive to
the criticism and unfriendliness of the Japanese.
Enlightened individuals in the Japanese Government,
the International Cultural Relations Society, and in the
Japan-

=== Page 10 === (confidence 0.99)

Japan-America Society recognized the fact that if the
Group B "Nisei" could be made into active agents of pro-
Japanese propaganda in the United States, it would be worth
a good deal to Japan. Such pro-Japanese agents would be
expected to work first of all upon the Japanese-American
communities in the United States, and if those large groups,
on the Hawaiian Islands and on the Pacific Coast, could be
developed into strong pro-Japanese centers, they would be
able to develop more pro-Japanese sentiment in the United
States in general. In the second place, the Group B "Nisei"
are in a position to advise Japanese on American psychology
and to contact other Americans directly. This is important
to the Japanese agencies which have contact with Americans.
"Nisei" are employed in the Japan-America Society, the
International Cultural Relations Society, by Japanese news-
papers, and by the Japanese Government radio broadcasting
company to give an American flavor and appeal to the Japa-
nese propaganda efforts.

2. Propagandizing "Nisei" Clubs
The Japanese Government and the Japan-American Society
and the International Cultural Relations Society have been
working steadily upon the Group B "Nisei" during the past
four years. For purposes of better surveillance the "Nisei"
have been encouraged to organize clubs. The membership,
purposes

=== Page 12 === (confidence 0.99)
-10-
purposes and activities of the clubs are carefully watched
by the police. "Nisei" are of course also carefully
watched by the police outside their club activities, as
are other foreigners. The "Nisei" clubs are encouraged
in every way to pursue the study of Japanese culture.
Along with culture studies the "Nisei" are given as
much Japanese propaganda as they will absorb.
On pages 50-52 of Enclosure #1 is given a list of
the "Nisei" clubs. The emphasis upon Japan-America
friendship and the study of Japanese culture is important.
The Japanese have stressed this so much that the "Nisei"
occasionally complain of it. The social intercourse which
the clubs provide is more important from the "Nisei"
point of view.
3. Propagandizing "Nisei" Students
The Japanese authorities are of course trying to
influence the Group B "Nisei" through the schools which
most of them come to Japan to attend.
In the beginnine,
"Nisei" had to attend regular Japanese institutions and
no special arrangements for them were available. In
most regular Japanese schools and colleges there are still
no special facilities for them. The Section "Education",
on pages 8-16 and pages 53-55 of Enclosure #1 give detailed
information on "Nisei" education in Japan.
It was found advisable, from the Japanese point of
view, to "prepare" "Nisei" for their studies in regular
Japanese schools. Actually, aside from political con-
siderations a preparation in spoken and written Japanese
is essential for the "Nisei". Additional culture courses
are also helpful. But the Japanese authorities have
found

found that this preparatory period provides an excellent
opportunity for heavy doses of propaganda. The only
example of this I have seen is a translation of the lecture
given by Professor S. Natori, Vice President of Waseda
International Institute, on "The Ideals of the Second-
Generation Japanese and the Japanese Spirit".
A copy of
this is enclosed (Enclosure #3).
The waseda International Institute is a special part
of Waseda University which was set up in September 1935
to prepare Group B "Nisei" boys and girls for entering
regular Japanese educational institutions. The lecture

The Consulate is
reliably informed that a great part of the "instruction"
given the "Nisei" at this institution is pure propaganda.
Similar propaganda is given to "Nisei" at other "prepa-

It is not possible to measure the effect of this
propaganda upon the "Nisei". Most of them return to the
United States and its effect upon them cannot be followed
from here. Unquestionably some of the "Nisei" who remain
in Japan are affected by the propaganda although its
effect depends largely upon the measure of their success
in finding agreeable work with Japanese agencies. "Nisei"
naturally desire friendly relations between the land of
their birth and the land of their parents. Those who are
living in Japan would not be expected to be anti-Japanese.
However, having had an American education they probably
do not absorb propaganda as readily as Japanese and generally
the ones I have heard disseminating pro-Japanese propaganda
are those who are paid to do so. In this connection,
several Americans not of Japanese race have also been

4. "Nisei" as Pro-Japanese Propagandists
Those "Nisei" who are employed by Japanese newspapers,
by the Radio Broadcasting Corporation of Japan are the out-
standing propagandists of their group for Japan. A few
have been unusually successful as journalists and radio
announcers. Some of the men have married Japanese women
and expect to remain in Japan the rest of their lives.
Presumably their sympathies are pro-Japanese. In some
cases they may have dual Japanese and American nationality.
Their American origin and background have made them effective
propagandists since they can present Japanese viewpoints
much more convincingly than a Japanese can present it.
Three "Nisei" news reporters have been sent by the
Japanese authorities with the Japanese troops in China.
One "Nisei" representative of "Nisei" newspapers in Cali-
fornia was reported in the JAPaN TIMES on November 23, 1939
as planning to lecture in the United States for the purpose
of urging "Nisei" on the Pacific Coast to go to China so
they can return to the United States with information to
counteract Chinese propaganda.
The Waseda International Institute sponsored a pro-
paganda magazine, to be issued twice a year, in which
"second generation and foreign students of the Waseda Inter-
national Institute plan to explain Japan's stand in the
far east." The first issue appeared in December 1938.
Professor Natori, the author of Enclosure #3, was one of
the contributors (JAPAN TIMES, December 26, 1936).
On Ootober 31, 1939 "three hundred "nisei" students in
Tokyo appealed to the American people by radio Tuesday
afternoon

Furthermore they are not particularly qualified by
nature or training to engage in propaganda work. As indi-
viduals and as a group they are more concerned with their
everyday problems than with politics.

V "NISEI" PUBLICATIONS
The problems and interests of Americans of Japanese
race living in Japan are largely distinct from the problems
and interests of other American residents. The "Nisei"
feel the need of cooperation and organization among them-
selves, quite apart from the desire of the Japanese
authorities for them to organize to facilitate surveil-
lance and control.
An interesting manifestation of their special problems
and interests is the appearance of publications by and for
the Group B "Nisei".
There is a weekly column in the TOKYO NICHI NICII
with "Nisei" gossip. It is written by "Nisei" members of
the NICHI NICHI staff.
Some attempts were made by "Nisei" during the past
six years to publish a magazine or newspaper devoted to
their interests. None was successful. A new attempt is
being made with PAN-PACIFIC YOUTH which was begun as a
quarterly last summer. Two issues have appeared thus far.
Perusal of those issues gives one the impression that the
"Nisei" group in Japan is very like other groups of young
Americans of college age. There is a lively interest in
their daily problems and very little interest in politics.
Their American background is the most conspicuous aspect
of the group. à copy of issue Number 2, Autumn 1939, is
enclosed herewith.
The "Nisei" students in the Keisen Girls' School in
Tokyo have recently published in book form the results of
a study which they made of "Nisei" in Japan. They were
assisted financially as well as with advice by the Inter-
national Cultural Relations Society of Tokyo. The result
of

PREFACE
who...what...why...
We, the members of the Summer Class of 1939, Keisen
Girls' School, finding ourselves confronted with the same sort
of problems that other Nisei before us have had to face, and
realizing the need for specific and accurate information about
Nisei life in Japan, have undertaken this survey as our gradua-
tion project.
The survey was suggested to us by our principal, Miss
Michi Kawai, an outstanding Christian leader and educator,
who has long been interested in the welfare of the Nisei.
Because of her desire to aid these foreign-born students, she
is now experimenting with a two-year course, known as the
Ryugakusei Department of Keisen Girls' School, which is
especially designed to meet their educational needs. In ap-
preciation of her efforts and those of many other people who
have unselfishly and earnestly sought to help the Nisei solve
their problems, we took up this work. We feel that the time
has come for the Nisei mutually to help each other make the
necessary adjustments and thus bring about lasting solutions
of their own problems.
Realizing that we are inexperienced in this kind of work,
we feel that neither the survey nor this report of it are perfect
by any means. They are in parts ambiguous and in parts in-
adequate, but we hope that the little we have accomplished will
serve as an incentive to others to carry on such work. The
need for this type of work on an even larger scale is clearly
evident and it is the sincere hope of each one of us that our
work, inadequate and incomplete though it is, will be just the
beginning of greater and more comprehensive work to be done
in the near future.
In conclusion, may we express our deepest gratitude and
sincere appreciation to the Kokusai Bunka Shinkokai, the
Japan-America Young People's Federation, the Tokyo Union

FOREWORD
It was toward the end of 1934 that I visited several of
the cities on the Pacific Coast. It was a time when people were
focussing their attention on the Far East. The early Japanese
settlers felt the great necessity of having their children taught
more about Japan, its past and present, which condition the fu-
ture history of the country. It would be, I felt, a mutual benefit
for us in Japan to have some of them come to live with us
and study as students rather than coming merely as tourists.
Since then the school has been exceedingly fortunate in
getting the cream of these young people who are ready to learn
the essential things which have built up Japan as it is now.
Their minds are keen and alert and their adaptability to any new
situation shows their good heritage.
The task of gathering the materials for this pamphlet was
not an easy one. This survey, however, shows the intellectual
training as well as the spirit of cooperation among them. I
hope readers will be generous in judging their work even though
it shows incompleteness and immaturity and give their blessing
to the whole-hearted endeavor of these girls. May I thank
every individual and organization which has aided them to
send out this booklet into the hands of those who are interested
in the welfare of the Nisei at home and abroad.
Principal, Keisen Jogaku-En
(Keisen Girls' School)

THE NISEI PROBLEM AND
THE SURVEY
..."Just what is it all about?"
In the trend of present-day international affairs, the unique
and somewhat difficult position of American and Canadian-born
young people of Japanese parentage (Nisei) is of great signifi-
cance especially in the maintenance of goodwill and peace
among the nations bordering the Pacific Ocean. The Nisei
themselves and those who have taken special interest in their
welfare have been asked, and are being asked, many questions
as to their future. For this reason, many influential individuals
both in the United States and in Japan have unselfishly given
their tine and effort to help in Nisei adjustment and assimila-
tion in whatever country they may be.
Although much disapproval is voiced concerning the com-
mon use of the term "Nisei," it is used throughout this study for
the want of a better word and in view of the fact that the term
is being so widely used in Nisei circles. "Nisei" is used to imply
the foreign-born offspring of the Japanese men and women who
first migrated to the American continent and the Hawaiian
Islands, approximately a generation ago. These "second-genera-
tion" Japanese young people, Americans by birth and environ-
ment and at the same time Japanese by ties of blood, have
found themselves in a most unusual position.
Today, some fifteen years after the stream of migration
ceased, the heirs of these pioneers have grown up to the realiza-
tion that full and complete adjustment of their group can and
must be attained through their own efforts. In an attempt to
make this necessary adjustment some have gone on to higher
institutions of learning, some have gone out into the world to
make their living, and still others have come to the homeland
of their parents to seek an education which will aid in their
adjustment, or to establish permanent homes.
In this report we are interested for the most part in the
1

=== Page 27 === (confidence 1.00)
actual Nisei situation existing in Japan. The so-called "Nisei
problem" in Japan is a most complex one and its complete
solution is as yet to be found. The problem primarily arises
from the clash of two modern and advanced civilizations; the
one stressing individualism and freedom, and the other stressing
filial piety and loyalty to the Emperor based on the family
system. The so-called bad reputation of the Nisei may have
some rational basis. It is evidenced by a rather low standard
of appreciation of American life and education among the group,
and a rather mature, sophisticated attitude towards storing
intangible riches for the future. Oftentimes, the Nisei conduct,
life, and actions do not make favorable impressions upon the
Japanese. There are many other complaints in regard to
manners and behavior, and lack of adjustment in the educa-
tional, vocational and social life, and these constitute the major
part of the problem. However, despite the many discussions
in regard to the different aspects of the problem it was found
that very little accurate and correct information is available
concerning the Nisei situation in Japan. Hence this survey
was conducted to fill this specific need, for it was felt that true
understanding can only come about as the result of securing
correct information and knowledge of the existing situation.
The survey work was begun in the fall of 1938, and by
April of this year over one thousand copies of a questionnaire
had been sent out to the Nisei residing in Tokyo and Yokohama.
The work was limited to these districts, for to have to cope
with a larger, broader field would have been an impossible task
for the committee. It was estimated that approximately 1500
Nisei were living within this area, but great difficulty was en-
countered in compiling a complete and accurate mailing list.
It was found that the Nisei population is an ever-changing one
—that the Nisei do not generally stay in one place very long.
Every method at our disposal was employed to make the survey
complete. The Nisei were questioned by mail, through schools,
clubs, organizations and friends. We realize that we may have
missed many a name along the way; however, we tried our
very best and cannot say that our efforts were in vain, for
we have received approximately 440 answers to our question-
naire, an average well expressing the consensus of Nisei
opinion.

The questionnaire was divided into four general divisions;
namely, (1) General Information—sex; status; home town;
parents' occupation; date and purpose of coming to Japan:
(2) Education—previous education; purpose and method of
study (curriculum, extra-curricular activities, finance); (3)
Social Life—general adjustment; activities (recreation, amuse-
ments, religion); marriage; citizenship; future plans; and (4)
Vocation—type of vocation; method, difficulties in securing
positions; special advantages of being a Nisei; and the type of
work open to Nisei in Japan. It was later discovered that
parts of the questionnaire were ambiguous and vague, and also
that some items were unnecessary, whereas some important
points had been entirely left out. However, despite all its
shortcomings, we are happy to say that the majority of the
examinees were very cooperative and helpful in giving sincere
answers and opinions.
This pamphlet, as a summary of the survey, contains not
only the statistical results, but the conclusions drawn from them
and also the opinions and suggestions of interested individuals.
In regard to the vital problem that they face, it was revealed in
our survey that the attitude of the Nisei is reasonable
in that they are quite willing to try to come to a mutual under-
standing, at the same time contributing something worth while.
In the words of one young Nisei—"It is far better and wiser
to try to understand the Japanese than to try to make them
understand us"—is evident the desire to know, to understand
and to appreciate the Japanese mind, culture, life, customs, and
traditions.

Disadvantages of studying in a school:

VOCATION
"Opportunity Knocks...."
One of the most vital problems of the youth of today is
that of Vocation. The rising Second-Generation Japanese is no
exception.
There are many Nisei in the United States, Hawaii, and
Canada, who have graduated from college, but who have great
difficulty in securing positions in the fields for which they have
prepared themselves. So they have been working in fruit
markets, florist shops, or as clerks in retail firms, etc., while
their more ambitious brothers and sisters have come to Japan
believing that here they could find something bigger and better.
It was found that 80% of the answers to the questionnaire
were from students, and the other 20% were from those em-
ployed. As we had difficulty in getting in contact with the
latter, we did not get as many returns from them as we should
have liked. This survey is not by any means complete, but we
believe that it will serve as a good cross-section of the Nisei's
Vocational World.
Out of the 437 who sent in returns, only 99 are working
at present; 28 of them have worked before coming to Japan;

SOCIAL LIFE
ADJUSTMENT
"When in Japan do as the Japanese do"
Much has been said on the subject of the Social Life of
the Nisei. Problems arise as to whether or not the Nisei on
the whole have enough outlet in social activities, how and where
they spend their leisure time, whether or not they have been
able to continue their religious activities; problems arise also
concerning marriage, citizenship, and plans for the future.
Such social problems, as they appear in the survey, are discussed
in this chapter.
Can the Nisei adjust themselves to life in Japan? Having
been reared and educated in a country where democracy is the
keynote, the sudden change to the other extreme makes it hard
for them to adjust themselves at first. This is true of anyone
in a foreign country but the Nisei are faced with a problem
peculiar only to themselves. When in the country of their
ancestors, because of their physical characteristics they are
looked upon as Japanese and anything that may seem foreign
about them is looked upon with a critical eye.
According to the survey the greatest difficulty faced by
Nisei women in this problem of adjustment is the change in the
mode of living. This, no doubt, is because of the fact that women
are expected to live up to the strict standards of social etiquette
for Japanese women. For example, it is a strain for ordinary
men or women to sit on the "tatami" floor for any length
of time without finding themselves numb in their lower limbs.
And though leniency may be shown because one is a Nisei, still
it is considered a breach of social etiquette to sit with legs
stretched out in front of one. Furthermore women, in parti-
cular, have been taught to be careful of their speech; so the Nisei
women have greater difficulty in their language adjustment than
do the men. It is with the use of honorifics that women find their
greatest problem in language. Although in present-day Japan
29

=== Page 61 === (confidence 0.99)
less stress is placed upon rank or position in society still the use
of honorifics prevails. A Nisei with little knowledge of the Japa-
nese language might, at first, use the same terms in addressing
any person from the maid to a woman of rank. There is a time
and a place for everything and this is especially true with regard
to the Japanese language.
An experiment was once made at Aoyama Gakuin to as-
certain the number of years it would take a boy student of
average intelligence to become completely adjusted to Japanese
student life. The time differs according to the individual but
two years was found to be the average time for the adjustment.
However, it would probably take somewhat longer for a Nisei
woman to become completely adjusted to Japanese society. The
status of the Japanese woman in Japan is such that it is quite
understandable that Nisei women are faced with greater diff-
culty than men in making adjustments. Lack of understand-
ing of the Japanese people follows the language problem as a
difficulty for Nisei women in adjusting themselves to life in
Japan.
The problem of the change in food was not as great as
that of the other problems of adjustment. For some who have
had the same kind of food in the United States the change may
not have been noticeable to any great extent. For some, how-
ever, the sudden change from a diet of American food to one
of rice, pickles, and fish, the typical Japanese diet, lowered their
resistance and resulted in cases of beri-beri, jaundice, and other
ailments caused by an unbalanced diet. Many have been com-
pelled to discontinue study and return to their native country
because of ill health. However, the average Nisei is compara-
tively healthy and well built, having had proper health training,
an ideal climate, and wholesome food in the United States.
Men place language as their major adjustment difficulty.
Those attending school state that one of the most difficult sub-
jects in their curriculum is the study of the Japanese language.
And they must have a good command of Japanese in order to
secure positions or to enter the higher schools.
Many of those who answered the questionnaire, are of the
opinion that it is advisable for Nisei to spend at least one year
in a Japanese home, living as a Japanese, in order to learn the
culture and customs of Japan through home life. Though it

CONCLUSION
"A hint or two......."
To those of you who are coming to Japan:
Probably the greatest problem confronting you is that of
the reputation of the Nisei in Japan. We have found that the
so-called bad reputation is attributable to the fact that we are
too materialistic. We have come from a materialistic country
and have an idea that money can and will buy everything, Too
many of us value things in terms of money. Some are egotisti-
cal which is certainly contrary to Japanese ideals. The Nisei
complain of an inferiority complex brought about by race prej-
udice, but in reality egotism and self-concern are the roots of
this trouble. Where an inferiority complex reigns, a superiority
complex also is sure to be.
All of these things are a barrier between the Nisei and the
Japanese, who are proud of their social heritage which is nat-
urally different from that of the Nisei. However, if the Nisei
can overcome their egotism and resolve to observe the adage
"When in Rome do as the Romans do," they will be welcome in
Japanese society rather than considered social outcasts. This
is the responsibility of each Nisei. In society, the majority is
apt to be prejudged by the actions of the minority whc are not
representative of the whole. Therefore, the reputation of the
Nisei as a whole is in the hands of every Nisei. He should work
for a better Japanese attitude toward Nisei life and activities.
This can best be done by a more co-operative and friendly at-
titude on the part of the Nisei toward the Japanese and to other
Nisei as well.
"May we suggest....."
May we suggest the following as points to remember:
A Nisei should not come to Japan only because his
parents want him to, but of his own accord with the determi-
nation to study hard and not merely to have a good time. On
the other hand, if the Nisei desires to acquire an understand-
ing of Japanese culture and life, a certain amount of social

individual Nisei must decide what to make of his life, but that
decision will be an extremely difficult one to make.

are different in individual cases according to age, previous
education, purpose of study. A Nisei intending to come to
study must seriously consider his motive and the advantages
of studying here rather than in the land of his birth.
Furthermore, those who are intending to come to study

Notice to All Students
High-school graduates of America, Canada, and Hawaii who
wish to take the entrance examinations of any higher school,
college or preparatory department of a university in Japan, may
obtain an official certificate giving them an academic standing
in Japan equivalent to that of graduates of a middle school or
girls' high school, upon application to the Department of
Education.
In order to obtain this certificate, they are required to pre-
sent the following items to the Department of Education:
1. High School diploma (it will be returned).
2. Report card (record of entire high-school career).
3. School rules (School prospectus or year-book).
4. A certificate showing they have Japanese citizenship.
(Those who possess only foreign citizenship, present a
birth certificate.)
5.
Application (to the Department of Education, in Japa-
nese).
6.
A record of school life (a brief summary of schools
attended, dates of attendance, graduation, etc.).
7. Photograph (Full face, hatless, waist-length).
This official certificate is not necessary in many schools
where special consideration is given to foreign students, but
where there is none, entrance examinations cannot be taken
without it. This information can be obtained from the schools
themselves.
It should be remembered that if the name on the diploma
does not correspond with the one on the certificate showing
Japanese citizenship, (e.g. the George on the diploma and the
Taro on the Japanese register), the two will not be considered
the same person. Another precaution to be taken is to be sure
that the American birth certificate and the Japanese certificate
coincide. These little things may be the cause of ineligibility for
a certificate from the Department of Education.

NISEI OPPORTUNITIES IN JAPAN
by Tosuke Yamazaki
Nisei migration to Japan started 12 years ago,
but the majority of those came then were not sure whether
to make Japan their permanent home. Although the Japanese
Empire had been advertised abroad as a land of unlimited
opportunity for the second generation only a few chose
to stay to curve a fortune. Most of them returned to
the United States, but every year the number of nisei
migrants continued.
The general impression created in the United States
was that anybody with a smattering of English and a
speaking knowledge of Japanese could get a job. References
were frequently made to the nisei boy who crooned his
way to stardom, of the girl who became a well known
singer, of another who became an up and coming newspaper-
man. The future certainly looked bright. But today
it is evident that there was a great number of nisei
who had not apparently the future carefully when their
entering their chosen work. This is evident in the
great number of nisei throughout all Japan who are changing
their positions. They are going through the same process
as their parents have done or still may be doing in
America.
Now, what are the chances today for the young second
generation men and women who come for the first time to
the Far East? Whatever I may have to say, I hope that
my listeners will consider that I am speaking in general
terms and that I am aware that there are exceptions.
But whatever the trend, we must not discard the fact
that our lives may be subject to drastic changes, depending,
of course, upon the relations which are to pass between
the United States and Japan in the next 12 months.
At the present moment, no one can safely predict
the future, but it is accurate to say that any bettering
of relations between Japan and America will consequently
bring happy benefits to the nisei. Any worsening of
relations will also bring consequent unhappy results.
But leaving out all speculation, let us return to
some basic facts. I have talked with not a few second
generation and the parents of many nisei who have spent from
seven to ten years in Japan, and when I ask them if Japan
is still the land of unlimited opportunity, this is what
they usually say:
The old Japan of unlimited opportunity is gone. Every

My Ideals
By Isamu Kumai
I was born, brought up, and educated in the United States
and therefore even now have the so called merican individual-
ism and egotism.
However, since I have come to Japan I think I have been
influenced by Japanese thought, cooperative spirit, morality,
etc.
I wish to possess ideals that combine with harmony those
of the two countries. Individualism has many faults but it
is not absolutely in the wrong, one of its virtues being that
it respects the character of the individual, a very important
point to the individual. Cooperation is a necessity in the
life of the community but, on the other hand, it very rarely
recognizes the character of the individual. Therefore my
desire for a blend of the two.
In America the thought of equality is strong, in other
words, everyone is treated as an equal. However, I desire more
respect for elders than is found in this ideal of equality,
for equality does not consider age. Its merit being that it
gives everyone an equal opportunity. But however much this
equality extends onportunity to everyone I think there should
be a difference between the elders and the young. In some case
when my opinion does not agree with that of an elder or a friend
I would like to think of conceeding a point in their favor.
In this way I would like to receive the merits of both the
systems and I also would have individualism and egotism reformed
and improved as soon as possible.
Born in North America, brought up in North America, and
speaking English, the second-generation Japanese are American-
ized souls whose thoughts cannot be understood and who live as in
a different world, but in their veins courses the blood of
the Japanese race of Asia. When the mask is torn away and the
sleeper awakens, on that day the Yamato Spirit will blossom
forth. Ah, this solemn, utter truth! The second-generation
Japanese, who are of the Japanese race though American citizens,
raise the virtues of Japan, increase the merits of America, and
with their Japanese spirit purify the materialistic spirit of
the Americans.

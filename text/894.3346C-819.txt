AMERICAN CONSULATE
DEPARTMENT OF STATE
Colombo, Ceylon, June 26, 1934.

SUBJECT: VISIT ON - Japanese Naval Training Squadron
THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

COPIES SENT TO
O.N.I. AND M.I.D.

I have the honor to report that the Japanese coast defence vessels I-111 and 1111A visited Colombo from June 10 to 22, 1934. This visit was in the course of a routine training cruise which took the ships from Japan to Europe.

During the squadron's visit numerous official entertainments were held including a dinner given by the Governor and large receptions by the Japanese Consul and the Vice Admiral commanding the squadron. It was interesting to note the difference between the reaction of the local people to the Japanese visit and to that of the German cruiser HOLL. In the latter case there were no official entertainments by the British, and the German Consul was the only person who entertained on behalf of the personnel. While the local government and British naval authorities seemed to go out of their way to advance the cordial relations between Great Britain

Britain and Japan, there was an unexplained under-current during the period when the vessels were in port, and this has not yet ceased.

The officers and cadets of the Japanese ships made every effort to acquire as much information as possible during their visit and there has been a great deal of comment as to the boldness of many of the questions which were asked.

The day before the squadron sailed the vice Admiral commanding was the principal guest at the Colombo Rotary Club's weekly luncheon and spoke to the gathering. His speech was extraordinarily devoid of the usual Japanese propaganda and he concentrated on the development of mutual understanding as the most essential factor in world peace. He stated that he was convinced of the fact that "if every navy understands each other quite satisfactorily the world's peace would be truly maintained."

The squadron did not make very much of an impression on many of the people of Ceylon due, perhaps, to being so zealous to create a satisfactory impression. The ships themselves were badly kept and the personnel appeared to be below the standard which is expected of the Japanese navy. On the surface there appeared to be an unexpected amount of fraternising between officers and men and the commanding officer of M. M. S. COLOMBO, which was then in

in port, remarked on this feature. Possibly the behavior of the personnel is regulated to suit the ports visited.

The squadron left Colombo on the morning of June 22nd. The authorities were anxious as to whether an excuse might be found for the ships to put in at Trincomalee, which has been visited by German, French, and Persian warships within the past year. At the time of writing this despatch no report has been received that the squadron did call at the naval base.

Respectfully yours,

Frockholst Livingston
American Vice Consul.

AMERICAN CONSULATE GENERAL
Tokyo, Japan, June 5, 1931.

SUBJECT: Delay in Securing Data Called for by Questionnaires of the Bureau of Mines for 1930.

THE HONORABILITY
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

SIR:

I have the honor to refer to the Department's mimeographed instruction of March 20, 1931 (File No. 800.63/934), transmitting copies of questionnaires received from the United States Bureau of Mines relating to mineral production in Japan for the calendar year 1930, and directing that data indicated by these forms be secured and submitted.

It is desired to inform the Department that the data called for by the questionnaires will not be available at the appropriate Government offices until sometime late in the present calendar year or early in 1932. A similar delay in obtaining material for these questionnaires was experienced in connection with the last report on this subject, and in consequence it is believed that these delays can be attributed only to the fact that compilations of mineral statistics in the appropriate Japanese offices are running considerably behind.

Respectfully yours,

Lee D. Sturgeon,
American Consul in Charge.

AMERICAN CONSULATE GENERAL.
Tokyo, Japan. January 21, 1932.

SUBJECT: Mineral Report for the Bureau of Mines.

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

SIR:

I have the honor to refer to the Department's memorandum instruction dated March 20, 1931 (File No. 800.63/934), transmitting copies of questionnaires and supplementary memoranda relating to mineral production during the calendar year 1930, in certain foreign countries and areas, and directing that the data indicated be obtained and submitted to the Department.

The report called for by the Department's instruction has been prepared on the outline forms supplied by the Bureau of Mines of the Department of Commerce, and is transmitted herewith. The statistics utilized in completing the forms have been obtained from the Bureau of Mines of the Department of Commerce and Industry, Tokyo, and are therefore official in character. Figures in all cases are presented in as complete form as possible.

In this connection, reference is made to the Consulate General's despatch No. 180, dated June 5, 1931, in which the reason for the unavoidable delay in the submission of this report was explained.

Respectfully yours,

Arthur Garrels
American Consul General.

G-2 REPORT
JAPAN (ECONOMIC)
Subject: Metallic Minerals.
Plans for Conservation.

1. As an example of the concern felt in governmental circles concerning the matter of conservation and consumption economy in the use of important metals, steps are now being taken with a view to a further tightening of the various control measures which have already been introduced within the past few months.

a. In general, the measures invoked for the purpose of conserving key metals have been those which limited imports to amounts which would be sufficient for carrying on key war industries and at the same time provided the raw materials necessary for manufactured exports, measures prohibiting the use of processed raw materials for any local consumption other than those connected with war needs, and a strict rationing of such materials to users in accordance with the two foregoing considerations. Added to these measures have been steps for a general conservation of metals already in the country.

b. It has been found, however, that the foregoing measures left something to be desired, and that, due to the difficulty of supervision of all the details of the above measures, the production increases have not reached desired objectives. One of the contributing reasons for this condition has been the difficulty of keeping idle stocks in motion.

c. The government therefore, has come forward with plans which involve the buying (by the Government) of all idle stocks of such metals as iron, steel, copper, nickel, lead, zinc, tin, and other non-ferrous metals in order to better control their distribution and consumption by users engaged in manufacturing for war purposes as well as those manufacturing for export. The details of the mechanism by which this control is to be effected are not yet divulged further than that hereafter all purchases will be made through joint sales companies which will handle various classes of the above materials, and that the purchase prices will be based on quotations made by those companies. Government circles appear rather sanguine as to the outcome of these measures, and point to the fact that it is estimated that the suspension of work on various buildings will bring into use for war and export purposes some 50,000 tons of steel alone.

d. In conjunction with the above measures, there are being organized salvage concerns which will attend to

a systematic collection and distribution of all scrap metals of all kinds. This collection includes the scrap resulting from various manufactures as well as waste metal incident to small industries and ordinary households. In short, every scrap of waste metal is to be conserved.

2. Comments.

a. While various steps taken toward the conservation of metals are not new in the course of the present emergency, those mentioned above are of interest in that they indicate the trend toward a continued tightening of control in order to render more effective the measures already taken, as well as for the reason they indicate that, in a country situated as is Japan with reference to raw materials, the conservation and most effective use of those materials is a subject requiring the most careful coordination.

b. While it is felt that the government ownership and distribution of key metals may well lead to a more effective distribution and use in accordance with the main objectives of keeping war industries going and at the same time furthering the development of the export market, the fact that this step has been taken, together with the establishment of scrap collection companies, is an indication of the concern which is felt concerning these materials and the lengths to which the government is prepared to go in order to achieve the limit of conservation. The success or failure of the movement will, of course, depend upon the broader question of national economy as a whole in the present emergency, as well as upon the intensity and duration of the present military activity in China.

Source: Press.

Harry I. T. Creswell,
Major, Acting General Staff,
Military Attache.

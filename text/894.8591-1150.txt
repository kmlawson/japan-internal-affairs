Reference is made to the Embassy's strictly confi-
dential letter of June 22, 1934, requesting that the Con-
sulate report any concentration of Japanese merchant vessels
in home waters.
Since March 1, 1934, there has been a concentration
of over 400,000 gross tons- one-tenth of the entire mer-
chant marine- in home waters. This is, however, for the most
part a normal seasonal trend, accounted for by the demands
of the summer lumber trade from Hokkaido and Karafuto. The
increase in the tonnage of vessels in the coasting trade
between March 1 and June 10, 1934 is 305,624 gross tons.
When it is seen that the 59 steamers in the lumber trade
alone aggregate 315,927 gross tons it can read 1y be com-
prehended that the bulk of the increase is due to this
seasonal movement of north-seas logs. The shioment of lumber
commenced early in May and will continue to the end of Sep-
tember.
It is noteworthy that since March 1 450,000 gross
tons of Japanese merchantmen have been taken from deep water
runs and allocated to coasting and nearseas service. As of
June 10, 1934 there were 19 vessels in the trad with Europe,
aggregating 176,28l gross tons, a decrease from the March total
of 195,734 tons. On the same date 35 ships of 327,349 gross
tons were trading to Atlantic ports of North America, a de-
crease of 70,898 tons. In the trade to the American Pacific
Coast on June 10 there were 35 Japanese vessels, totalling
345,874 gross tons, or a decrease since March 1 of 99,942 tons
Ships trading to India and Australia numbered 19 of 141,360
gross tons, or 87,299 gross tons less than three months ago.
Ships in the near-seas and coasting waters increased by
419,144 gross tons between March 1 and June 10. Most of this
was represented by the increase of 305,624 tons in the coasting
trade, in which there are presently engaged 299 vessels of
1,395,248 gross tons. There are 90 ships in service to the
Mandated

An analysis of the above data reveals the following

Although the tonnage of ships in home waters has

of deep water ships have left the foreign trade in

is not revealed by the statistics under reference,
which are cewpiled by the Kobe Shipping Exchange for
the entire merchant marine. It is possible that
the discrepancy may be accounted for by the failure
of certain members of the Exchange to return com-
plete reports for their ships in the coastwise
and near-seas services.
2.
The principal increase in the number of ships in
home waters is due to the normal seasonal demand
of the lumber trade.
3.
The fact that the increase of tonnage in home
waters is accompanied by a decrease of tonnage
in the foreign trade indicates that the ships
recently allocated to near-seas service are large
vessels. The 59 steamers in the lumber trade
average 5,355 gross tons. None, however, is
capable of a speed in excess of 15 knots.
4.
The ships which are most useful to the Japanese
Navy are still in the deep water trades . Reference
is made to the score of magnificent motor freighters
which have been built, many with a Government bounty
of ¥52 per gross ton, for the trade to New York and
the Pacific Coast. These ships can make 18 knots
and over and are the fastest freighters in the world.
They would be more effective for purposes of fleet
train than any auxiliary craft in the American Navy,
as regards speed and cargo capacity. (Cf. this
Consulate's report on Shipping in the Japanese Empire
During Apri1, 1934, pp-3-8 .)

Since October, 1932 there have been laid down in
Japanese dockyards thirty-one of the fastest merchant
vessels in the world.
Twenty seven huge motor
freighters have either
been ordered or built for the
trade to New York alone which are the fastest cargo
ships on any ocean.
These Japanese vessels operate
to the Pacific Coast at a speed reater than that of
any transpacific liners under the American flag save
only four. Japan has fifty-four merchant vessels in
the Pacific trade which are capable of speeds of
894.859
eighteen knots or over.
The United tates has twenty-
five such ships. There is no freighter in the American
register which can even approximate the speed of these
11
Japanese cargo vessels. There is no unit of fleet
5
train in the United States Navy which they could not
leave far astern. While American Navy tankers lumber
along at from 12 to 14 knots and American naval supply
ships have a maximum speed of 12 knots, the Japanese
Navy has at its disposal potential tankers and supply
vessels of 18 knots. In a Pacific conflict involving
two large fleets, both remote from bases, the pos-
session of fleet train capable of keeping up with the
combatant units might mark the difference between
victory and defeat.
While this paper is concerned with the military
and naval implications of the new Japanese merchant
fleet, it must be recognized at the beginning that the
primary impetus for the subsidized construction program
which resulted in these new ships was more commercial
than strategical.
Japanese shipyards were at a low ebb
of

of activity in 1932 when the Ship Improvement Law was
passed and the merchant marine was burdened with an
incubus of obsolete tonnage.
Furthermore, the Osaka
Shosen Kaisha had just shown the way to a new and
lucrative trade by laying down huge 18 knot freighters
for the silk trade to New York, which formerly had gone
by rail across the United States. The other Japanese
lines in the New York trade were quick to follow suit.
The Ship Improvement Law, therefore, was the outcome
of combined pressure from the dockyards, the steamship
operators and the Navy, which saw in the building of
the new fleet the significant possibilities of such
potential units of fleet train.
The Ship Improvement Law which went into effect
in October of 1932 provided for the subsidized
construction of 200,000 gross tons of freighters in
return for the scrapping by owners of 400,000 gross
tons of vessels more than twenty-five years old. The
subsidy paid by the Imperial Government was ¥54 per
gross ton for vessels of l8 knots and over, ranging
down to ¥48. Although not so provided in the law, the
propelling units of all the ships laid down under its
terms were diesel motors, with the exception of one
vessel fitted with geared turbines.
Although the Ship Improvement Law of 1932
contemplated a two year program terminating in 1935,
there was such a rush of applicants for subsidy that
the project was brought to completion within eighteen
months. The results of the law may be summarized as

Such is the new fleet of Pacific merchantmen evoked

built ships, plus this very modest bounty of v54 per ton
whieh made the differential favorable to Japanese yards.

BUREAU OF FOREIGN AND DOMESTIC COMMERCE
WASHINGTON

September 13, 1932.

Hon. Wilbur J. Carr,
Assistant Secretary of State
Department of State,
Washington, D.C.

My dear Mr. Carr:

In looking over material preparatory to a special compilation on Opportunities for American Dentists in the Far East, the Division of Regional Information has encountered certain statements from American consular officers stationed in Japan as follows:

Consul E. R. Dickover, Kobe, March 19, 1931:

Japanese law does not require examinations for license to practice if the applicant is a graduate of a recognized college of dentistry. Licenses are obtained from the Department of Home Affairs in Tokyo, and are issued under the following conditions: (A list of six separate rules or regulations is concluded with the following statement);

"While there is usually considerable delay in obtaining a license, as a rule there is little doubt of any properly qualified applicant eventually receiving a license."

Consul General Arthur Garrels, Tokyo, April 12, 1932:

The practice of dentistry without a license previously obtained from the Ministry of Home Affairs is prohibited under heavy penalties. Licenses may be issued without examination only to graduates of Japanese dental colleges and to nationals of England and Mexico who are able to qualify under the terms of special reciprocal arrangements...

From the above discussion you will note that while it is possible for an American dentist to practice in Japan, inherent difficulties exist in securing a license.

The foregoing excerpts from correspondence of the consular officers in question have led to some confusion with regard to the facts, and in the interests of accuracy we would appreciate, before attempting to complete the compilation in mind, any further information which the Department of State may have upon the subject.

Very truly yours,

Robert J. Phillips
Robert J. Phillips,
Liaison Officer.

October 7, 1932.

Dear Neville:

I am sending you herewith a copy of a letter from the Department of Commerce, the contents of which are self-explanatory.

Upon receipt of the letter in question, the Department contemplated sending instructions to the Tokyo and the Kobe Consulates asking the Consulates to harmonize the information. However, it has seemed to me, recollecting that there has in the past been some friction between Messrs. Garrels and Dickover, it might be expedient, on behalf of peace and harmony, to ask some other office to get the Department the information which is needed rather than refer the matter back to the two offices from which originated the inconsistent data. Therefore, I have suggested that we handle the matter by means of this personal communication to you. I would suggest that the Embassy make the subject of the dental regulations a matter for an original despatch as of Embassy initiative.

I dislike bothering you with this, but I imagine that it will not require a great deal of effort and I believe that by this procedure the Department will receive the information desired and no possibility of embarrassment as among the various offices concerned will be obviated.

Yours cordially,

Enclosure.

Edwin L. Neville, Esquire,
Counselor, American Embassy,
Tokyo, Japan.

A true copy of the signed original.

EMBASSY OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA
Tokyo, May 3, 1933.

Dear Stanley:

Upon receipt of your letter of October 7, 1932, regarding the practice of dentistry and medicine in Japan the Embassy addressed a Note to the Foreign Office on October 27th, asking for data on this subject. After many personal calls at the Foreign Office the Embassy has finally received its reply, dated April 25, 1933. The Foreign Office's reply is being transmitted to the Department in the Embassy's despatch No. 374 of May 2, 1933, and I hope that the information contained therein will clear up the confusion mentioned in the enclosure to your letter under reference.

Sincerely yours,

Dr. Stanley Hornbeck,
Chief, Division of Far Eastern Affairs,
Department of State,
Washington, D. C.

dentistry in Japan.

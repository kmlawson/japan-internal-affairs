Referring to my despatch No. 831, dated July 27,
1937, File No. 800, entitled "NETHERLAND INDIA'S REAC-
TIONS TO THE PRESENT SINO-JAPANESE CONTROVERSY", and
specifically to paragraph numbered "3" on page 2 thereof,
I have the honor to report that Portuguese Timor, accord-
ing to a high official of the Government of the Nether-
lands Indies, has practically fallen into the hands of
the Japanese. The official in question, who is Advisor
to the Governor General for East Asiatic Affairs, regard-
ed the matter as of sufficient importance to warrant his
coming to my office to furnish me with the details of
the moves which have been made and the plans which he al-
leges the Japanese will probably carry out.
It appears that the "Nanyo Kohatsu Kaisha" has pur-
chased the Portuguese concern known as the "Sociedade d'
Arricultura y de Travalho" (sometimes known as the "Sociedade
Agricola Pahia Travalho") for the sum of 5,000,000
Yen. The arrangements for this purchase are said to have
been

been made by Mr. S. Segawa of the Nanyo Kohatsu Kaisha in Lisbon with Dr. Charles Luis (Dr. Carlos Luiz), formerly manager of the "Sociedade" and a relative (probably son-in-law) of Senhor da Silva who was formerly Governor General of Portuguese Timor.

According to my informant, the "Sociedade" is the largest and most important business concern in Portuguese Timor and has, among other things, about 15,000 hectares of land partly planted in coffee and rubber and partly undeveloped.

The old name of the concern will be retained only temporarily, the new name being the "Timor Gunto Kaihotsu Kaisha".

My informant also said that "fake directorships" had been offered to the present Director General of the Banco Nacional Ultramarino and to Senhor Montalvan da Silva and that both of these gentlemen would probably accept "this apparently somewhat doubtful distinction". Mr. Segawa and Dr. Luis are said to be en route to Timor where the business is temporarily being managed by a Japanese, Mr. Ogawa.

The Government of Portuguese Timor, according to my informant, is at present financially self-supporting, obtaining its funds through financing the export trade of the colony and in taxing the exports, especially of coffee, wax, rubber and sandalwood. With the exception of a small head tax, there is practically no other source of revenue for the Government. It is believed, therefore, that the commercial policy of the new Japanese concern will be to force the hand of the Government by cornering the export trade

trade of the colony, largely through outbidding the local traders most of whom are Chinese who are weak financially. If the Japanese should succeed in cornering the trade of the colony and then export nothing, the Government would be without funds and would be forced to fall back on loans from the new company. It is said that the loans would be furnished freely by the Nanyo Kohatsu Kaisha, the parent company, which is said to be a part of the "Oriental Development Company", which concern, in turn, is believed to be under the control of the Japanese Navy Department.

The purposes behind these moves, my informant claims, are the following:

1. To force the Portuguese Government to grant exclusive and extensive concessions to the Japanese;

2. To force the Government to grant permits to all Japanese interests, especially those behind the Nanyo Kohatsu Kaisha, to trade freely in Portuguese Timor;

3. To force the granting of permits to Japanese interests to engage in coastwise trade and the overseas transportation business to and from Timor, the projected line running from Dilly via Surabaya, Menado, Singapore, and Macao to Japan.

Great secrecy is said to have surrounded the transactions which led to the sale; and it is thought that if any suspicions had arisen that the "Sociedade" contemplated selling its interests in Timor, other countries, such as the Netherlands or Australia, might have offered higher bids. Secrecy was apparently maintained, however, through the pressure which Japan is said to have exerted either on certain individuals or on the Government of Portuguese Timor itself. Five million (5,000,000) Yen appears to be an insignificant sum to pay for the enormous interests of the "Sociedade", but it is rumored in some circles that still

other sums may have been paid not to the "Sociedade" itself.

In size Portuguese Timor is relatively small, but its strategic value is said to be very important, especially if Japan should decide to carry out its alleged policy of expanding to the South. A military, naval or air base, or all, built up slowly, steadily and secretly, might be a great menace to the Netherlands Indies.

The above are the fears now being felt by local officials who are endeavoring in every possible way to learn all of the facts of the case and their possible or probable implications.

Subject: Portuguese Colony, Timor.

DIVISION OF

JAN 29 1938
DEPARTMENT OF STATE

JAN 26 1938

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington, D.C.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that the other day I was approached by the Dutch Minister, John Sillem, on the subject of the Island of Timor.

The Portuguese half comprises 7,330 square miles and half 460,665 inhabitants. It was administered until 1896 from Macao and since then has been an independent colony. The Portuguese part is just east of 125° East and about 9° South latitude. The island is approximately 400 miles from Port Darwin and about 300 miles from the nearest Australian possession. The western half of the island belongs to the Dutch.

There was a rumor of Japanese penetration which worried
the

=== Page 7 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

the Dutch Government to such extent that it instructed its representatives to make inquiries. At the time I knew nothing about the case but I assumed that the English would also be interested so I spoke to Mr. Bateman, the Counselor of the Embassy; I have also spoken in an incidental way to Mr. Sampayo, the Secretary General of Foreign Affairs. This matter has also been referred to in Despatch No. 523 from the American Consulate General of Hong Kong, April 15, 1937, a copy of which marked "Strictly Confidential" arrived here on May twelfth.

Apparently, a large property on the island was accumulated by one of the earlier Portuguese governors of Timor which on his death was incorporated, the stock remaining in the family. This company leased its property to a Japanese concern according to the British information or borrowed a large sum according to the Portuguese story.

The English and Dutch very frankly fear Japanese penetration and the Portuguese Government appears to be somewhat worried. Mr. Sampayo told me that the question was under serious consideration and that they would not allow any such leases in the future. Although the story from the British Embassy agrees in general terms with that of Mr. Sampayo, there were certain minor differences including the name of the former governor whom the English refer to as Santa Cruz and the Portuguese as de Silva. Apparently the Portuguese Government is quite as worried about this affair as either the Dutch or the English. It would not do for the present Government to lose its colonial possessions of which the people are inordinately proud.

One of the frontiers of Macao was not clearly defined and the Portuguese made an agreement with the Chinese Government some years ago to fix the limits. This was not done and the Japanese have occupied that part which the Chinese claimed, apparently

apparently treating themselves as the successors of the Chinese Government. This has been met by protests from Portugal and, according to the Portuguese papers, the Japanese have kept out of that part which the Chinese recognized as being Portuguese. The papers did not refer however to Japanese planes flying over it.

This information about Macao comes exclusively from Mr. Sampayo. A certain number of Portuguese airplanes have been sent to Macao to reinforce the Portuguese garrison there.

It is doubtful if the Japanese will respect Portuguese rights any more than they have those of Great Britain and it is certain that the British will not defend the Portuguese any more seriously than themselves. The Timor episode will probably have some repercussions in Australia.

=== Page 14 === (confidence 0.98)
DEPARTMENT OF STATE
DIVISION OF FAR EASTERN AFFAIRS
April 24, 1935.

Mr. Southgate.

It will be noted that the original of the telegram to the President from Mr. Swift, of New Haven, asking that a fleet with relief supplies be sent to Taiwan, was referred to the Navy Department. We have telephoned the Navy Department asking that no action be taken in the matter until there has been further discussion between the State and Navy Departments.

The Red Cross confirmed the accuracy of a report which appeared in the papers this morning, to the effect that the President of the Japanese Red Cross had telegraphed the President of the American Red Cross expressing confidence in the ability of the Japanese Red Cross to take care of the present emergency.

It is, therefore, suggested that Mr. Swift be informed of the substance of the telegram from the President of the Japanese Red Cross Society and that there would appear to be no occasion for acting on his suggestion.

Also suggest sending him copies of President Roosevelt message of sympathy.

M. M. J.

May 2, 1935.

Dear Mrs. Roosevelt:

In reply to your inquiry about the Formosa disaster, I quote the following telegram which was sent immediately by Admiral Grayson to the President of the Japanese Red Cross:

"Deepest sympathy Formosa disaster. Earliest dispatches here report characteristically prompt and effective action by Japanese Red Cross in extending relief. Advise if we can be of assistance. Grayson. AmCross."

The reply received was as follows:

"Thanks deepest sympathy Formosa disaster. Relief arrangements seem at present sufficient our part."

The President sent the following message to the Emperor of Japan:

"The people of the United States join with me in extending to Your Majesty and to the people of Japan deepest sympathy in the suffering and loss of life caused by the traffic disaster in Taiwan."

The

Mrs. Roosevelt
The White House.

The Secretary of State also sent a message of sympathy to the Foreign Minister and has received a very appreciative reply.

As you suggested, I have spoken with Cary Grayson. He tells me that following the disaster he called upon the Japanese Ambassador to express sympathy on behalf of the Red Cross and also to inquire whether the Ambassador had anything to suggest with regard to assistance from the American Red Cross. A day or two later the Ambassador returned the call upon Grayson with the information that about 5000 persons had been killed, but that the authorities had the situation entirely in hand.

Inasmuch, therefore, as inquiries have already been made in Tokyo and in Washington as to whether we could be of assistance and that from both sources replies have been received that no assistance is needed. I feel we have done all we properly can in the circumstances.

Sincerely yours,

# AMERICAN CONSULATE,
Taihoku, Taiwan, Japan, May 14, 1935.

SUBJECT: Damage Caused by Recent Earthquake in Taiwan.

COPIES SENT TO
O.N.I. AND M.I.D.

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE:

WASHINGTON:
I have the honor to refer to my telegram of April 22, 9 a.m., reporting an earthquake which occurred in the southern part of Shinchiku and the northern part of Taichu provinces of Taiwan on April 21, 1935, and the loss of lives caused thereby.

The Government General of Taiwan issued on May 11, 1935, a statement summarizing the damage sustained as a result of the earthquake. The salient points of this statement were as follows:

Killed, Injured, Etc.

Out of a population of 950,000 in the areas affected, 3,249 persons were killed and 11,993 were injured, total casualties reaching 15,242. Most of the killed and injured were Formosan Chinese. Only 8 Japanese were reported killed and only 29 injured. No foreigners, other than Chinese, were either killed or injured.

A total of 260,000 persons had to be taken care of by relief measures, due principally to loss of homes.

Property Damage

The greatest damage done to property, and of course the most distressing, was the total and partial destruction of

of thousands of dwelling houses of the poorer classes. Houses totally destroyed amounted to 17,835; those termed half destroyed totaled 11,244, and an additional 9,429 were badly damaged. Destruction and damages to buildings are estimated at Yen 14,000,000.

Damage to the mountain line of the Taiwan State Railway running between Chikunan and Shoka via Taichu is estimated at roughly Yen 6,000,000. In fact, tunnels and bridges on this line have been so badly damaged there is a rumor that the railway authorities are contemplating an abandonment of the line, using instead the coast line between these two points. This move however is, according to press reports, meeting with the opposition of the Army which claims that the mountain line is more easily defended against air raids.

According to the Government's report, damage to crops, livestock, business, etc. have not yet been investigated, but that they are also considered large.

The Taiwan Electric Power Company has reported that its vast hydroelectric plant at Lake Jitsugetsutan was not damaged by the earthquake. The Company has made an examination of the dam, intakes, water canals, transmission lines, et cetera, and has reported no damage to any of its holding except small lines in the earthquake area. However, a small station in the center of the affected area was destroyed.

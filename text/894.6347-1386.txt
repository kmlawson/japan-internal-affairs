SECRET 30th April 1943

Dear Bliss,

As arranged, I enclose, herewith, a copy of a letter received by Strevens here from Owen who is the M.E.W. representative at the Office of the Minister of State, Cairo, regarding the reported flooding of the Martapena diamond fields in North Borneo.

For your information Larkins was formerly the British Commercial Agent in Batavia and the relative part of paragraph 29 of the above "Notes" reads as follows:

"Germany cannot supply Japan with the following:-
(IV) Oil for the hydraulic mechanism and diamonds for a grinding machine. Although the Japanese Commercial Agent in Berlin had already stated that these could not be supplied, (as reported previously) his firm in Manchuria advised him to try again."

I also enclose a copy of a minute by Larkins dated the 27th August 1942 regarding the Japanese occupation of Sumatra and Borneo.

According to the information we have on our files, the diamond production available to the Japanese in Borneo in 1939 amounted to 2,287 carats. In 1935 it was much higher at 4,219 carats, but during the last 10 years it has averaged well under 1,000 carats annually and in view of the fact that it is estimated that Japan requires at least 250,000 carats of industrial diamonds each year, and possibly as much as 400,000 carats, to supply her war machine, it does not seem that any appreciable proportion of her requirements can be obtained from the Martapena fields. Incidentally the recent B.E.W. report in the Japanese abrasives position stated that, with extreme measures, Japan might obtain as much as 30,000 carats per annum from Borneo.

However, I am personally of the opinion that they could not obtain anything approaching such a large quantity.

Yours sincerely,
(Signed) J. L. READING

18th March 1943.

Secret
By Air Bag

My Dear Strevens,

Larkins has made the following comments on Paragraph 29 of the report "Notes of Economic Intelligence" for the week ending February 12th.

"It is interesting to see that Japan cannot, apparently, supply industrial diamonds. It must therefore be concluded that the flooding of the Martapena fields (North Borneo) was more successful than I thought would have been the case when I advised this course. The Java production was less important, but the despatch of all known stocks to Ceylon and Australia must have been a big set back for the Japanese as they would have to start from scratch. Unless the Japanese cannot prepare diamonds for industrial purposes, which would account for their having to get them from enemy occupied diamond cutting centres (Belgium and Holland) it would seem that the failure to supply diamond grinders should be Japan's, not Germany's, as the former controls some sources of supply. At the same time, it seems probable that the few European cutters caught in Java and Martapena have been unwilling to cut as internees. I doubt if the native cutters, without European supervision, would be able to cut for industrial uses. The Japanese Consul at Surabaya was very active in his search for diamonds but we managed to prevent any reaching him."

Yours ever,
Rowland Owen.

E. J. Holford-Strevens, Esq.,
Ministry of Economic Warfare,
London.

MINUTE BY COMMERCIAL SECRETARY, CAIRO

Secret

I submit the following observations in connection with the Notes of Economic Intelligence for the week ending 31/7/42.

1. The settlement and control of the East Coast of Sumatra by the Japanese will be far more difficult of achievement than the Transocean report would imply. They will no doubt keep Medan strongly occupied, but the hinterland of Atjea is believed to be already in revolt.

2. The Japanese will naturally fortify the approaches to Palembang and the mouths of the Palembang river, but outside such protecting works, they are unlikely to make such headway in any settlement ventures.

3. It would be interesting to learn which diamonds are intended for the proposed transaction with Switzerland. The fields at Martapura (Neth: Borneo) and Java were flooded before the occupation - though of course, they can have been pumped out by now - but it is doubtful whether Japan can find the experts to grade N.E.I. diamonds unless they can get some out of Holland or Belgium. One of the Gutwirths (Aaron) is still believed to be in Java, Konsommer, and perhaps three or four others, but Gutwirth is more likely to hide his stones for future business after the War. However, he must not necessarily be trusted, except that he knows well enough that his parents are within our influence (at Lorenzo Marques after escaping from France) and his brother (Hendrik) and sister-in-law are in Australia. Aaron therefore has no incentive to help the Japanese as the family affection is sincere.

(Sgd.) L.B.S. LARKINS

27.8.42

DEPARTMENT OF STATE
DIVISION OF FAR EASTERN AFFAIRS

April 29, 1943.

Reference Kuibyshev's despatch no. 270, March 18, 1943 with which is transmitted a translation from the Russian language of an article entitled "Ferrous Metallurgy in Japan Under War Conditions".

The author of the article states that the combined production of Japan, Korea and Manchuria during 1941 is estimated at 4,500,000 tons of pig iron and 7,000,000 tons of steel. He gives pig iron capacity of the area as 8,000,000 tons but does not give steel capacity. He does not give the source of these figures.

The writer proceeds to examine in considerable detail the iron ore and coal resources of Japan, Korea and Manchuria and other areas under Japanese control together with past performance and capabilities to increase output. He also discusses the position with respect to scrap. His conclusion is that the Japanese do not have the facilities for obtaining the materials necessary to increase production of iron and steel appreciably over that of 1941.

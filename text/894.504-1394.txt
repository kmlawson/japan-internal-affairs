With reference to a series of despatches which I have been forwarding to the Department concerning various phases of the present politico-economic situation in Japan, I have the honor in this despatch briefly to discuss certain aspects of the labor situation in Japan at present.

For some time there has been considerable discussion in vernacular periodicals and newspapers of a "labor shortage" in Japan. It has been reported that to alleviate this shortage Korean laborers have been brought in and employed largely in the extractive industries, particularly coal mining. In addition to mobilization of man power for the military effort on the continent, the rapid expansion of industry and the building of new factories have placed a great strain on the "reservoir of laborers" who have hitherto resided in the country districts and been readily available to Japanese industry. The rapid depletion of these reserves has seriously handicapped agricultural pursuits and has in turn made for a general increased cost of living resulting from decreased agricultural production accompanied by increased cost of agricultural production. The most serious shortage in Japanese labor is found in the ranks of skilled workers. This shortage is not limited to Japan alone but is noticeable throughout the "yen bloc" area, particularly "Manchukuo" and North China. Technicians graduating from trade schools and technical schools are in such demand that it has been necessary for the Government to allocate the graduates to various industries. There is being undertaken a special census of labor and technicians which is to be used as the basis for "labor mobilization, strengthening of the labor power of Japan, and improvement in the mobilization of technicians". This census is to be completed by the end of March 1941.

In view of this shortage of labor it would be expected that wages, both real and monetary, in Japan at the present time

=== Page 3 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

time would be so high as to assure all laborers an unusually high standard of living. Real wages have not, as has been usual in inflationary trends and periods of rising prices, kept pace with rising commodity prices, and have, it is believed, in the last two months shown a serious decline in relation to commodity prices. The Government has been forced to announce reconsideration of the established "starting wages" for unskilled laborers not only in the already well paid heavy industries but also in the less favored general industries. From time to time there have been announced various government projects to alleviate conditions of laborers, such as the establishment of community kitchens, subsidized housing projects, and "government family allowances for laborers".

In contrast to this picture of general low real wages and unsatisfactory labor conditions, the shortage of labor in Japan has at the same time resulted in fabulous wages -- as high as 30 yen per day -- being paid to certain types of skilled workers in the munitions and other heavy industries. In many instances these laborers are paid salaries far above those ordinarily received by responsible government officials, store managers, and other office workers. There are many amusing stories of the squandering of this new-found wealth which is said to be detrimental to the Government's program for "spiritual mobilization". While the number of such skilled workers is comparatively small, the excessive remuneration which they receive serves not only to encourage the migration of laborers from the countryside and from other industries seeking to obtain a share of this prosperity, but also makes for discontent among the less fortunate workers.

It was announced early this month that Mr. Isoo Abe and other members of the Social Mass Party who resigned following the disagreement over the question of disciplining Mr. Saito were to form a new Labor Party. This announcement was followed shortly by the report that the Home Ministry had decided to prohibit the formation of the new party as it was believed that such a party would run counter to the Government's efforts to unify all elements in the nation and would, contrary to the best interests of Japan, promote "class struggle". At the same time it was reported that the Government would probably take some action against the largest labor union in Japan since, it was said, this union was to be the nucleus of the new party.

In summary, Japan's plans for industrial expansion are hampered by a shortage of technicians and skilled laborers. Real wages are in fact declining and the condition of the laboring classes in Japan is not in keeping with the inflationary trend of large Government expenditures. While there have been no overt expressions of labor unrest, undoubtedly owing to careful Government supervision, it is worthy of note that the Government has made efforts to prevent the formation of a group which might articulately express the urgent desire of the laboring classes for more considerate treatment.

In line with the Japanese Government's policy of maintaining secrecy with regard to all phases of national economy, all statistical data with regard to labor conditions have been suppressed, and it has, therefore, been impossible to support with reliable figures the statements

Reference the underlying copy of a letter dated July 23, 1943 from the Economic Warfare Division of the Embassy at London to OEW, with which are enclosed summarized translations of three recent articles which appeared in Japanese magazines.

(1) The first, from the magazine Japan Review of February 1943, discusses labor supply under new conditions. It calls attention to the great importance of labor as a "national service", and states that efforts to increase labor are highly important. It is pointed out that in spite of all efforts, the utilization of female labor seems to be decreasing in Japan rather than increasing. The use of prisoners of war and other prisoners is discussed, but the question is left open as no information was available to the writer. The writer expressed the belief that Chinese coolies comprised the most important future source of additional labor.

It is interesting to note that Government efforts to supply labor have not been entirely successful, as the author states that on many occasions workers who have been promised by the Government have failed to show up.

(2) In an article entitled "Eat Unpolished Rice", the author calls attention to the seriousness of Japan's food position. He states that the war has become a war of production in which shipping is vitally important, and that in order to save shipping Japan must become self-reliant in regard

"LABOUR SUPPLY UNDER THE NEW CONDITIONS"
By Yoshiyuki Tomiishi
(From "Labour Monthly" (Rodo Geppyo))

(Summarized Translation from "Japan Review" (Nippon Hyoron) of February, 1943)

The New Management Policy.

This is management inspired by national service, and its resulting ideals are really the same as those of the Factory Law when you come to look at them. The workers have certainly shown a willingness to work, regardless of wage questions, but how much new spirit did managements show until ordered to set the example last October?

A "co-operative structure" has been proclaimed, but if the new labour management policies rely too much on individualistic measures they can hardly be called "co-operative". The recent attention paid to ways of systematising labour management policy is therefore a step in the right direction. It was for instance clearly an advance when the system was adopted last autumn of allotting specific duties and factories to each director, thus establishing where the responsibility lies. Another good sign is the consolidation by several big companies, including Kikyu Engineering Works, of their methods of treatment of their office and labour forces. Last of all one must not omit mention of the championing nowadays by the authorities and industrial leaders of conversion to a monthly wage system for workers. At any rate, there is now a consensus of opinion that the former pay system will not do for securing and expanding production, and I myself believe that payment of a "living wage plus efficiency allowance" will be one of the measures considered.

2. New Sources of Labour Supply.

Those usually mentioned are persons who have just left school, women as yet unemployed, persons transferred from non-essential work, and Koreans; but examination reveals the imaginary nature of these schemes.

For instance, scholars have been enthusiastic over female labour since 1937, but their examples are all from European countries in the last war. Indeed the facts belie popular ideas on this subject, as the following shows (from the Cabinet Information Bureau's labour statistics of female workers in factories coming under the Factory Law).

The only increase is in April, '42 (due I think to accessions of graduates), so the real effect of the war appears to be the withdrawal of women from the labour front, and the idea that they can fill the labour void is merely ludicrous.

As for Koreans, the authorities talk of bringing them over, but no definite decision has yet been made, so this good source is no help at present.

The value of women as a source is similarly non-existent so long as there is no conscription enforced by the authorities.

Yet the labour question must be solved and one new source has been found, prisoners of war and ordinary prisoners. The size and manageability of the former source are problematical and render it of doubtful value, but there has been to my knowledge one case where the latter proved useful. I refer to the group of 40 paroled prisoners who have been doing valuable work since December 8th, 1942, in a certain Tokyo shipyard. The latter source is worth investigation.

I think myself China coolies are a big future source of labour supply. The authorities attach importance to this; but as officials visited the recruiting centres at the end of December, 1942, I must not anticipate their report which is imminent, but will only re-iterate my firm conviction of the value of this source.

3. Committee for Increase of Production.

This was established as a Cabinet committee on 27th November, 1942, local liaison councils of the government agencies involved being established at the same time, to provide immediate decisions. Those of us in the production field know well what a big acceleration of production could follow from unified decisions by government agencies. It is to be hoped the committee will see that labour adequacy is obtained by attention to those other aspects which are as important as the number of workers, namely distribution of housing, clothing, food and the other essentials of life.

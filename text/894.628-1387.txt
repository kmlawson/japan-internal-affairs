REPORT FROM ALASKA FISHERY INVESTIGATIONS REPORT FOR FISCAL YEAR 1942

Project 1: Investigation of the Salmon Resources in Bristol Bay.

This investigation deals with the greatest red salmon producing area in the world, with an average pack during the past decade of 1,150,000 cases of 48 one pound cans per case. Being a resource of such great economic value, it has been intensively prosecuted, and must be constantly and closely supervised to avoid depletion.

Broadly speaking, the purpose of the Bristol Bay salmon investigations is to determine the background necessary for the proper regulation of the catch so as not only to maintain the high level yields of the present cyclic years of good production, but also to augment the runs in cyclic years of low abundance. In order that this broad purpose may be accomplished, it is necessary to have a knowledge of the life pattern of the species both in their fresh water environment and during their sojourn in the ocean. The studies conducted on each phase of the problem are discussed separately. Mr. J. T. Barnaby is in charge of this work, with Mr. G. W. Hilsinger, recently appointed as assistant, the only other permanent employee. Mr. Kelez, formerly in charge of the inshore investigations, left for service with the naval forces on May 1, and two former employees, Mr. R. L. Hacker and Mr. C. H. Baltzo also left the investigation, the former by resignation, the latter by transfer to another Division of the Service. Mr. W. C. Sands, who had been in charge of the oceanographic work, was also called into active service in the Navy.

Bristol Bay Oceanographic Survey.

This phase of the program of investigations on the salmon resources of Bristol Bay was undertaken to establish the relation of the hydrographic conditions in the waters of Bristol Bay and in the waters of the adjacent Bering Sea to the ocean phase in the life history of the salmon of the region. This work has been conducted through a cooperative arrangement with the Coast Guard

=== Page 3 === (confidence 1.00)
which made available to the Service the vessel "Redwing" from which operations could be carried on.

The survey in the summer of 1941 began on May 23, but was terminated on June 2 because of the recalling of that vessel for duty with the naval forces. For the short period the vessel was available during the season, however, a number of stations were run between Unalaska and the Pribilof Islands, and to the north and west of the Islands.

The data on oxygen, chlorinity, phosphate, nitrate, and silicate content so obtained, together with similar data obtained in preceding years, were analyzed, and a draft of the preliminary report on this survey, from its inception to date, was prepared. This work, which has been carried on under the direction of Mr. Walter C. Sands, was necessarily suspended when Mr. Sands was called to active duty in the Navy shortly after the first of the year, and no further operations on this important program are anticipated for the duration of the war.

Bristol Bay Offshore Investigations.

These studies, begun in 1939 and continued in 1940 and 1941, comprise an important part of the investigational program. Their purpose is to delineate the range of the salmons in the waters of Bristol Bay and in the contiguous waters of the Bering Sea, as well as to obtain a measure of the relative abundance of these species and their availability in the coastal and offshore waters through which they pass on their spawning migration.

This involves the operation of various types of fishing gear of standardized design at stations throughout the range of the species during their spawning migration, in order to obtain comparable data on abundance and availability throughout the region.

=== Page 4 === (confidence 1.00)
The vessel "American Star" was chartered during the 1941 season for this work, and operations were carried on from Unimak Pass westward more than 500 miles along the Aleutian chain to Petrol Bank (just north of Semisopochnoi Island in the Bering Sea), and from Cape Mordvinof adjacent to Unimak Pass, 300 miles north and west to Cape Mohican on Nunivak Island and from this point south and west on a line to the Pribilof Islands, a distance of some 200 miles.

The gear used in the 1941 operations consisted of 700 fathoms of 5 1/2 inch linen gillnetting, together with 225 fathoms of 4 inch, and 100 fathoms of 2 1/2 inch cotton webbing. These several sections were hung to form a net 6,150 feet in length and 42 feet in depth. This gear was set out at designated stations throughout the area covered, and based on the number of salmon taken at the various locations, it becomes evident that:

(a) Salmon may be taken with this type of gear in all of the waters explored.

(b) Salmon are not abundant in the waters west of the Islands of Four Mountains, which lie due south of the Pribilofs on the Aleutian chain.

(c) Red salmon, while relatively scarce in the area between Nunivak and St. Matthew Islands, are present in some abundance in the vicinity of the Pribilofs. However, chum salmon are present in these more western waters, probably from stocks originating in the watershed between the Kuskokwim and Yukon Rivers.

(d) Within the limits of the continental shelf salmon are everywhere present, in some localities in abundance sufficient to warrant commercial operations.

(e) Contrary to popular belief, the Bristol Bay salmon do not all follow a fixed migration pattern. It has been generally accepted that these fish migrate only by way of Unimak Pass to the waters south of the Alaska Peninsula,

3

that they spend the greater part of their ocean residence in these waters,
to return by way of the same Pass at maturity enroute to their spawning grounds.
The taking of salmon between the Islands of Four Mountains and the Pribilofs
indicates that some of the salmon undoubtedly enter the Bering Sea through passes
in the Peninsula west of Unimak, and it seems likely that many of the Bristol
Bay salmon never leave the Bering Sea at all, but remain north of the Alaska
Peninsula throughout their ocean residence.

Because of the difficulties of obtaining a suitable vessel and the equipment
required for the work, and because the area of operation lies so closely
adjacent to a probable war zone, this offshore investigation has been abandoned
for the duration of the war, and no operations were carried on during the 1942
season.

Bristol Bay Inshore Investigations.

These inshore investigations deal with the host of problems involved in
a full understanding of the fresh water history of the red salmon. These include
an appraisal of the "run" - the catch as well as the escapement - of the spawning
adults in each of the five major river systems which comprise the Bristol Bay
watershed, and an estimate of the effective fecundity of the spawners in each
system, together with an appraisal of the effect of the various factors which
are responsible for the differences in the survival of the young between different
brood years. The first of these presents a direct problem of management -
the catch must be governed by the need of an adequate spawning escapement if the
runs are to be perpetuated - and the adequacy of escapement can be ascertained
only by observation on the grounds. In connection with this problem of providing
for adequate escapement, it is necessary to have knowledge of the interrelation
of the several populations entering the river so as to provide for an adequate
seeding of all parts of the watershed by controlling the time at which the catch

4

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
as made. This requires a full knowledge, not only of the differences which exist between populations in different reaches of the system, but of the time at which each passes through the commercial fishery. Without this knowledge some sections of the watershed may be afforded unnecessary protection - and so reduce the optimum production level - while other sections may be understocked, with equal or even greater loss in the future productive capacity of the system.

The problem of appraising the degree of success attending a spawning so as to ascertain the extent of the mortality which depredates the larvae to a small fraction of their original numbers before they have attained a sufficient maturity to migrate to the sea as fingerlings, is one most urgently repairing solution.

A prerequisite to the understanding and interpretation of data on escapement and on survival during fresh water existence is a knowledge of the age composition of the stocks in each of the river systems. Such data are of especial importance in promulgating sound regulations for future years, because, unless the age of the fish in the run be known, it is impossible to tell when the progeny from the spawning escapement will return as adults. Without this knowledge, of course, the appraisal of spawning intensity and its success would have little value.

During the 1941 spawning season ground survey parties covered the accessible portions of the five major river systems, and the findings of these parties were supplemented by aerial surveys of the remote and inaccessible regions. From these reports it has been established that the escapements of spawning adults were inadequate in the Nushagak, the Illiamna and the Egegik systems, that they were fair in the Naknek River, and good in the Ugashik. These findings

5

=== Page 7 === (confidence 1.00)
will be of great value in establishing the yield to be expected from these various river systems in the future when the progeny from this spawning, in their turn, reenter the rivers to spawn.

At the rapids in the Naknek River 78,000 seaward migrants were marked by fin removal in order to determine the mortality during the period spent in the ocean, and also to determine to what extent these salmon return to their home stream. The results of this experiment must await the return of these marked individuals as adults in two to four years.

In the program of determining the mortality occurring between the depositing of the eggs by the adults and the departure of the seaward migrants, a field station at Brooks Lake on the Naknek River system is being established. Here a weir to permit the counting of the spawning adults into the lake was constructed and operated, and a count of 124,000 individuals established as being the spawning stock. The installation of a weir designed to permit the counting of the progeny of this escapement at the time of their migration to the sea is contemplated, which will make available the percentage loss of the progeny from this escapement during the fresh water phase of their life history. Climatological data were maintained at this locality throughout the field season as a means of appraising causes for the degree of success of this spawning. In order fully to understand the varying meteorological and hydrographic factors and their influence on the survival of the young salmon, it will be necessary to maintain such observations throughout the year. To accomplish this a field station of a type to provide facilities suited to the rigors of this northern climate must be available. Accordingly the construction of such a station was undertaken, and the foundation and much of the main structure was completed during the season.

The scales so collected were mounted, and the ages read during the winter period. Because of the large number of readings required to adequately sample these complex river systems, this analysis assumes large proportions. In addition to this, morphological data for analysis of population differences were collected for future analysis.

My dear Mr. Jackson:

I wish to refer to the salmon fishery survey made in the Bristol Bay area by your Department beginning in 1938 and continuing for two or three years. You will recall that the survey was instituted in order to obtain data on the salmon fishery which this Department might require in connection with Japanese fishing operations in Alaskan waters.

As our records do not indicate that we have received a full report on the results of the survey, I should appreciate it if you would supply us with whatever material you have available in order that it may be utilized in connection with current attention being given to questions involving coastal fisheries.

NEW HAVEN, CONNECTICUT

October 18, 1944

Mr. Leo D. Sturgeon
Fisheries Section
Department of State
Washington, D. C.

Dear Mr. Sturgeon:

I am writing to ask if you could possibly come to Yale for a day and lecture to the officers in our Civil Affairs Training School on the subject of fishing and fisheries in Japan. The students in the School, as you may know, are preparing for military government duties in Japan. They attend a six-months course here, during three months of which they are given a very systematic series of lectures on every aspect of Japanese living, economic and social habits, political institutions, etc. One of the most important of these topics is that of fisheries, and we would be very glad if you could spare the time to present this to them in a general lecture followed by an informal discussion. It would fit most appropriately into our plans if we could schedule this lecture on Tuesday, December 15, although we could adjust the date to suit your convenience.

Mr. Frank Williams of the State Department and Mr. Wolf I. Ladejinski of the Office of Foreign Agricultural Relations, U. S. Department of Agriculture, are also contributing lectures on various aspects of the Japanese economy. I very much hope you will find it possible to talk to the School on the vitally important subject of the Japanese fishing industry. I will be glad to send you further details as soon as I hear from you.

Sincerely yours,

A. Whitney Griswold
Director

MEMORANDUM
September 1, 1942.

At the request of Commander Miller, a summary of the information available concerning Japanese fishing boats operating out of the port of Santos is given below.

A letter of December 10, 1941, Serial No. 41615 addressed to His Excellency Vice Almirante Américo Vieira de Mello and signed by Rear Admiral A.T. Beauregard is the first record concerning this subject. Pertinent paragraph of this letter is quoted below:

"It is therefore my duty to request of Your Excellency that an adequate supervision, inspection and control of these Japanese fishing craft and other floating craft that may be under Japanese ownership, be insured in order to protect American shipping when within the harbors of Brazilian jurisdiction. Your Excellency will know in which ports such measures are particularly applicable. Recent events reveal that more than normal precautions are necessary to safeguard American shipping that is so vital to both the interests of the United States and of Brazil."

Our records indicate that on January 14, 1942, the American Vice Consul at Santos reported that there were 42 fishing vessels in the Colonia de Pescadores Z-1 José Bonifácio, owned by Japanese who are either Brazilian born or naturalized. Of these, 38 are diesel powered, and 4 gasoline powered. On March 7, 1942, a telegram from the American Consul in São Paulo to the Embassy in Rio, stated that plans were being made by the Brazilian authorities to cancel the Brazilian citizenship of those who were Japanese by birth and engaged in the fishing industry.

On March 20th, the American Consul at Santos reported that the Captain of the Port has issued orders that all fishing vessels must obtain a Passe de

de Saida before leaving port. Their operations were confined to a radius of twelve miles from their Colonias or operating bases, and they were not to remain at sea for more than 36 hours. All fishing vessels, irrespective of their owners' nationality, are searched when leaving or returning by representatives of the Guarda Moria stationed at a Posto Fiscal at Ponta da Praia.

On April 2, the American Consul at Santos reported that the Captain of the Port had issued instructions prohibiting Japanese nationals or Japanese who were naturalized Brazilians, from going to sea, in their fishing boats. This order does not affect the sons of Japanese nationals who were born in Brazil.

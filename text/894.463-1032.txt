Subject: American Independence Day.

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
WASHINGTON.

SIR:

I have the honor to report that on July 4, 1931, the anniversary of American Independence Day, I held an official reception at the Taiwan Railway Hotel which was attended by the Acting Director General of Administration and other high officials of the Taiwan Government General, the Governor and high officials of the Taihoku Prefectural Government, the Mayor and ranking officials of the Taihoku Municipal Government, the leading banking and business men of Taiwan, the Consular Corps, and by the majority of the foreign residents in Taihoku, over 70 persons in all.

The reception was held between the hour of 11.30 a.m. and noon, the Acting Director General making a short speech of congratulation and felicitation with a toast to the President of the United States, to which I responded with assurances of appreciation and expressions of pleasure in the very cordial relations of the past and the harmonious cooperation of the present, this being followed by a toast to the Emperor of

## Translation

Washington Bicentennial Service Held in Nagoya.

A Washington Bicentennial Memorial Service was held at St. John's Church, Higashi-Kataha machi, Higashi ku, Nagoya, on February 21st, at 3:45 P.M. The American and Japanese flags and Washington's portrait were hung over the pulpit. The American Consul and more than fifty local resident Americans were present. Mr. Jackson and Dr. McAlpine conducted the service. Hymns were sung and the service was closed with fond memories of the Great Man.

(From the "Nagoya Shimbun", February 22, 1932.)

I have the honor to inform the Department that on July 4, 1932, the anniversary of Independence Day, I held an official reception at the Taiwan Railway Hotel. It has been customary at this Consulate to hold such a function on Independence Day and, as in the past, the reception was attended by ranking officials of the Taiwan Government General, military and naval authorities, the Taiwan Consular corps, prominent business men of Taihoku both Japanese and Chinese, and most of the foreign community.

A short speech of felicitation was delivered by the Director General, which was translated into English by the Secretary-Translator of the Government General, to which I replied with a brief address. Mr. Matsuo of this Consulate translated my speech into Japanese, after which I proposed the health of the President of the United States.

The reception received favorable newspaper comment, and the Japanese flag was flown from the Government General building on July fourth, as a special mark of respect to the United States.

(Copy of Speech Delivered by Consul John B. Ketcham on July 4, 1932, at Independence Day Official Reception)

Your Excellency and Gentlemen:

I want to thank you all for the honor you have done me by your presence here to-day, and you, your Excellency, for your very kind words of felicitation.

On this one hundred and fifty-seventh anniversary of the birth of the United States as an independent nation, my country together with all other nations of the world is facing a grave and difficult situation.

The United States, in the course of its history, has met and overcome many a crisis, and I am confident that, through international co-operation, the problem confronting us all will eventually be solved. Recent history has shown that every nation is inter-dependent on the others, and it is only through mutual understanding and good will that each nation may prosper.

It is a source of pride to all Americans that we feel that the United States has perhaps contributed in some small measure to the marvelous development of modern Japan. Ever since the visit of Commodore Perry to Japan in 1853 the relations between our two countries have been of the closest, and to-day, as we face the future, I feel sure that the ties of friendship and commerce will become firmer than ever.

On behalf of my government and of the Americans resident in Taiwan, I again thank you for coming here to-day.

THE HONORABLE

WASHINGTON.

SIR:

As required by Section 438, Note 1, of the Consular Regulations, I have the honor to transmit herewith a copy of the brief remarks made at the flag-raising ceremony at this Consulate on the morning of July Fourth.

The short ceremony (the Japanese national hymn, brief remarks, the flag-raising and the "Star Spangled Banner) was attended by most of the American residents of Nagasaki and by a number of Japanese officials. One of the latter stated that he had been deeply impressed by the ceremony as he had not known that Americans were patriotic! Buffet refreshments were served in the residence quarters after the ceremony.

Following the example of the Embassy at Tokyo, no formal reception was held for Japanese officials and the consular corps.

The Nagasaki American Association held a picnic supper for its members in the evening.

Respectfully yours,

Ralph Cory

Remarks of Vice Consul Ralph Cory at Flag-Raising Ceremony, July 4, 1938, at American Consulate, Nagasaki, Japan.

On this 162nd anniversary of our national independence it is well that we who are so far removed from our country and from its institutions should unite in this group expression of renewed loyalty.

We are not unmindful of the privilege of living in this land, and of friendship with its people. We are deeply grateful for that friendship, and for the innumerable courtesies which are extended to us. Such friendships, and our study of Japanese history and culture have, we believe, promoted in no small degree that good-will and mutual understanding among nations which all men desire. We are particularly grateful for the presence here this morning of so many official and private representatives of this nation.

Today we recall to mind that memorable July 4th, 1776, when the Continental Congress issued its historic Declaration "That these United Colonies are, and of Right ought to be, Free and Independent States". We remember that that Declaration concluded with the solemn pledge: "For the support of this Declaration, with a firm reliance on the protection of Divine Providence, we mutually pledge to each other our lives, our Fortunes, and our sacred Honor".

Let us remember also the years of struggle before independence was accomplished, the union of the 13 war-torn states under the Constitution, the long years of development, of westward expansion, of civil war and reunion, and our subsequent progress in freedom, unity, and democracy. And, in this day of confusions, let us also remember that national interests, and the superior claims of the nation, transcend all personal and class interests.

With the same firm reliance on the protection of divine Providence, with the same readiness to pledge our lives, our fortunes, and our sacred honor that the nation may continue to be free, united, and democratic, let us, therefore, "strive on to...do all which may achieve and cherish a just and lasting peace among ourselves and with all nations".

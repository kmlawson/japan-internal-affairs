4. The price of rice:
Government's retrenchment policy.

The fall in the price of rice brought about considerable discussion of aid to the farmers. On March 20th the Rice Investigation Commission drafted a plan for submission to the Government with respect to the adjustment of the demand, supply, and price of rice, supplementary to the Rice Law already in operation. No decision with respect to this plan had been arrived at by the end of the month. The principal suggestions of the Commission were as follows: that the Government shall, when the fixing of standard prices of rice is deemed urgent, promptly investigate and decide upon maximum and minimum standard prices of rice necessary for the operation of the Rice Law; that encouragement shall be given to warehouses for agricultural products, and that monetary accommodations shall be extended to them at a low rate of interest; that the Government General of Chosen shall formulate promptly a proper policy for the adjustment of shipments of Korean rice to Japan Proper so that the equilibrium of the supply of rice each month may be maintained; and that a system shall be inaugurated to obtain control and supervision of the export and import of rice.

Export of Gold Specie.

During November there was widespread interest in the possibility of a reimposition of an embargo on the export from Japan of gold specie and agitation in favor of such a step increased. In November 10th the Diet members of the Opposition party met and passed a resolution advocating an immediate prohibition of free export of gold; certain newspapers, the Tokyo JIJI in particular, published editorials denouncing the Government's adherence to the policy of free export; Mr. Sanji Auto's book, which is strongly in favor of a gold embargo, entered the class of "best seller", 600,000 copies having been reported sold during a period of less than six weeks; a number of members of the Tokyo Chamber of Commerce announced themselves in favor of an embargo.

Several factors affecting adversely Japan's financial position helped to influence public opinion in favor of such

such a step. The gold reserve had decreased by November 30th to Yen 542,716,000, about half of what it was when the embargo was removed on January 11, 1930. The Japanese military action in Manchuria continued to be a drain on the Government's financial resources, while the resultant intensified Chinese boycott and interruption of communications in Manchuria adversely affected Japanese foreign trade which was already depressed. Japan's foreign trade continued to suffer also from the decline in the value of sterling. The delay of the Finance Minister in balancing the budget added to the widespread feeling that some stringent financial measure might be necessary.

These various factors, however, did not seem to affect the Finance Minister in his policy of free export of gold specie. On November 15th, for example, he made a speech at Osaka in which he showed no inclination to return to the gold embargo, explaining that no significant outflow of gold would be required in settlement of the adverse balance of trade.

# THE RICE PROBLEM.

1. For fully three decades, ending in 1894, Japan had a well sustained period of export surplus in rice, interrupted only at wide intervals by seasons of import excess, brought about by short crops in Japan proper. (1) Records show that Japan during this period figured as an export nation in this commodity. Throughout the next 38 years, however, barring the single year 1930, this country has been a heavy buyer of the cereal, with a balance of trade consistently unfavorable, even in those years when bumper harvests were raised. The tendency toward this import excess during the 17 years ending in 1932 was more marked than during the 21-year period ending in 1915. During these 38 years Japan had an import balance in rice aggregating 10,380,000 tons which, if calculated on the basis of Yen 120 per ton, represented foreign payments amounting to the stupendous figure of Yen 1,250,000,000.

2. This unfavorable balance has never been ascribable to any reduction in domestic production of rice. During the 30-year period beginning in 1865, production was steadily expanding from an original figure of 5,400,000 tons a year to one of 5,500,000. During the succeeding 21 years production gains increased to 7,800,000 tons, and then expanded to the neighborhood of 11,000,000 tons in 17 years more. During this latter period rice culture in both Chosen and Taiwan made remarkable progress, so that those lands are now shipping into Japan proper 1,500,000 tons of surplus tonnage every year. A continued inflow of foreign rice on top of the vastly amplified supply derived from home production is attributable to a fast expanding volume of consumption, brought about by an elevation in the living standards and an increase in population, outstripping the increased output of rice.

3. Let us here briefly describe measures which the Government had adopted since 1910 in respect of rice, as this information is essential in gauging the present position and future trend of rice values.

4. Owing to several crop failures in succession, the supply of rice was reduced to its lowest level in 1910 to 1912, when its market price rose to Yen 157 a ton, until that time an unheard of level. The following two or three years, however, saw rice prices suffering a perpendicular decline to almost half the former position, namely, to Yen 87 a ton, the higher prices of the previous two or three years having stimulated production so that supply scarcity no longer prevailed. During seven succeeding years rice was again rising, due to a scanty supply condition, and also to a generally higher level of commodity prices originating in the World War and aggravated by speculative buying. Rice for a time was as high as Yen 380 a ton. Such conditions occasioned many grave social problems, and the Government took steps to prohibit market "cornering" by promulgating the Anti-Profiteering Law. This 10-year period was most eventful from the standpoint of the staple, supply problems and the bitter experiences encountered during this decade of wild price fluctuations having brought home to the mind of the nation the necessity of formulating food policies with an eye to the future.

5. Public opinion was now for establishing a definite national policy for a food supply, with the immediate goal of attaining a position of self-sufficiency, to the relief of both procuder and consumer. In order to enable the Government to regulate rice supply to demand, the authorities in 1919 were empowered to buy or sell rice on its own account by means of the Rice Law. A few years later this Law was so amended that the Government was authorized to take necessary steps for regulating the market price of rice, in addition to quantitative regulation. This regulation proved useful in checking violent up and down movements of rice prices, but public complaints were incessant about the price changes being still too wide.

6. In 1920, the Government amended the Rice Law by establishing a basic price to serve as a guide for regulating the market price. This standard value is calculated and determined by taking into consideration the movement of general commodity prices in comparison with rice. When the market price appreciates more than 20% above this predetermined basic price the Government steps in to release rice from its held tonnage and so fight the rise; when the cereal moves in the opposite direction to the extent of 20% below the basic level, the Government again goes in the market, this time on the buying side. The result of all this has been most satisfactory, and rice quotations have never run out of the price territory defined by the Law.

7. Up to the present the Government measures has always had to do with amplifying a supply which has failed to measure up to demand. In other words, the encouragement of production has been the pivotal principle. When a market stability was assured to their product and the farmers were offered a practical guarantee that, in case of decline, the Government would purchase rice out of their hands, they redoubled their efforts toward production, being aided by improved agricultural methods. The Government distributed improved seeds, brought under cultivation new tracts of land, provided better means of irrigation, and gave many grants. Farmers in Chosen and Taiwan were the first to reap the fruits of Government measures for production increase, which sometimes verged on the extreme, while weather conditions also were favorable for a considerable time. In this manner, rice production in Japan has received an appreciable expansion. Particularly since 1930 has the country been self-supporting. Not only does necessity no longer exist for imported rice, but production exceeds consumption.

8. The Government now had to face another problem, this time overproduction. Prices are necessarily moving downward. While price precipitations have never gone below the 20% line of the basic rate, on the other hand no tendency toward a rise is discernible. Farmers are no longer able to sell their rice except at prices below the cost of production.

9. The popular voice was soon clamoring that for so important a foodstuff as rice its growers should be guaranteed against market decline, at least to the extent of cost of production. Political parties and both Houses of the Diet looked upon this movement with favor, with the result that, at an extraordinary session in 1931, the Legislature approved the motion to revise the Rice Law for the third time since its enactment in April, 1921, and the ensuing deliberations led to the adoption of the Rice Control Law now in operation. The sum and substance of this piece of legislation is that Yen 163.10, which is the cost of production per ton, has been made the minimum official price for rice, and at this limit the Government is authorized to purchase in any amount. At the same time the maximum official price is fixed at Yen 213.50 a ton, and at this level the Government may sell in unlimited quantity when the market in rice is soaring.

10. Beneficient weather conditions prevailing throughout the crop season, improved farming methods, and soil conditioning - all these combined in 1932 to produce another good rice crop. Japan proper harvested in excess of 10,000,000 tons, and the exportable surplus in Chosen and Taiwan assumed the proportions of 2,000,000 tons, which, inclusive of a carry-over from the previous year, represented a prospective surplus supply tonnage of 2,400,000 tons above normal consumption needs of 11,000,000 tons. The market was over-run with rice in such a manner that, but for the rice stabilization measures, the market must have been broken so widely that it would be selling at half of the current level. The Government accordingly purchased and segregated from the market 1,800,000 tons, acting on the authority given by the Rice Control Law. Including its holding from previous purchases, the Government has now succeeded in withdrawing from the market 2,100,000 tons of rice. At the same time the farming population is encouraged to store unhulled rice, and such stocks, retired from circulation, now reach a total of 700,000 tons. The measures under consideration have been singularly successful in restoring confidence to the market, as over-supply has now been eliminated.

11. Ever since the peak season of the crop movement was passed, rice has been steadily advancing, and at present it is holding its ground in the neighborhood of Yen 172 a ton, well above the minimum Government price. As its market is mainly artificially maintained by the execution of Government measures, the so-called first principle of demand and supply is not in operation as in the case of other articles of commerce. To look into the future of the rice market therefore with any amount of accuracy, one will have to surmise the action of the authorities with respect to rice. The provision of the Law requires the Government to make a disposition of 300,000 tons out of its holdings during the balance of the current fiscal year, and then there are 700,000 tons of unhulled rice held by private interests. The time when this tonnage will be released to the market and the manner of doing it will greatly affect the market. It is probable, however, that the Government will not be releasing the 300,000-ton stock for some time to come, as there has been no complaint anywhere against a continued holding of this tonnage, and there is also the fear on the part of the Government that the release of so important a tonnage might break the market, so as to undo much of the good

some thus far. Another danger should be taken into account. In view of the fact that - superficially speaking - an over-supply can no longer exist, values are liable to be buoyed up very near the official maximum of Yen 213,50 should weather conditions damage the crops, or should bull interests be tempted to operate in force. The Government therefore is no longer concerned over possible declines in the rice market, but is worrying now as to whether a sharp rise may not take place, so as to make living conditions difficult for the masses through the high price of this staple. Should there be signs of the present firmness developing into a violent upward rush, the Government will be called upon to take measures for checking it, and the ammunition for doing so will be the 300,000-ton Government stocks, as well as the 700,000 tons of unhiled rice held by the people. No immediate release of these stocks, therefore, is probable; but it is at the same time likely that rice after all will be slowly rising for some time to come in a chastened trading, operators all the while being on the alert lest they be caught in a market reaction following the possible disposal of Government rice.

12. While there are complaints as to certain details in the manner of applying the Rice Control Law, on the whole credit is due the administrators for having lifted prices to the level where producers have been able to make both ends meet and for restoring a well-sustained stability to the market, all in spite of the fact that supply exceeded consumption enormously. The new Law has enabled the long impoverished agrarian community to raise several hundreds of million yen additional income, a dose of nourishment sorely needed by hard-pressed farmers.

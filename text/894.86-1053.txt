SECTION I
INTRODUCTION
Today Japan ranks third in the world in the gross
tonnage of her merchant marines. This rise of Japanese
shipping from fishing and coast-wise traffic by sampan
to a high position among world leaders during the past
80 years is remarkable not only as a business and
engineering feat, but also as an accomplishment in train-
ing efficient personnel. It is the latter with which
this report will deal.
Special attention is devoted here to Tokyo
Nautical College, which in age and prestige is the
ranking institution for training men for the Japanese
merchant marine.
Attention is called to the fact that all govern-
ment schools are devoted to the training of officers.
Two unofficial schools conducted by the Japan Seamen's
Aid Association are the only ones devoted to persons
desiring to become ordinary seamen.
Materials for the report are Japanese language
publications and interviews.

Section II
LEGAL BACKGROUND
With Particular Reference to Tokyo Nautical
College
The general legal provisions for training schools
for seamen are found in Imperial Ordinances, under the
authority of which ordinances of the Ministry of Edu-
cation are made, with further delegations of regulating
powers to prefectural offices and to school authorities.
Imperial Ordinance of March 30, 1935, provides for
technical schools of various types, including merchant
marine schools. The prefectural merchant marine schools
are of this category. Complete discretion is left with
the Ministry of Education in the matter of establishing
school standards. This Ordinance makes reference to the
earlier Imperial Ordinance of January 20, 1928, which
deals with "Semmon Gakko" (schools of college rank),
including "Jitsugyo Semmon Gakko" (Technical or Voca-
tional Colleges), under which latter classification are
placed Tokyo Nautical College and Kobe Nautical College.
Minimum standards are prescribed before delegating
regulating powers to the Minister. Typical of the
Japanese school system, in these Ordinances special
stress is placed on teaching character building and
on the cultivation of ideas in conformity with the
Imperial Constitution.
A third Imperial Ordinance, pertaining to candi-
dates for the naval reserve officer list and promul-
gated October 18, 1934, is of interest here. One
provision

for that list on entry into the Nautical Colleges con-
ducted by the Ministry of Education.
A ministerial ordinance of l93l provides that

Ministry of Education. The educational qualification
of students who have been invited must be approved by

Turning to the Tokyo Nautical College, an Ordinance

TRAINING SCHOOLS
A. TOKYO NAUTICAL COLLEGE
1. Historical Sketch
a. General
The task of. training seamen was given to the
Mitsubishi Company along with a present of most of
the Japanese Government's vessels, in 1875. A yearly
subsidy of ? 15,000 was granted, and the training
school, a private institution, was to be placed under
the supervision of the Communications Bureau of the
Home Department. The Mitsubishi Merchant Marine
School opened during the following year under the head-
ship of Rokusaburo Nakamura and with a faculty of four,
including one Dutch and one English professor. Fourty-
four students were selected from l80 applicants.
The School had two sections, a five year course
for high seas and a three year course for coastal
shipping. Entrance examinations covered physical,
reading, writing and mathematics for both, and English
conversation and reading for the high seas department.
Promising students could borrow funds for school ex-
penses. All were forbidden to terminate their studies
voluntarily except by special permission, a rule still
in effect. Beginning in l877 there was a department
for engineer officers as well as deck officers. There
were facilities for both sea and shore training.
Translations of foreign trade terts were used during
the early years.

Due to shortage of men, short-term schools were
opened in Osaka and Hakodate and a one-year course
was added at the Mitsubishi School to train personnel
quickly. Applicants had to have at least four year's
experience at sea. The Seamen's Aid Association took
over this branch of the Mitsubishi School in 1881.
In 1882 the School was taken over by the Ministry
of Agriculture and Commerce and re-named Tokyo Merchant
Marine School, and from the following year graduates
became second mates and engineers without government
examination.
The School was transferred to the Ministry of
Communications in 1885 and named Merchant Marine School,
which name it bore until it was placed under its present
Ministry and name in l925, except for the short period
of 1890-1896 when it was known as Tokyo Merchant Marine
Schoo1.
During 1889-1891 the Osaka and Hakodate Schools
became branches of the Tokyo School, were assigned the
three year and the one year courses, and were given
a special department to train sailors for entrance
examinations of the Merchant Marine School. Both were
abolished in 190l when the Department of Education
established district schools. In that year arrange-
ments were made for local preliminary examinations,
additional facilities being provided in 1916 and 1930.
Stimuli to shipping from the wars in which Japan
participated led to successive expansions of the School,
whioh

=== Page 9 === (confidence 0.99)
- 7 -
which has tried to adjust its enrollment to the demand
for officers.
From 192l all students have been on a paid scholar-
ship basis, with the obligation of serving in work
specified by the School for six years after graduation.
In 1925 the Ministry of Education was put in
charge of the work and the institution was given the
name Tokyo Nautical College.
The great earthquake of l923 virtually destroyed
the School properties, but the training ship Meiji
Maru with 6oo refugees escaped. The School soon re-
opened, and its present plant was finished by l932.
Dormitories were later acquired in various ports.
A one-year course was added in l937 for non-
graduate experienced seamen who wished to secure First
or Second Mates' or Engineers' papers.
There have been over 4,500 graduates, of whom
over 3,300 are now serving at sea.
b. Foreign Students
In 1895 a special department was created to pre-
pare young Koreans for entry into the School. In 1905
the first Chinese were admitted, and, finally, in l934,
two "Manchukuoan" students were accepted, with the
stipulation that they were to serve in naval work of
their country.
c. Naval Reserve Status
In l884 graduates became naval reservists, and
special courses in naval matters were added to the
curriculum.

curriculum. Provision for naval gunnery and engineering
training was soon made. From l904 students became
student reservists on entrance. Increasing stress has
been put on this phase of the work, and by 1933 a six
month period in active reserve training was required.
Since l934 graduates have been able to go directly on
the active list, and many are serving during the present
incident." A special unit was created in l896 to keep
up interest. Reservists may rise to the rank of captain
in both deck and engineer branch.
d. Training Ships
Many vessels have served as training ships for this
institution. At first they were Mitsubishi ships, then
those of other companies were used as well. By 1892
the practice of borrowing company vessels was abandoned,
and one was purchased for school use. In l898 the first
vessel was built especially for this purpose, but soon
was lost with all on board during a storm. The Taisei
Maru, now in use, was built in 1904. Sailing vessels
have been used, even the Taisei Maru using a motor
only for auxilliary purposes.

3. Students
a. Entrance
Entry into Tokyo Nautical College is by selective
examination, the strictness of which may be judged from
the fact that in 1936 only 120 out of 935 applicants were
successful.
To qualify for examination, a young man must have
a middle school education or the equivalent and be 20
years of age or younger.
Provision has been made whereby the principal part
of the examination is given in certain designated places
in addition to Tokyo. This part of the examination con-
sists of a physical examination of Naval Academy Stand-
ards and by naval doctors, an oral examination before a
board of three, and a written examination which stresses
English, Mathematics and Japanese of Middle School gra-
duate standards. Survivors become probationary students,
who before final acceptance must pass a second rigorous
physical

physical examination at Tokyo by School physicians.
The only charge for the entire 5t year course
is an entry fee of ¥200.00*, which may be paid in
small installments.
b. School Life
The long daily routine of the students is as follows:
Before breakfast there is a short period devoted to
practice in giving commands and calisthenics, after which
the students must clean their own quarters. Then comes
breakfast, followed by a short rest period for reading,
letter writing, etc. After this there is a roll-call,
and at 8:l0 the seven hours of school begin. When
classes are over each student must devote an hour to
a sport of his own selection. Then supper and a period
of relaxation in the recreation hall before commencing
three hours of home-work. Before retiring, all must go
out of doors for a breath of fresh air.
Saturday afternoons and Sundays are free, and
holidays and vacations give an additional leisure
of about 2t months. There is a year-end vacation from
December 25 to January 7 and a summer vacation from
July 21 to September l0. Usually the first ten days
of the summer vacation are devoted to cruises on small
sailing boats for deck department students and to
practical factory work for those of the engine depart-
ment.

5. Graduation and Promotion
Upon the completion of the 5t years of study, of
naval training, and of practical training (See Section
IV), students return to the college for final examina-
tion, after which they receive "A" Class 2nd Mates'
and 2nd Engineers' Certificates.
Graduates of Tokyo Nautical College are promoted
physical
to Captain and Chief Engineer subject only to ay exami-
nation, after completing stipulated years of service at
sea.
6. Special One Year Course
A special one-year course recently has been added
at Tokyo Nautical College. This course is for the
benefit of experienced seamen who wish to secure first
or second Mates' or Engineers' papers, and are not
graduates of the college. Those who wish to enroll
must survive a selective examination. Courses are
designed to meet individual needs and to give prepara-
tion for government examinations.

B. KOBE NAUTICAL COLLEGE
The College was founded as a private school in
1917 under the name of Kawasaki Merchant Marine School,
to train high-class seamen. Three years later it was
placed under the Department of Education and given its
present name and college status.
Kobe Nautical College is of the same rank, although,
due to its youth, perhaps has less prestige than Tokyo
Nautical College. Since it has the same standards and
offers similar facilities for training young mariners,
no separate description of them is necessary.
To date there have been about 1,600 graduates,
evenly divided between the deck and the engine depart-
ments. As in the case of Tokyo Nautical College, gra-
duates are promoted subject only to a physical exami-
nation to the rank of Captain and Chief Engineer upon
the completion of stipulated years at sea.

D. OSAKA SEAMEN'S TRAINING SCHOOL
The Osaka Seamen's Training School was established
in l9l7 by the Osaka Prefectural Government for the
purpose of preparing experienced seamen for government
examinations for promotion to higher rank. In l923
it was raised to the status of a technical school by
the Ministry of Education, and in 1930 a Section for
training "B" Class personnel was added to the original
"A" Class groups. Tuition and other charges are low.
Dormitory facilities are available if desired.
There are three courses given, to which a total
of 300 students are admitted:
(1) "A" Class deck officers:
(a) To become captain.
(b) To become lst mate.
(c) To become 2nd mate.
(a) Post graduate course.
(2) "A" Class engineer officers:
(a) To become chief mate.
(b) To become lst mate.
(c) To become 2nd mate.
(3) "B" Class officers:
(a) To become lst mate.
(b) To become 2nd engineer on steam
vessels.
(c) To become 2nd engineer on motor
vessels.
For courses (1) and (2) the term is one year of
two six-month semesters, except that no term is fixed
for (1) (d); for (3) the course is six months.

E. JAPAN SEAMEN'S AID ASSOCIATION
The Association was formed in l880 to aid and to
train seamen. It is with the latter phase of the work
that the present report is concerned, and a brief
summary of it appears below:
(1) Training in seamanship.
Schools have been established in Yokohama and Kobe
to train inexperienced young men in seamanship. Two
month courses of a practical nature are provided for
would-be sailors and engine workers. Board, lodging
and training are given free of charge. Association
figures show that up until 1931 80,000 men had been
given instruction.
(2) Training for officer's certificates.
The Association seeks to encourage ordinary personnel
to rise in rank, and for that purpose has opened schools
to prepare them for government examinations. Schools
are located in Kobe, Kure, Moji and Sasebo. By l931
over 10,O0o Association trained men had had pasoed deck
or engine officer's examinations. The length of course
and subjects are regulated to meet the needs of the
individual.
Neither type of school is organized under regular
government education ordinances.

B. SHIPYARDS, ENGINE DEPARTMENT
Arrangements have been made with naval and private
shipyards for engine department students of both colleges
and prefectural schools who are ready to begin practical
training. Such students have completed their period of
school and of naval training. Efforts are made to send
a student to both a private and a naval shipyards, since
each offers special advantages, but frequently, perhaps
even usually, a change is not practicable. The ideal
outline of the year's training is as follows:
1. Two months in the blacksmith shop,
handling steel and iron.
2. One month working with copper, -
welding, brazing, etc.
3. Three months of machine shop work.
4. Two to three months of boiler shop
work.
5. Three to four months doing repair and
other work in ship engine rooms.
Generally speaking, students who go to private
shipyards spend most of their time and get their best
experience in (5), while at the navy yards (1) through
(4) takes up most of their time and offers the best
training. The difference is due to the facilities
available at the two types of yards.
Students do actual work, not merely observation.

C. PRIVATE COMPANY VESSELS,
BOTH DEPARIMENTS
For the final year of education both deck and
engine department students are placed on board vessels
of private steamship companies. Steamship lines re-
ceiving mail subsidies are oblidged by law to accept
such students, but all companies are glad to have them
on board because they are usually willing workers and
are paid very small salaries.
While on board company vessels students have what
might be termed a sub-officer status. That is, they
are over the men but under the regular officers, who
supervise and assist them as much as possible. They
are given practical and regular duties, and an effort
is made to have them devote time to all phases of work
done in their respective departments.
The type of vessel - steam or diesel - is of no
great importance in the case of deck students, but
engine students are given as great a variety of ships
as possible. They never serve more than six months
on board diesel engine vessels, but some spend their
entire year on vessels with different types of steam
engines since this is considered the more important.

MISCELLANEOUS INFORMATION AND OPINIONS
A. SCHOOLS FAVORED BY STEAMSHIP LINES
The most highly favored school is Tokyo Nautical
College. This is only natural, in view of its long
and successful existence. It seems safe to say that
at the present time at least 80 percent of the captains
and chief engineers of the leading steamship lines are
graduates of this institution. Most of the balance
are prefectural school graduates. The fact that Kobe
Nautical College is as yet hardly represented is ex-
plained by its comparative youth and the circumstance
that about 15 years at sea are necessary before a
deck officer obtains an important captaincy. It takes
about one or two more years to reach a corresponding
chief engineer's post due to the fact that more en-
gineers are carried on board. In the lower brackets
there is a larger proportion of prefectural and Kobe
graduates. This is explained partly by the improving
standards of the prefectural schools and consequently
increasing recognition, and by the time element men-
tioned above insofar as the Kobe college is concerned.
Another reason is of course that more positions are
available than at the top. The lesser companies like-
wise desire college men, but due to the competition
from important lines must lean more heavily on the
prefectural schools for personnel. For the same
reason, the relatively small number of men who have

B. COMPARATIVE ABILITY OF GRADUATES
OF THE SEVERAL SCHOOLS
Generally speaking, graduates of the colleges
are definitely superior to those from the prefectural
schools. One explanation is age, the latter being
younger and consequently less mature. Again, prefec-
tural graduates are usually weaker in their knowledge
of foreign languages, a matter of some importance,
especially for deck officers. Thirdly, the prefec-
tural students receive a definitely inferior education
in the fundamentals of science, mathematics, etc. This
is especially important in the case of engineer officers
who must constantly study to keep up with new develop-
ments. Almost no prefectural engineer officers have
reached the top of their profession. The same measuring
stick indicates that deck officers are less handicapped.
C. A NEW TREND
Certain companies are beginning to encourage
promising prefectural graduates and ordinary personnel
to seek promotion in rank through government examina-
tions. To this end, assistance is being extended them
to attend the special courses at Tokyo and Kobe as well
as the Osaka Nautical School. The main reason is a
shortage of officers.
D. ORDINARY SEAMEN
Little or no effort in the line of special pre-
paratory work is made by the steamship lines in behalf
of untrained men who wish to become sailors and engine

room workers. Consequently, the Japan Seamen's Associa-
tion is virtually the only agency for special training
for such men.
While most lines call upon the Japan Seamen's Aid
Association for a good deal of their ordinary personnel,
they have no high opinion of the two months' course
offered by that institution. The course is helpful
to a certain degree, but too short to be of much value.
E. WHY SAILING VESSELS FOR TRAINING SHIPS?
As might be expected, there are differences of
opinion concerning the wisdom of using sailing vessels
for training cruises. Some people, including many in
naval circles, feel that the sailing ship is outmoded
and hence should be abandoned or else the training
period should be shortened with increased time devoted
to regular ships. They feel that the supporters of
the present system over-estimate the value of such
vessels in instilling through necessity a great res-
pect for care in observing currents, winds and other
meteorological phenomena. The majority of the men in
the merchant marine believe that this aspect is suf-
ficiently important to warrant the continuation of the
present plan. As the older men disappear, this argu-
ment may lose ground. However, it does not seem pro-
bable that modern type vessels will be built for school
purposes. Operation expenses would be too great. But
it does seem possible that the future may see a reduc-
tion in the time spent on board the sailing type train-
ing ship.

F. DO GRADUATES CONTINUE WORK AT SEA?
No attempt has been made to secure statistics on
this question, but the general opinion is that the per-
cantage of those leaving the sea for reasons other than
physical disability is very low. This is especially
true of graduates of the colleges. It is perhaps lower
in the case of the engineers than it is for the deck
officers, due in large measure to their greater speci-
alization. A deck officer, for example, is better
qualified to do shore work of various kinds allied with
shipping.
G. IMPROVEMENTS FOR PRESENT SYSTEM
Except for the matter of the sailing vessels, dis-
cussed above, the writer has heard no concrete sugges-
tions for improving the present system, especially of
the colleges. As for the prefectural schools, it is
generally agreed that better teachers and more thorough
courses would be helpful. Most graduates of any of the
schools feel that they could well use more practical
experience, but if a young man is to get a reasonable
early start in his work it is hardly wise to lengthen
the course. To shorten the study period also would
be unwise because of the importance of fundamental
knowledge in an age when new developments in the pro-
fession are constantly arising. More important than
changes in the system is constantly attracting better
young men to a career at sea.

PREFACE
The world has seen with astonishment the enormous advance of
Japanese industry and the expansion of its export trade in recent years.
It is a matter for regret that this enormous expansion has been
attributed to social dumping at the expense of labour over and above
the increase due to the fall of yen price.
The latter is the natural consequence of the gold embargo, as a
result of which the exchange value of the yt" has been settled at a
point of equilibrium governed by international economic forces.
As japan must import from foreign countries most of her materials,
at the present time we are at a disadvantage in purchasing such materials.
Fortunately we produce sufficient foodstuffs within the Empire, and prices
of necessaries for subsistence, therefore, are very little influenced by the
low rate of exchange.
. Consequently the wage rate also has not taken
an upward movement since the gold embargo until the last few months
when we have seen signs of a tendency to rise.
The supposition of social dumping on the part of Japanese industry
and trade depends chiefly upon the comparison of our money wages.
calculated at the present exchange rate, with those of foreign countries.
Such a comparison is quite unscientific and the denunciation of our
industries rests on false premisses.
In the following survey instead of merely making statements and
giving opinions, we intend to analyse our industrial and labour conditions
as scientifically as possible and make clear the causes of the high efficiency
of our industry and the reasonableness of our wage rate and other
conditions of labour.
In connection with this problem, Nagoya City and its district have
the same features in contrast with the whole country, as Japan has in
contrast with foreign countries. Therefore, we have made a special
i

=== Page 34 === (confidence 0.99)
survey of Nagoya and its neighbourhood as exemplifing the problem in
our own district, Aichi Prefecture.
The City of Nagoya, well known from the time of the ancient regime
through its beautiful castle with its two golden dolphins, lies in Aichi
Prefecture and is the third largest city in Japan, having a population of
about one million ; surrounded by a vast, fertile hinterland, equipped with
a ne wly constiucted haibour for forein steamship lines and with a
railway junction of three trunk lines.
The natural situation of Nagoya has been favourable to the development
of communications and a varied range of new industries and has enabled
the city to spread out freely until it has become the centre of
communication and commerce both by land and sea.
The rapid expansion of industry and trade in Nagoya is now
astonishing Japan, just as that of Japanese industry and trade is causing
a sensation in the whole world. The explanation of these events is
given in this survey.
In closing this preface we wish to express our deep thanks to the
Bureau of Business Research in the Nagoya Imperial College of Commerce
for the compilation and publication of this volume.

living. Although the standard of living in the different nations cannot
be strictly compared owing to the different modes of living, it appears to
be lower in Japan than in other civilised nations; that is the unavoidable
fate of the entire Japanese nation overcrowded in such a narrow space
with fewer materials and scantier arable lands. The only hope for Japan
to improve its standard of living lies in the extension of industrialisation
and export through the peculiar advantages of our industries, and through
rationalisation adapted to their peculiarities, and through the abundant
supply of industrial labour.

III. RATIONALISATION.
Rationalisation of industries in Japan goes on in two directions; one
direction is to cut down cost of production through mechanisation and
inventions of new processes of production. Another is to control
production through trade associations.
How the efficiency of Japanese industries has increased in this
century is shown in Table 15, in comparison with figures in the United
States of America, whose industries are supposed to be the most rationalised
in the world.
 The physical volume of production per worker employed
in Japan has increased far faster than that in the United States. This
fact shows the vitality of the Japanese industries just now reaching full age
after a long period of adolescence.
Concerning the cotton weaving industry, we have the following figures,
Table 16, which shows the enormous increase in productive efficiency, more
than doubling the number of looms per operative during past ten years.
As regards the spinning industry, Table 17, shows us the same increase

IV. CONDITIONS OF LABOUR.
As already mentioned, the pressure of over-population in Japan makes
it difficult to raise the standard of living in general. Moreover the
workers, excepting those in the large scale factories, do not receive high
pay and live a comparatively low standard life, but most of the employers
also of the small scale works make only moderate gains and live plainly.
In the smaller scale works the family system prevails and the relation
between employers and employees is personal and not regulated by the
idea of legal right and duty.
. Of course, this system does not develop
fully in the large factory, where the division between capitalist and work
people has come to be dearly marked, as the scale of factories becomes
larger. But these factories have made good arrangements for the
well-being of the workers. There are hospitals, cinema theatres, tennis
courts and base-ball play-grounds etc., for the amusement and health of
the workers. Most of the workers, especially females are young and
unmarried, so they are also educated and receive culture in the educational
institutions attached to factories, and in the course of two or three years
they get the knowledge necessary for a house wife, while saving a fair
amount of money for marriage, etc.

V. CONCLUSION
In this survey we have seen the special conditions of industry and
labour in Japan illustrated by those in the Nagoya district. -As the
situation of the latter is to Japan so is that of Japan to the whole world.
As the background of the problem, the overpopulation in Japan in relation
to her small arable area, the scanty supplies of raw materials and the
severity of life in farming districts owing to the enormous increase of rice
production in Korea and Formosa, has been stated.
In consequence of such a state of things, it is necessary for Japan to
be industrialised just as it was for England about one hundred years ago,
owing to the importation of cheap wheat from the continents old and
new. Without industrialisation and exportation of her products, there
is no way for Japan to improve its standard of living.
Our next observation was concerned with the special feature of our
industries, namely their small scale.
This feature comes chiefly from
over-population on the one hand and from the nature of the textile industry,
the predominant one in Japan, and equally so in the Nagoya district,
on the other.
r. Naturally the greater part of the spinning industries,
cotton, silk and wool, is carried on by large factories, but the weaving
industries are mostly conducted on the small scale, as that is suited to the
varieties of the products. This point has a great connection with
labour conditions in Japan and especially in the Nagoya district.
Before entering into the observation of the conditions of labour, the
rationalisation of Japanese iudustries was discussed. As the main causes
of the enormous increase of our productive power we have indicated
(a) the wide diffusion of electric motors; (b) new inventions of
machines and processes of production ; (c) the skill and adaptability to
the new processes of Japanese girls who have, on an average, only three
or four years of experience ; (d) economies derived from the localisation
of industries; (e) the efforts of trade associations through collective
52

=== Page 91 === (confidence 0.99)
measures to improve the quality of products, to cut down the cost of
production and to regulate desperate competition.
Regarding labour conditions, we took special notice of the Japanese
family system which prevails in the factories as well as in the homes of
labourers, and constitutes the special feature of labour conditions. It is.
as a matter of fact, not reasonable to compare only the rates of money
wages in Japan with those in other civilised nations and to judge that the
wage in Japan is lower.
As we have already mentioned, the money wage in a country is
always related to the general condition of living, being influenced by
population, resources, habits and customs in that nation. The family
system in Japan is one of the most important factors among them.
Moreover we defended the reasonableness of comparative low money
wages in Japan, allowing for the special features of our industrial and
labour conditions; the greater number of small scale works and young
female employees owing to the preponderance of textile industries.
These features are most outstanding in the Nagoya district as compared
with other parts of the country.
Although the average wage in Japan has not yet taken an upward
movement, the wage rate and also the real wage in Japan has become
much higher during and after the great war in comparison with that of
other countries, and the standard of living has been very much improved,
which can be seen also from comparing the returns of the surveys of the
household expenses of labourers in various countries. A low money
wage may be a high real wage in Japan, and the same is true particularly
of wages in the Nagoya district.
As for hours of labour, Japan has not yet ratified the Washington
treaty. This is chiefly due to the grcat number of small scale works,
which will be destroyed when a strict limit of hours is applied. But as
a whole, as we have seen, there is a steady tendency towards shorter
hours in Japan.
In short, Japan is now struggling to support an enormous population
53

=== Page 92 === (confidence 1.00)
at a reasonable standard of living through industrialisation and exportation.
But, as we saw. it can not be said that this effort is being pursued at the
cost of labourers, and that therefore the increase of Japanese exports is
due to social dumping.
 Such a judgement arises from a superficial
observation of Japanese labour conditions. If the conditions of Japanese
labourers were really unreasonably bad in comparison with the standard
of living in general. they could not possibly have reached the high state
of efficiency which, as we have shown, they have, in fact, reached.
To maintain and to improve the standard of living in a country,
payment by that country must be, at least equal to what she receives.
Japan has paid more than she has received for a long succession of years.
excepting during the period of the great war. One of the main causes
of the excess payment by Japan was the international gold standard system
which kept the Yo" at a higher value than was represented by the real
strength of the industries. With the suspension of the gold standard.
the value of the Yo" has fallen and rests now at a point at which it really
represents Japanese productive power. In this natural state of things.
the international payments of Japan tend to be balanced in the only way
possible viz. exportation of more goods, for Japan is no investor or
lender to get a surplus of interest and dividends to balance a visible
unfavourable balance of trade.
The increase of Japanese exports
belongs to the natural course of things to restore the equilibrium
whenever it is destroyed. Let Japan but be allowed to export goods as
much as she imports, in accordance with the international division of labour.
If her export is checked by the unnatural measures of foreign countries,
Japan will be compelled to lower her standard of living and consequently
labour conditions. We believe the justice of international policy lies
in the maintenance of an equilibrium of international payments in each
country without lowering its standard of living.

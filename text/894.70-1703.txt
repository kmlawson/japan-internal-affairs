JAPANESE TRANSPORTATION

End of War - At the time of surrender, Japanese transportation facilities were in extremely poor condition. The Merchant Marine was about one-fifth of its prewar strength. Most of the usable vessels had been built during the war to sub-standard specifications and had deteriorated badly due to improper maintenance. The railways suffered little war damage, but poor maintenance and failure to rehabilitate war damage currently, caused serious inefficiency. Municipal transportation was severely disrupted by war damage, with over fifty per cent of the rolling stock totally destroyed or badly damaged. Deterioration of both vehicles and roads, coupled with an extreme shortage of fuel, left highway transportation at very low ebb. The manufacture of transportation equipment and repair parts had fallen far below requirements.

Ownership and Control - Prewar - During prewar years the relationship of transportation and the government was a mixture of control and operation. Marine was privately owned and operated but subsidized and controlled by the government. Municipal transportation was a mixture of city ownership and operation, and private ownership and operation with city control, the major systems being city owned and operated with the exception of the Tokyo Subway, which is privately owned. The war pressure tightened controls on all transportation and moved the merchant fleet wholly into government control and operation.

Ownership and Control - Postwar - Immediately after surrender the Occupation Forces assumed supervisory control over transportation to the extent necessary to serve the requirements of the Occupation. A Shipping Control Authority for Japan (SCAJAP) was established to supervise the operation of the merchant fleet. SCAJAP, now functioning under the Commander, Naval Forces Far East, directs the activities of the Civilian Merchant Marine Committee (CMMC), which is the operating agency of the Japanese Government.

Government and municipal relations with transportation remain much the same. While the present Japanese administration leans to government ownership, the spirit of individual free enterprise is awakening and is being protected by SCAP.

# CIVIL TRANSPORTATION SECTION

## Genesis
In September 1946, the Civil Transportation Section of GHQ, SCAP, was established and quickly made operative in most of its divisions.

## Mission
The mission of the Civil Transportation Section is to:

1. Advise the Supreme Commander on policies relating to the use and rehabilitation of water and land civil transportation facilities of Japan, except those marine operating responsibilities assigned to SCAJAP.

2. Advise the Supreme Commander on all such aspects of the reparations program as affect transportation systems and transportation industries.

3. Oversee and assist water, rail, highway and municipal transportation activities for the purpose of making sure that the Japanese government is working towards sound economic rehabilitation of these systems and their related industries.

## Divisions
Air transport being non-existent in Japan, the four media of transportation to be rehabilitated, coordinated and planned are rail, water, highway and municipal. Chart 1 provides for these divisions of the Civil Transportation Section, with the additional division of Plans, Policies and Requirements for overall planning.

CTS experienced serious initial delays in recruiting qualified personnel. Japanese agencies are being utilized as fully as possible in the studies for and implementation of policies and measures. The dual purpose of this practice is to economize on Occupation costs and to strengthen those agencies in efficient transportation methods and democratic processes. This has permitted a broad, yet detailed coverage at a minimum of expense.

"Line" Divisions - Mission - Each of the four "line" divisions (Rail, Water, Highway, and Municipal) is organized similarly to study, observe and analyze the condition, traffic, operation and legal phases of its respective medium of transportation to the extent necessary to advise and recommend to the Chief of Civil Transportation, policies and measures to assure in Japan, the:

**Japanese and United States Railroads - Comparison** - The railway system of Japan is the main artery of the present-day Japanese economy. Chart 2 draws a comparison between the railways of Japan and those of the United States. The latter has twelve times as many kilometers of main track as Japan. The entire Japanese main railroad system is approximately equal to the Pennsylvania and Southern Pacific systems combined. The gauge used almost exclusively in Japan is 3 feet 6 inches. Three-fourths of the Japanese kilometerage is government-owned.

The volume of passenger traffic carried by Japanese railroads is partially reflected in passenger car ownership. There is twelve times as much main line trackage in the United States as in Japan, but only three times the number of passenger cars. The private lines derive nearly all of their revenue from passenger traffic, and the government rail system 74%.

The number of employees per kilometer is particularly significant. The Japanese use 26 for each kilometer of main track, while the railroads of the United States operate efficiently with less than four. The explanation lies in the greater mechanization of operation and maintenance of Stateside railroads and the inefficient utilization of labor by the Japanese. Advocates of private operation in Japan may draw satisfying conclusions from the observation that government-owned railroads employ almost twice as many workers per kilometer as do the private railroads. Since V.J. Day, despite a postwar reduction in operations, the number of employees on government railroads has increased by 100,000. Part of the increase is due to the repatriation policy, under which the railroads absorbed 180,000 Japanese repatriates.

Passenger Traffic - Passenger traffic on the Japanese government railroads increased steadily from 1936 to 1944, as illustrated in Chart 3. The end of the war halted this rise temporarily, but today these railroads are hauling more passengers than ever. The government railroads haul about 300 million passengers each month. The private railroads carry nearly 200 million passengers. The equivalent of the entire population of Japan is moved once each four or five days.

These figures are even more impressive when consideration is given to the fact that under a reduced scale of operations imposed by coal shortage, the Japanese railroads are today running less trains than at any time during the last ten years. Before the war, daily passenger train kilometers averaged 320,000; now the average is 185,000 (Chart 4). Obviously, the increased traffic is handled by continually overloading the cars beyond the limits of safety.

The Japanese rides a train when he travels, since that is his only swift and usually frequent means of transportation. The Japanese, unlike the average American, has grossly inadequate highways and few passenger motor vehicles. Moreover, passenger traffic has been greatly increased by the dislocation of population and dispersal of industry to support the war effort and avoid bombing. During Japan's present economic crisis, passenger trains are also being used by a large number of small-scale black market operators. These conditions have increased travel to and from suburban areas.

Passenger traffic supplies 74% of the revenue of the government railways and most of the income of private lines.

Freight Traffic - The freight tonnage hauled by Japanese government railroads (Chart 5) increased steadily from 8 million tons per month average in 1936 to 12.6 million tons per month at the

=== Page 15 === (confidence 1.00)
outbreak of war with the United States in 1941. The tonnage dropped slightly in 1942. Marine shipping losses in that year caused a government decision to reduce inter-island water movements to a minimum, in order to free ships for far-seas service. This placed an increased burden on the railroads, and freight tonnage reached the all-time high of 13.9 million tons per month in 1943. The end of the war and the subsequent breakdown of the Japanese economy caused rail freight traffic to drop off severely, practically back to the 1936 level.

During 1947, gradual improvement in the Japanese economy is reflected in the increase of freight tonnage movement from 7.4 million tons in January to 9.5 million tons in July.

The years 1943 for freight and 1944 for passenger are the prewar peaks of traffic handled by the government railways. The Japanese refer to this period as the "saturation point", based on their experience. In 1943 there were 111,966 freight cars in service with 109,935 operable. These transported 13.9 million tons per month, an average of 126.4 tons per operating car per month. There are now 96,857 freight cars in service, with 88,796 operable (4.8 days turn-around time and an average haul of 188 km.). These are moving 9.5 million tons per month or 107.1 tons per operable car. This indicates that "saturation" of the presently available operable cars would permit 18% more tonnage, or a total of 11.2 million tons per month.

Freight traffic now produces 23% of the revenue of the government railways.

**Summary of Rail Conditions** - The Japanese railway system, although not modern according to United States standards and although suffering from deferred maintenance during the war years and lack of adequate materials today, is a well established transportation facility fully capable of bearing its share of the minimum economy. In common with other industry, the government railways are suffering the ills attendant upon "deficit" operation and surplus employees and the remedying of these problems constitutes one of the major tasks with which the Japanese must cope.

Effects of War - During the four years of war, Japan lost all of the gains of the preceding thirty years, the 6 million-ton fleet being reduced to less than 2 million, as shown in Chart 7. The British had gained 3 million tons, in spite of heavy losses, and the United States had gained fourfold, as revealed in Chart 6. During each of the war years, the rate of loss exceeded the rate of construction. Japan began to lose the war of shipping as early as 1942

Present Merchant Fleet - Today Japan has about 1,350,000 tons of operable steel vessels, as shown in Chart 8. Of this fleet, 65% is of wartime construction and probably is uninsurable by reputable classification agencies, such as Lloyd's, for example. The remaining 35% is over twenty years old, only a few of such ships being suitable for extended overseas service. Adding to the operable fleet of steel vessels, salvagable vessels, vessels under construction, wooden vessels and inoperable vessels, the total merchant fleet of Japan aggregates 2,350,000 gross tons. This fleet includes 190,000 gross tons of steel tankers, less than one half being operable. While it is considered that the war-time constructed vessels are not suitable for extended overseas service, these ships can perform limited service in the Near Seas area between Japan and Korea and North China. The quality of Japan's merchant fleet is such that the average ship spends 25% of the time in shipyards undergoing repairs.

There are some 400,000 gross tons of wooden ships under 100 tons engaged in shorthaul or intra-harbor service. Wooden ships over 100 tons size aggregating 200,000 gross tons, haul coal, mostly within the inland sea area. To complete the "fleet", the fishing fleet, so important to Japan's food supply, totals about 366,000 gross tons of all types of vessels, which is believed to be near to prewar strength. This tonnage is not included in the Merchant Marine totals, and, moreover, supervision of this fleet rests with the Natural Resources Section of SCAP.

In common with all industry in Japan, the threat of exacting reparations has been hanging heavily over the shipping industry. Upon the announcement of the Pauley Plan, owners of ships affected became reluctant to repair, salvage or rehabilitate vessels. Though the Civil Merchant Marine Committee (CMMC) is responsible for running repairs to shipping in all categories, owners generally are responsible for certain aspects of long-range repairs and improvements to such shipping. However, the government had to step in and assume the responsibility and cost for all such work. Needless to say, there also was an immediate lack of interest in pursuing the construction of ships over 5,000 tons.

**Government Control** - All steel ships of 100 gross tons or larger are forcibly bare-boat chartered from the owners by the government agency, the CMMC. This agency operates the ships under the direction of the U.S. Naval Shipping Control Authority for Japan (SCAJAP). With all shipping under government control, there appears to be a lack of individual enterprise and economic stimulus to operate shipping as efficiently as it could be operated under private management. There is little incentive to get ships out of the shipyards and a lack of interest to solicit sufficient cargo for ships.

**Rehabilitation** - Since the beginning of Occupation, there has been a slow but steady rehabilitation program, which has brought back approximately 150,000 tons of shipping into the operable category. In addition, construction of about 200,000 tons of shipping, excluding fishing vessels, has been completed.

**Traffic - Coast-wise** - Immediately after surrender, traffic in the coast-wise trade was exceedingly small. This situation was caused in part, by the poor condition of the ships, the great navigation hazards, and wrecks, and the stringent U.S. Naval control over all Japanese ship movements. However, from a low of 190,000 tons per month in the Autumn of 1945, the volume of coast-wise traffic in steel ships has now increased to around 700,000 tons a month. While comparable data are not available, best estimates are that the present coast-wise trade carried in steel and wooden ships amounts to about 50% of the average volume between 1930 and 1934.

**Traffic - Foreign** - Soon after the Occupation began, Japanese shipping under Naval control was permitted to engage in SCAP's Foreign Trade Program. Japanese ships in foreign trade have been carrying from 100,000 to 150,000 tons a month. This traffic, however, has been confined largely to the Near Seas area, China, Korea and Formosa.

Fuel Supply - Still hindering marine shipping is the irregular supply of coal. Ships of over 1000 gross tons, aggregating about 70% of the tonnage, are coal burning.

Rate Structure - A major and complex problem of water transportation in Japan is the establishment of a fair and adequate rate structure. Ships built during the war at high cost must earn high freight rates to bring adequate returns on the investment they represent. Through inflation, marine labor and all costs of operation have sky-rocketed. Marine freight rates do not cover the cost of operation. The disparity between operating costs and revenue is the CMMC deficit, which in the fiscal year of 1947 reached one billion seven hundred million yen. In the fiscal year of 1948 the deficit will be upwards of two and a half billion. This deficit may be considered as the Japanese government subsidy to marine shipping.

A complicating factor in the rate structure is the disparity between water and rail rates. Since the middle of the war period, it has been cheaper to ship by rail than by water. This partly explains why the railroads are over-burdened with a backlog of freight while marine shipping is suffering from short loadings.

Inflation and Costs - Aside from upsetting the rate structure, inflation has seriously interfered with new shipbuilding. With government control of prices during the war, shipbuilding cost was about 500 yen per gross ton. In the Fall of 1946 the costs reached 20,000 yen, and ten months later reached 40,000 yen. Stabilization of prices is not yet assured.

Summary of Water Transport - Japan has enough shipping to maintain the current volume of coastal traffic at the present low level of her economy. Improvement in incentive can materially increase the shipping capacity. The present Merchant Fleet could not meet minimum demands of an economy of the pattern prevailing in 1930-1934. If an economy similar to that era is approved, the program of rehabilitation should include an adequate fishing fleet, a more efficient far-seas fleet, and a renewed coastal fleet.

# HIGHWAY TRANSPORTATION

**Position in Japan** - Highway transportation is the genesis and exodus of all other modes of transportation, particularly in Japan, where a large proportion of manufacturing plants and warehouse facilities are "off track". While vehicle haulage handles more tonnage than rail and water combined, its base of operation, the highway system, may be compared with conditions prevailing in the United States when the great majority of improved highways were terminal feeders and the motor truck had completed approximately one half of its replacement of horse-drawn transportation.

**Undeveloped Highway System** - Japan has a network of roads which, for the most part, are narrow, steep and winding, poorly surfaced, and poorly maintained. The entire road network includes 1,070,887 km, as shown in Chart 11, of which only 12,266 km. (1.1%) are paved. A very small portion are wide enough to permit two trucks to pass, the highways having been designed for animal and human-drawn carts. Its "Class I, National Road" system consists of 8,893 km., of which only 1,680 km. (18.9%) are paved and 1,172 km. are so narrow that two trucks cannot pass.

Few Motor Vehicles in Japan - The use of motor vehicles in Japan has never been extensive, and comparisons with other countries are meaningless. Motor vehicle haulage is confined to relatively short distances, intra-city movements and feeder service to rail and marine terminals. Among the factors responsible for poor highway development and limited use of motor vehicle transportation are:

1. Extensive and cheap coastwise transportation, utilizing Japan's excellent harbors.
2. Extensive railway system, serving all industrial and commercial areas and most of the towns and villages.
3. Availability of cheap human and animal power for short hauls.
4. Ignorance of the economic relationship of rail, water and highway transport.
5. Lack of private automobiles cheap enough for large numbers of citizens to own.

Production of Motor Vehicles

Japanese production of motor vehicles did not become significant until after 1935. The country was largely dependent on imports until 1938, when for the first time, domestic output exceeded imports. Foreign automobiles then made up 40% of the number absorbed by the economy, but imports stopped completely in 1938. Domestic production continued to rise until it reached its peak in 1941, during which year a total of 47,900 vehicles of all types were produced. Highway Passenger Transport - The same adverse factors which have hindered motor freight transportation also hinder bus transportation. This mode of transport was much worse hit during the war. The number of these vehicles dropped from 32,000 in 1935 to 12,500 in 1945, a decline of 61% (Chart 14). While the government railroads transport an average of 297.4 million passengers each month, buses carry only an average of 52.5 million passengers. 37.7 million of these passengers are carried on inter-city buses. Bus transportation will triple in capacity and service as soon as equipment, parts and fuel are available. This would be returning to 1935 conditions. As intra-city buses replace worn out traction equipment and as highways further improve, and the economy will permit the importation of critical supplies, this industry will develop many-fold.

Fuel Situation - Rationing has been necessary because of the severe shortage of gasoline. Many vehicles have been and are deadlined, or their operation drastically restricted, for the same cause. Charts 13 and 14 show the percentage of trucks and buses that are operative. A large proportion of the vehicles have been converted to charcoal or wood-burning operation. These fuels are not available in sufficient quantities because of the high priority given to demands for domestic heating to prevent sickness and unrest, and operating costs are higher than with gasoline. The fuel situation appears to be getting tighter instead of better, and it is expected that rationing controls will become more stringent and the use of substitute fuels extended for some time.

Summary of Highway Transport - For the present level of Japanese economy, the highways and highway vehicles are adequate, but all parts of the system are in sad need of maintenance. Shortage of repair materials and parts is dangerously hampering this medium in carrying its proper share of the present transportation load. Poorly coordinated as it is with other media, the low performance of highway transport is adversely affecting the ability of rail, water and municipal transportation to perform their respective parts.

The program of development, as the economy permits or demands, must be in the direction of increased supplies of materials, equipment and parts by means of Japanese production or by import; repair and construction of highways; and fulfillment of the urgent demand for increased motor operations.

MUNICIPAL TRANSPORTATION

**Position in Japan** - Heavy concentrations of industry and population in a few metropolitan areas and the almost complete absence of private automobiles place heavy emphasis on the importance of municipal transport service to the maintenance of a stable economy. This service is provided by a combination of government and private railways, tram lines, subways, motor buses, taxis and rickshaws.

Prior to 1880, except for the limited services offered by the railways, the population was dependent upon animal and human drawn conveyances. The first city tramway operation was initiated in Tokyo in November 1880, to be followed by the other five principal cities of Osaka, 1903; Nagoya, 1896; Kyoto, 1893; Yokohama, 1900; and Kobe, 1905. With the exception of Osaka, all of these operations were initiated and reached a high stage of development under private management. At the present time all of these, as well as the majority of the systems of secondary importance, are owned and operated by the municipalities.

In the early 1920s the motor bus began exerting a considerable influence on urban transport. Low cost of installation and flexibility of operation attracted private capital, and rapid expansion was seen until the outbreak of the war, when these private operations were consolidated in each area and purchased by the municipalities.

The first portion of the Tokyo subway (6.4 km.) was opened to traffic in 1927 and the remainder (7.9 km.) some years later. The Osaka subway (3 km.) was opened in 1933. This has been extended to 8.8 km. at present.

Taxicabs, which were in abundance at low fares, largely supplied the needs of rapid itinerant movement, especially in the business centers. Rickshaws performed this function in residential areas.

**Postwar Condition** - At the close of the war, municipal transport systems were practically immobilized. Operations on a limited scale were quickly resumed. Progress in rehabilitation has been steady but slow due to shortages of basic raw materials.

# TRANSPORTATION PLANNING FOR JAPAN

## Transportation Planning Requires Sound Base
Transportation rehabilitation plans must be built on a sound basis to assure efficient operation of all media during the Occupation period and to insure the accomplishment of the objectives of the Occupation. Current decisions relating to such plans and purposes must be consonant with a projected plan for a fully coordinated transportation system in Japan. The economic, political, geographical, production and cultural characteristics of Japan influence the pattern to which transportation must conform.

Transportation policy depends upon:

1. Topography and natural factors.
2. Historical and established methods of transportation.
3. Production facilities to support the transportation system.
4. Natural resources, indigenous and imported.
5. Labor supply and cost.

Some of the more important factors peculiar to Japan that profoundly affect present decisions and the projected pattern are:

1. Japan is rugged and extremely mountainous. The valleys are narrow. The rivers are short, rapid and generally non-navigable, dry in certain seasons and flooded in others. Rail or highway construction is expensive, and the geology permits frequent slides.

2. Only 17% of the land is arable, and some of this is on steep slopes. Level land is essential for crops and is much in demand. Agriculture is centered in a few areas, and transportation must be tailored to seasonal demands.

3. Seventy-eight million Japanese live in an area about the size of California. The population is concentrated in a relatively few industrial areas, and contrary to U. S. practice, farmers live in villages rather than on the farms. This concentration has influenced the transport pattern and will affect current and future plans.

=== Page 30 === (confidence 1.00)
4. Japan is far from self-sufficient in natural resources. Fish and lumber are among the few exceptions, but these barely meet the needs of the internal economy. Many of the products upon which industry feeds are lacking and must be imported. The Merchant Marine program is directly influenced by the export-import policy.

5. Additional foodstuffs, principally rice, must be grown in "exploration" areas such as Hokkaido, or be imported. Either creates a problem in transportation.

6. If Japan is to sustain a stable economy, it must import raw materials and export finished products. Since Japan will not have significant heavy industries, light manufacturing will predominate and may decentralize industry. This must be reckoned in planning a transportation system which meets the needs of a minimum economy under Occupation and which at the same time can be integrated in the future plans for rail, water and highway transportation.

7. Cheap labor has always characterized Japan. The new Japanese social and economic order—with shorter hours, collective bargaining, vacations, social security, etc.—will shorten the supply of cheap labor. This change, if successful, will profoundly affect the economy of Japan, and consequently influence the transportation pattern.

8. If the standards of living are raised, as is implied in factor 7 above, additional purchasing power will permit more motor transportation and create increased highway demands. As such demands are satisfied, the relation of rail, highway and water transportation will change. If, however, the economy continues at its former level, automotive development and highway improvement will be very slow.

9. The people of Japan have long been accustomed to government operation and central control. They have always looked for sanctions and guidance from higher up. Feudal relationships—the Oyuban-Kobun system—have limited action by the people. Individual initiative and private enterprise are not the natural heritage. Education on the benefits and obligations of private enterprise must go hand in hand with legislation and directives intent on achieving specific current objectives. Transportation is no exception.

Balanced Transportation Plan for Japan - Under present economic and political conditions in Japan, a rigid pattern for the overall, coordinated transportation system should not be crystalized. This is the proper time, however, to determine the basic factors and their proper inter-relationship, to mould an integrated plan for application as the economic pattern for Japan emerges. There is no room for idle speculation or continued unpreparedness. The basic facts are being determined.

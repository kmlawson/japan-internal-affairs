THE JAPANESE PROBLEM IN BRITISH COLUMBIA

From American Vice Consul
Nelson P. Meeks

Vancouver, B. C. Date of Completion: October 7, 1940
Date of Mailing: October 7, 1940

Approved:

Paul R. Josselyn
American Consul General

Prior to the publication of the pact which joins Japanese with the Axis powers and provides for the military aid of that country in the event of certain contingencies, the Japanese problem in British Columbia was largely an economic one.

The latest available figures indicate that approximately 90 percent of the Japanese in Canada reside in British Columbia. There are, therefore, nearly 24,000 Japanese in this Province and, of this number, more than 10,000 were born here. While, according to the last census, the Japanese population of British Columbia was less than 5 percent of the number residing here, they exercise an influence in the economic affairs

of the Province due to their penetration of certain key industries and low standard of living far out of proportion to their relation to the total population.

This has been particularly true insofar as fishing is concerned, and the Dominion Government has in recent years deemed it advisable to restrict the number of fishing licences to Orientals. As no Chinese here engage in fishing operations, the effect of the order has been to drastically reduce the number of Japanese that have heretofore earned their livelihood at sea. In 1921, 41 percent, or 3,121 fishing licenses, were issued to Japanese and, by 1937, this number had been reduced to 14.5 percent, or 1,964.

Orientals have for some time been a dominant factor in the production and sale of vegetables, and the Vancouver City Council endeavored, without success, last year to have the Provincial Legislature amend the city's charter so as to enable it to refuse new licenses to Orientals engaged in the sale of food. Although the necessary authority was not procured from the Legislature another attempt will shortly be made.

While Japanese capital in British Columbia is reported to control fairly extensive timber holdings and several mining properties, an investigation carried out by Provincial authorities in 1939 indicates that such holdings, as a whole, have not operated profitably for foreign interests.

Since the publication of the German-Italian-Japanese pact, feeling in British Columbia has become intensified, and numerous editorials have appeared in the press advocating a government embargo on copper ore exports to Japan. At present, copper exports amount to 35,000 tons a year. Half of it goes direct to Japan, and the remainder, refined in the United States, presumably also goes to that destination.

Partly as a result of such newspaper comment, various labor organizations and trade associations are pressing the Dominion Government to restrict the activities of local Japanese and to curtail trade with that country. As an example, the B. C. Mink Breeders' Association complained against the competition of cheap Japanese pelts and urged that their importation into Canada be prohibited. Similarly, west coast salmon packers are incensed because many of the best vessels belonging to white fishermen have been commandeered by the Government, while Japanese boats have not been molested. Furthermore, Japanese shipyards, equipped to build fishing vessels up to fifty feet in length, are for the most part operated as family concerns and, as such, are not subject to Provincial regulations governing wages and hours of labor as are independent firms operated with white labor.

The Vancouver City Council has recently had under consideration a motion to abolish Japanese language schools in this city. While this motion was defeated, another one recommending the internment or deportation of Japanese found guilty of subversive actions was approved by the Council.

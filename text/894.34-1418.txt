JAPAN:
SHIPBUILDING
Lack of labour, poor conditions of supply of
materials essential for building, and slow produc-
tion of all goods needed for shipbuilding, have
made it impossible to construct private or Naval
vessels in the Japanese private shipbuilding yards
in less than twice the normal time required before
1937. Large freighters, for example, 10,000 -
12,000 tons, took about ten months to build in
1937, and to-day they take twenty months.
Ordinary repairs cannot be made in less than
three to four times longer than in normal years.
Despite the abnormal activity in shipbuilding
circles, there has been no increase in the number of
ships put into service in 1940.

Subject: Reconditioning of Japanese Naval Vessels.
I called on General Wang Peng-sheng today for the
purpose of discussing the report he gave me on March

vessels.
Regarding the report, General Wang said that it
was obtained by a Chinese employed on a Japanese naval
vessel (in what capacity not stated); that it was receiv-
ed at Tientsin on February 13, l944, and taken by safe
hand across the border of northern Anhwei into free
China, and that it was sent from there by radio to
Chungking, where it was received late in February 1944.
General Wang stated further that the source from whom
the report was received submits relatively few reports
but that those received have invariably been good.
The information contained in the report concerning the
reconditioning of Japanese naval vessels is believed
by General Wang to be very reliable although some mi-
nor mistakes in figures may have occurred due to faulty
radio transmission or reception.
General Wang stated that the surface vessel pre-
viously given the name of Saion is now believed to be
the converted merchant ship Nishizono (), the
characters of which he said may also be read as Saion.
Regarding the comment by the Naval Attache that the Tight
cruiser Yura had been sunk by the United States Navy,
General Wang said that the Japanese possess another ship
named Yura; that it is a converted merchant ship and
may be the one referred to in his original report.
Regarding the withdrawal of so many submarines from
active service for reconditioning at one time, General
Wang stated that in his opinion the Japanese would not
hesitate to do so if they thought that thereby they
could make significant improvements, and that they un-
doubtedly realized that if they wished to make any im-
provements they would have to do so now, as it would
be impossible later on.
Genoral Wang said he would endeavor to obtain fur-
ther information regarding the submarines listed in his
report. Speaking of recent Japanese construction of
submarines, he said that in 1942 they planned to con-
struct 87 submarines and that by the end of 1943 they
had completed 28. General Wang said he had no informa-
tion regarding the present status of construotion of
the romaining units and would appreciate receiving in-
formation in regard to recent Japanese submarine con-
struetion, with especial reference to the figures men-
tioned above.

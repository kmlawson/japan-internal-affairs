Dear Mr. Hornbeck:

Mr. Price has sent me, with the suggestion that I show it to you, a sample of the effect of Japanese propaganda upon one of our more naive fellow citizens Dr. Rosetta S. Hall of Liberty, New York. The latter may not be of special interest either to you or to the files of the Division of Far Eastern Affairs since their general nature is familiar to you. But, I send them on as examples which can easily be thrown away.

Mr. Price also sent me the enclosed circular of the National Council for Prevention of War, dated August 9, 1940 which rather optimistically was sent to him. Since we use similar methods, we can hardly criticize Mr. Libby, who is no doubt perfectly sincere.

Lately I have seen some interesting examples of German propaganda, sent to a Roumanian with a German-Jewish name, in which reference is made to an alleged new book by Henry Ford, published recently in Germany under the title "Erfolg im Leben". Mr. Ford's office has denied the existence of such a book by him.

It does seem as if some more stringent measures might be in order for the control of such propaganda by foreign governments. In any case, it does not seem as if it ought to be entitled to second class privileges in our Post Office.

Yours sincerely,

Roger S. Greene

# Consul General Clarifies Policy Of Japan in China

NEW YORK — The Open Door policy of Japan in China, stressed time and again by Tokyo, assuring all third Powers of free and unhampered commerce, was repeated anew here recently by Kaname Wakasugi, Consul General of Japan in New York.

At a luncheon of the Living Age Forum at the City Club of New York, Mr. Wakasugi declared that stability in East Asia, an end to strife and a restoration of order is Japan's primal aim. With order reestablished — a New Order all nations, as well as Japan and China, will be the gainers, he said, because of the freer flow of international trade, unhampered by the strife that has impeded it for the last half century.

## New Order and New Deal

Very ably Mr. Wakasugi contrasted the New Order in East Asia to the New Freedom of President Wilson and the New Deal of President Roosevelt. He said:—

“For a century and a half the world witnessed with admiration the vision and courage of the American people in establishing a New World in this Western Hemisphere. Much in the same manner, Japan is now following the American example in organizing a New Order in the Asiatic continent.

“It is not difficult to understand and appreciate the sentiments and reasons of the American people in their determination to safeguard the Monroe Doctrine and the great importance they attach to the security and development of the Western Hemisphere.

## Slogan Like America’s

“The slogan of ‘A New Order in the Far East’ may sound strange to some in view of the fact that we are now engaged in armed conflict, and it may be misinterpreted as a new expression of destruction of an older order. But if you recall your own slogans, such as ‘New Freedom,’ enunciated by President Wilson at the end of the last World War, and ‘New Deal,’ with which you are familiar today, A New Order in the Far East is not altogether a strange idea.

“It aims to assert to the peoples of East Asia economic and political security by friendly co-operation between Japan, Manchukuo and China, thus safeguarding their independence and stability from political encroachments by any foreign Powers, and also securing their natural resources for their won needs.

## By-Product of Experience

“After all, the New Order policy is a by-product of the past experiences of the Oriental peoples who have suffered from foreign pressure, in view of strategy or natural resources.

“By a New Order, therefore, we by no means intend to grab any portion of China’s territory, nor to squeeze China’s wealth, as it was declared by Prince Konoyo the year before last when he was Premier that Japan would demand neither territory nor in demnity from China.”

Referring to Wang Ching-we recently inaugurated in Nankin as the new chief of a rebor China, Mr. Wakasugi called hi “the most outstanding and brilliant statesman in China today. He undoubtedly will collaborate with Japan,” the Consul General said, “in establishing permanent peace and amity in East Asia.”

## Calls for Co-operation

“By a New Order again,” Mr. Wakasugi continued, “we do not mean to exclude other people nor close the door to foreign interests. On the contrary, a New Order aims at the harmony and co-operation of different races and interests, without which we can hardly attain that goal. Consequently, foreign rights and interests will be duly protected and foreign investments will be welcomed, as there are vast fields and opportunities for foreign activities, economic as well as cultural.

“Japan opposes, however, any anti-Japanese policy and pro-Communist propaganda of any nation, as no nation can tolerate a hostile neighbor against her. You will understand, therefore, that the ultimate end of the New Order policy is to promote peace, progress and prosperity in the Far East.

## Peace in the Pacific

“It is needless to say how important it is for America, as well as for Japan, to promote peace and prosperity in the Pacific,” Mr. Wakasugi added. “But, in order to contribute to the general welfare of the Pacific we must put our own house in order in the Far East. No matter what the world may say today about our policy in the Far East, we are convinced that our sincere endeavors and tremendous sacrifices will be recompensed in the dawning of a new era in the Orient, the benefits of which will be shared by mankind in general and by the Far Eastern peoples in particular.”

Pointing out that 25 percent of the total of American Cotton exports is bought by Japan, which supports practically 2,000,000 on Southern plantations, and that a half a million American mill folk are supported by weaving the silk that comes from Japan, Mr. Wakasugi said:—

“If the U. S. A. is Japan’s best customer, Japan is America’s third best customer and on the whole a bigger customer of American goods than all the South American republics put together.”

## Letter, Who Was Undersecretary of State When Stimson Was His Superior, Stands Firmly Against Ex-Chief's Embargo Ideas

**BY HARTLEY B. WHYTE**
Former Reuters Correspondent in the Far East

NEW YORK—Automatic termination of the 1911 American-Japanese Treaty of Commerce and Navigation on January 26 has created a serious problem between the two great Powers at either side of the Pacific which cannot be treated tightly and which calls for calm deliberation, despite the uproars raised by moralists and jingoes for immediate war-like embargoes.

At the very moment when both the United States and Japan have a chance to act reassembly to solve the problems which have greatly strained the nearly century-old traditional friendship between the two nations, a tremendously unreasonable and prejudiced temper has been fanned in this country among the sentimentalists for an outright embargo, despite the mounting and increasingly vocal plea of business men against any rash action on the part of Congress which might imperil an already crippled domestic economic set-up.

## Stimson Renews Old Hostility

It was on January 10 — more than two weeks ago—that Henry L. Stimson, former Secretary of State, proposed in a letter to the New York Times that Congress pass one of the four bills now before it to prohibit the export of arms, munitions or raw materials for arms to Japan.

Mr. Stimson identified, at the time, "a comparatively small number of individual Americans who are aiding Japanese aggression in China," and said they were "chiefly the merchants who are selling to Japan the ore, steel and scrap iron indispensable for her bombs, and the large oil producers who are selling to her even more indispensable aviation gasoline." He therefore urged the State Department to extend the moral embargo "now effective on export of planes and on machinery to manufacture special aviation gasoline."

## Fanned Flames Before

Mr. Stimson, Secretary of State in the Cabinet of President Hoover, said he had been a member of the National Committee on the National Defense of Japan's policy to import a war with the United States." The lengthy statement by Mr. Stimson was challenged immediately by the Foreign Office spokesman in Tokyo who, referring to a paragraph in Mr. Stimson's statement that the United States should reject any Japanese move for the conclusion of a new trade treaty until Tokyo gives more concrete assurance regarding its program in East Asia, said: "Japan's aim has been clarified time after time while we are doing our best to safeguard the rights and interests of third Powers in China."

## Challenged on Sentiment

Challenging Mr. Stimson's assertion that American sentiment toward Japan was worsening, the spokesman pointed out that neither the New York Times nor the Herald Tribune were against a modus vivendi, or stop-gap agreement, with Japan, but that those two influential newspapers, instead, had voiced the opinion that a temporary commercial agreement be concluded with Japan even before the 1911 pact elapsed.

## Treaty Out of Date

"The American Government said it was denounced because a new, more specific and up-to-date treaty was necessary. When the old treaty was made, certainly nobody had thought of Japan in China and of Japanese interference with our interests in that country.

"I do not criticize the denunciation of the old treaty at all. It was out of date and Japan had been interfering with our China trade. On the other hand, I do think it was wrong to denounce the treaty without warning—because sometimes the courtesy employed or neglected is really more important in international relations than the action itself.

## Seriousness Recognized

"The denunciation of the treaty, followed by Ambassador Grew's admirable and restrained presentation of the American case on his return to Tokyo, impressed the Japanese with the seriousness of the situation, because during the last few months, the Japanese Government has been able better to control the Army in China—or perhaps the Army has itself become more reasonable. "And remember that the last thing in the world that Japan wants is war with its traditional friend, the United States. Remember, too, that our friendship for Japan and Japan's friendship for us have stood many strains, are just as solid a tradition as is our friendship with China.

"When our treaty expires on January 26 we shall have no formal trade relations with Japan. The large present volume of trade will automatically shrink, to the injury of both nations. At first, we shall be hurt more than the Japanese. For example, as you all know, our foreign cotton market has pretty well ceased to exist, Japan being our only remaining large foreign buyer. It has recently bought from us an average of something over a million bales annually.

"These cotton purchases, after the trade treaty expires, will be transferred to Brazil and Argentina and India. And we must remember that such a change will probably be permanent. Japan will not sell as much to us because her exports will have to pay higher duties when they try to enter the United States, since Japan will no longer have most-favored nation treatment. "Think what would happen if, after January 26, the Congress should vote an embargo on trade with Japan. That would be truly one of those acts which the President has called 'steps short of war.' 'An embargo is useless unless it is effective, and if it is effective it means war, . . . and historically, that is true.

"And if Congress should take such a step, what would be our excuse? In wanting to negotiate a new treaty, this old friend, Japan, is offering to give hostages for the future.' The Government, I have no doubt, hopes by this to assert greater control over the Army.

"If, in the face of this, we should clap on a trade embargo, Japan could only believe that we want war. "If we are willing to go to war we can actively oppose Japan's operations in China. Also, if we are determined not to go to war under any circumstances we can withdraw from the Orient. It was immediately after Secretary of State Cordell Hull, last July 26, dramatically and suddenly abrogated the 1911 treaty, that Congressional leaders, including such men as Senator Hiram Johnson of California and the late Senator William E. "Our total exports to Japan dropped to $178,000,000 in the first ten months of 1939, compared with $192,000,000 in the corresponding period of 1938.

YOU HAVE HELPED TO MAKE CONSCRIPTION THE GREAT ISSUE

Dear Fellow Worker:

Since I wrote you a fortnight ago you have witnessed the results in Washington of your mail. At the time of my last letter, it was the intention of the sponsors of the Burke-Wadsworth Bill to jam it through Congress long before this. The country had not waked up to its peril. It has now. I trust that the 2,100 who receive our workers letters helped materially to arouse the country and to let Congress know how strong and sincere the opposition is.

Two weeks ago today the representative of one of the most reliable news services told us that there would "not be ten votes in the Senate against the Burke-Wadsworth Bill." The mail received has changed all this. One Senator reported that his mail was running 20-1 against conscription. Another said that he had had 400 letters that morning, all against conscription. There are intentional cases. Occasionally on the 50-50; one or two day and the 50-50; one or two day and the 50-50; one or two day and the 50-50.

The anti-conscription hearings have been on the whole powerful indictments of the proposal. Two Catholic leaders, the Rt. Rev. Mgr. Ready and the Rt. Rev. Msgr. Barry O'Toole, several outstanding Protestant leaders including Dr. Harry Emerson Fosdick, Bishop William Appleton Lawrence of the Western Diocese of Massachusetts (Episcopal) and Dr. Charles F. Boss, Jr., presenting the official pronouncement of the Methodist World Peace Commission; E. Raymond Wilson and Paul French, Quakers who have been highly successful in securing an unprecedented broadening of the policy towards conscientious objectors; and several very effective spokesmen for the peace organizations from various parts of the country--all have helped to make the House Military Affairs Committee acquainted, not only with the widespread opposition to this use of regimentation, but also with the arguments against it.

On Thursday, August 1, the anti-war bloc of organizations joined with the Youth Committee Against War in sponsoring an anti-conscription mobilization in Washington. On five days' notice nearly 300 delegates from 23 States gathered here, first to see their Senators and Representatives; secondly to plan their campaign at home; and third, to take part in the mass meeting that evening. At the mass meeting Dr. Charles Boss presided. U. Simpson Tate, Monsignor Barry O'Toole, Fay Bennett of the Youth Committee Against War, Congressman Secrest, Norman Thomas, and Senators Holt, Nye and Wheeler were the speakers. An enthusiastic audience of more than 1100 was present and many were turned away.

As of today, it cannot be predicted how the fight will end. On our side we have determined blocs of Senators and Representatives who see in this Bill the end of the American way of life and are determined to oppose this new act of regimentation to the last ditch. William Green of the A.F. of L., John L. Lewis of the C.I.O., the United Automobile Workers and five great Railroad Brotherhoods have declared that the system of voluntary enlistment must be tried more fully before we resort to conscription.

3 September 1940.

Memorandum for the Chief of Naval Operations

SUBJECT: Japanese Propaganda Expenditures and Organization in the United States.

1. This office has learned from highly reliable sources that the Japanese government's direct expenditures for propaganda purposes within the United States now amount to about four and one-half million yen annually. Over half of this is expended under the direction of Japanese Foreign Office and its personnel in the United States. Of the remainder about one and one-half million yen is spent by agencies of the Ministry of Trade and Industry and one-half million yen by agencies of the Ministry of Railroads, including the Tourist Bureau.

2. The chief function of the "Press Attache" of the Japanese Embassy in Washington is the effective expenditure for propaganda of $2,500,000 by the Japanese Foreign Service in the United States. In addition the "Press Attache" is charged with coordinating the propaganda expenditures of the agencies of other Ministries.

3. Information now at hand indicates that Ambassador Morinouchi considers the above administrative organization unsatisfactory. In particular he states that there is a certain amount of overlapping and duplication of effort, resulting in some inefficiency in spending the limited foreign exchange funds now available to Japan. He has consequently recommended to Tokyo the following corrective steps:

(a) All propaganda agencies abroad should be placed directly under the Foreign Office.

(b) Especially able and competent civilians should be sent to the more important diplomatic offices abroad to administer this propaganda work. While on this duty they should be temporarily accorded a pay grade and treatment or rank equivalent to Minister or Counsellor of Embassy.

Tokyo's despatch no. 4457, January 30, 1940, entitled "Report of Conditions in Japan During the Month of December 1939".

With regard to the invasion of Finland by the Soviet Union, the Japanese press expressed much sympathy for Finland; distrust of the Soviet Union's abiding by any definite agreement entered into with it was also expressed (1, 24).

A decree of December 26 points to a practical difficulty which has been encountered in Japan's price control program; the decree provides punishment for dealers who possess commodities and yet refuse to sell them (p. 27).

The rate of absorption of national bonds during December was 75.2 percent as against 62 percent in November (p. 28). The national bonded indebtedness stood at 1,520,200,000 yen on December 31, 1939.

Official price indexes show that prices increased during the year 1939 as follows: wholesale prices 23 percent, retail prices 17 percent, cost of living 14 per cent, food 23 percent, clothing 21 percent. Increase in the cost of living since July 1937 - 30 percent (p. 32).

The bank of Japan's loans received a new high during December 1939, and exceeded one billion yen at the close of the month (p. 33).

The Japanese "Supreme War Directing Council"
Reference the attached article by K. K. Kawakam1,
clipped from the Washington Star of August 9, 1944,
regarding the new Japanese "Supreme War Directing Council",
which the euthor erroneously calls the new "Supreme War
Council".
Mr. Kawakami asserts thet the new Council is built
on top of, or supersedes, the regularly established
D
Supreme War Council, which is composed of high army and
navy officers, and he queries its necessity. As a
metter of fact, Mr. Kawakami is well off the track;
Japanese broadcasts stated that the new Council took the
place of the Liaison Council (established about 1937)
between the Government and the Supreme Command. That
Council was apparently consultative, whereas the new
XR
Council, according to the broadcasts, is directive.
The author of the article further states that al-
894.002
though the personnel of the new Council has not been
announced, the inclusion therein of civilians or officers
known to be "moderates" would indicate that the military
clique "has teken a signal step toward shifting respon-
sibility upon civilian statesmen in the inevitable event
that the Allied forces will close in on Japan's home
CS/A
islands".
It is not believed that such a shifting of respon-
sibility can reasonably be inferred from the Japanese
broadcasts; on the contrary, the evidence indicates that
the new Premier is doing everything he can to strengthen
the war effort and to coordinate all netional activities
toward that end. Mr. Kawakami appears to have missed
entirely the import of the new Council, which was created
for the purpose, as stated explicitly in the broadoasts,
of bridging over the gap between the Government and the
Supreme Command.
Under the Japanese system, the Government has nothing
to do with matters of high military strategy, although it
is of course concerned with such civilian activities of
military

=== Page 56 === (confidence 0.98)
-2-
military importance as the production of munitione. On
the other hand, the Supreme Command exercises the
emperor's military prerogatives and has no direct con-
nection with civilian matters. This created an awkward
situation during a total war, when the nation's military
operations must be closely correlated with its financial,
economic and industrial development. In order to bridge
this gap the original Liaison Council was created, to be
followed by an even closer integration of civilian and
military matters when Premier Tojo assumed the additional
post of Chief of the General Staff, War Minister and
Munitions Minister.
This solution apparently did not work, and Tojo was
forced to divest himself of his dual capacity and relin
quish the position of Chief of the General Staff. (This

governed by the decisions of the Council". Mr. Kawakami
regards this statement as "ominous", end believes that
it presages a shifting of the responsibility of defeat
from the military to the civilians. The statement is a
natural one, however, under the peculiar Japanese system;
there being no constitutional provision for the Council,
it has to be made cleer to the nation that the Cabinet
will follow the Council's direction on matters within
its jurisdiction.
The Council, despite Mr. Kawakami's assertions, was
not created in preparation for defeat, but, in harmony
with all the other reported activities of the Koiso
Cabinet, was established in an effort to correlate and
coordinate all civilian and military activities under a
united planning board for the purpose of exerting the
maximum of energy and efficiency toward the conduct of
the war.

the annihilation of Japanese militarism. The first is the mili-

of the Japanese people against their military dictators. The
third is the struggle of the peoples in Japan's occupied regions
and her colonies. Undoubtedly the militery forces of the Allies
are decisive in the war against the Japanese invader. Neverthe-
less, it would be incorrect in my opinion to neglect or under-

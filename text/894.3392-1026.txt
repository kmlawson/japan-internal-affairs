Visit of Japanese Cruiser

On February 17th the Japanese 5700-ton cruiser KUMA arrived outside the bar at Bangkok for a brief visit. The visit was made to coincide with the ceremonies connected with the cremation of the remains of the soldiers killed in the recent rebellion (see Section III). The delegation from the Japanese cruiser took a prominent part in the ceremonies, and the Japanese national anthem was played in their honor. During the visit the officers and men from the KUMA were entertained by the Siamese, and a large reception was held at the Japanese Legation. So far as is known, none but Siamese and Japanese were invited to any of the festivities held in connection with the visit of the Japanese war vessel. This would appear to be significant in view of the evident desire on the part of both nations for closer relations. During the visit foreign residents of Bangkok were very much surprised to see a Japanese seaplane flying at a low altitude over the city.

Bangkok's despatch No. 171 of June 1, 1938, in regard to the visit of Japanese training ships at Bangkok.

Two Japanese training ships, the Iwate and Yakumo, former cruisers, arrived at Bangkok on May 18 and remained until May 24, with a complement of about 1,000 officers and men, including some 400 Naval Cadets from the Japanese Naval Academy at Tokyo.

On arrival the commanding officer stated that the purpose of the visit was to give Midshipmen practical sea experience and for educational purposes. "Our present visit to Bangkok," he said, "has no more than these purposes". Adverting to friendship between Japan and Siam he said: "This friendship I believe, derives itself not only from the same constitutional state and the similarity of religion, but also from the coincidence of their sublime mission to maintain the peace of the Far East and to contribute to the World Peace". I have the honor to report that two Japanese training ships, the "Iwate" and "Yakumo", former cruisers, arrived at Bangkok on May 18, 1938 and remained until May 24, with a complement of about 1000 officers and men, including some 400 Naval Cadets from the Japanese Naval Academy at Tokyo. The ships were under the command of Rear Admiral Takasu, I.J.N. As the vessels were of too deep draft to enter the river, they anchored off Paknam at the river mouth and the officers and men came back and forth to Bangkok

Bangkok by bus and street car.

On arrival the commanding officer issued the following statement:

"It is a great pleasure and honour for me, Commanding Officer of the Japanese Training Squadron composed of the Iwate and Yakumo to call on Siam, our most friendly country in the Far East, and to be able to pay my heartiest respect to His Majesty the King and His Highness the Prince Regent as well as my friendly nation.

"The chief object of the Training Squadron, as you know, is to give Midshipmen practical sea experience, while at the same time, to broaden their outlook and to promote their education by observing everywhere the customs and civilization and meeting the people of the countries visited. Our present visit to Bangkok has no more than these purposes.

"These two countries, Japan and Siam, came into contact with each other several hundred years ago. Since then the friendly relations between us have been unaffectedly continued, although the International State of Affairs underwent countless changes. This friendship I believe, derives itself not only from the same constitutional state and the similarity of religion, but also from the coincidence of their sublime mission to maintain the peace of the Far East and to contribute to the World Peace.

"It is my earnest wish and desire that our present visit may serve, even in a small way to promote the cordial relations and friendly co-operation between the Japanese and Siamese people who have been tied up upon the admiring Common Mission to Maintain the Peace of the Far East."

The Japanese visitors received every courtesy and were invited to an elephant hunt at the town of Lopburi, where the Cadets were guests of the Siamese Defence Forces and were accommodated in barracks. There were the usual exchanges of calls and dinners, and on the departure of the vessels, the Rear Admiral commanding and his officers, according to

The press accounts are unanimous in pronouncing the visit a great success, but before the arrival of the vessels there were many rumors to the effect that the local Chinese population were contemplating terroristic actions in connection with the visit, one report being that the train which took the Naval party to Lopburi would be blown up. A Siamese Army officer of my acquaintance, who is attached to the Railway Ministry, told me that the visit was a nuisance to him personally, as he had to run a pilot engine ahead of the train carrying the Japanese party, for although the Siamese did not believe that there was danger, the Siamese Government was taking no chances.

In supplement of my telegram No. 2671 of June 13, 1942, regarding the Japanese merchant shipping situation, I have the honor to quote below in translation a short article from the FRANKFURTER ZEITUNG of June 5, 1942, which treats of the financing by the Tokyo Government of merchant shipbuilding in Japan.

## SUMMARY

Official Japanese public statements emphasize the vital importance of shipbuilding to the war and reveal difficulties being encountered. The Japanese Navy has complete control of the shipbuilding industry, including small craft. The Industrial Equipment Corporation is still the main instrument for financing Japanese shipbuilding. There is a great disparity between shipbuilding costs and Government fixed charter rates, which is proving troublesome. A reliable estimate of total construction for 1942 gives the figure of 800 thousand tons. The shortage of shipbuilding materials is causing a qualitative decline in Japanese vessels and a most serious difficulty is lack of internal ship machinery. Japanese shipping men are pessimistic upon the success of the Government program for mass production of ships. Ingenious substitutes for regular merchant vessels are utilized, including salvaging sunken ships, building wooden vessels, motorboats, sailboats, junks and even rafts. The Government is coordinating sea and land transport to utilize land and rail communications wherever possible and thus relieve shipping. The capital of the South Sea Shipping Company has been increased and all shipping companies' stock shares were advancing on the market. Small boats, because of their increased wartime importance, are now completely insured. The labor shortage is being met by importations of Korean laborers, the improvement of stevedoring methods, the payment of special bounties and increasing seamen's and officers' training schools. The office's informant states all foreigners in Japan recognize destruction of Japanese shipping as that country's weak link. Continued allied successes in destroying Japan's shipping would have a damaging effect upon the industrialists and the country's leaders. Motor torpedo boats might be utilized to attack the small vessels now used by Japan upon its long overseas communications.

## INTRODUCTION

It has been possible for the writer to continue a survey of Japanese wartime shipping conditions, originally made under the title of "Japanese Wartime Shipping: December 1941 - June 1942", which was forwarded to the Department on September 5th, 1942, by Consul Arthur F. Tower, formerly in charge of the American Consulate at Kobe, Japan.

The material for the present report was gained from information and complete newspaper data collected by Mr. Ramon Lavalle, who has just arrived in Istanbul from Tokyo, Japan, where he served as Special Attache in the Argentine Embassy. This information is supplemented by interpretative comment as far as possible, based upon the writer's past experience in reporting periodically upon Japanese shipping conditions while he was assigned to the American Consulate at Kobe, Japan.

Special Japanese Statements Upon the Shipping Problem:

Premier Tojo on August 8, 1942, is reported to have made a nation-wide radio speech, which marked the commencement of a national drive that was extended over several months, to urge the public, shipbuilders and workers to hasten the shipbuilding program by seeing that all ships were launched according to schedule. In the conclusion of his speech his remarks are reported to have been as follows:

"I take the opportunity of reminding you that the officers and men in the firing lines are giving their lives. To you, leaders of the shipbuilding industry and workers, I have an appeal to make in asking you to attend to your duties with an entirely new attitude and to permit ships which are essential to the conduct of successful offensive to be launched according to schedule, although I know that to do so means going through innumerable hardships."

Upon the occasion of Navy Day, July 20, 1942, the Communications Minister, Vice Admiral Terashima, made a radio address in which he reportedly said that although much of Japan's shipping had been directed toward war requirements, the present situation created a still greater demand for ships. Likewise he is claimed to have said that there were increased demands on shipping to maintain the present living standard of Japan and to enhance its productive power. Although many foreign ships were said to have been chartered and bought, while other captured vessels have been put into service in transporting materials from the South, the Communications Minister claimed that this method of gaining increased tonnage was limited in extent and that there was the most urgent need for Japan to build standardized vessels "designed for higher efficiency."

Again later in the year, addressing the workers in the Mitsubishi Dockyard at Yokohama, Vice Admiral Terashima, dwelt upon the great American shipping production capacity, which he "thought was producing an average of 3 ships a day." He warned that national strength could not be augmented without ships, which were an indispensable preliminary to expansion of wartime production. He allegedly confessed that the Government was facing many obstacles in its shipbuilding program and he requested the full cooperation of all workers in order that the construction plan could be realized. Shipping, according to the Communications Minister, was being given "first preference in labor supply, materials, funds and in all other matters, for the purpose of expediting the construction program." He admitted that the labor shortage was forcing the shipyards to receive many unskilled workers, but he hoped that these disadvantages would be overcome with a "patriotic spirit." Reportedly he warned in conclusion that greater demand would be made in the future upon the shipyard workers in particular and that "sacrifices must be quickly made and all handicaps met with determination."

The impression gained from the above described speech of the Communications Minister is the way in which he stressed the key role of Japanese shipping in the war and the frank admissions he is said to have made regarding the obstacles and material shortages confronting the shipbuilding industry.

An adviser to the Japanese Navy, Mr. Kakichi Takeuchi, gave a reported interview to the newspapers at the end of August 1942, in which he developed the thesis that without a proper merchant fleet and without a considerable improvement in the types of auxiliary vessels for the navy, "the southern regions are empty spaces for Japan." In his interview the Naval adviser claimed that the productive facilities in the South were being quickly and efficiently restored, but that the principal problem still remaining unsolved was a sufficient quantity of shipping to bring these needed commodities to Japan. He is said to have gone so far as to suggest "the need for moving part of the factories in Japan to southern areas", in order to cope partially with the shipping shortage.

**Naval Control of Shipbuilding:**

Prior to June 1942, in fact before the war with the United States, the Japanese Navy had gained practically complete control of the entire shipbuilding industry of Japan on the basis of Government ordinances. A revised ordinance is stated to have been issued at the end of July 1942, which gave still further control of shipbuilding to the Navy. Reports of the revised ordinance claim that not only are shipbuilding plants and certain factories placed under the control of the Navy, but also all matters in connection with the construction of vessels of over 50 meters in length, which is said to be equivalent approximately to 500 tons. All questions regarding ship repairs, equipment and engines are subject to Navy approval, but the new ordinance allegedly extends naval control in supplying material and equipment to vessels less than 50 meters in length, in order to conserve and regulate every available bit of ship equipment and shipbuilding.

The Navy is charged with adjusting the construction of merchant ships with that of warcraft, and it is said that its decisions in these matters are final. It determines which shipyard should construct vessels of one type and which shipyard is to build vessels of another design, by seeking to imitate American mass production shipbuilding methods.

**Shipbuilding Organization:**

The Industrial Equipment Corporation, whose functions have been previously reported to the Department, is still said to be the main instrument for the financing of Japanese shipbuilding. Construction subsidies and heavy Government regulation are not new phenomena, but

but have been increasing even before the war under the stress in Japan of actual wartime conditions. In connection with shipbuilding finances, it is interesting to note that the Bank of Japan allegedly supplied 190 million yen to shipbuilders in 1942, in addition to capital coming from other Governmental agencies.

## Difficulties of Shipbuilding Organization:

In view of the low-price policy taken by the Government to avoid inflation, there are stated to be serious difficulties confronting shipbuilders and shipping companies, who build and operate their ships under the present Government plans. There is a great disparity between actual construction costs and the value received by shipping companies, when the Government requisitions their newly constructed vessels and pays them charter rates previously set by the Government. These charter rates have no relation to actual construction values, since it is estimated by the Soviet Naval Attache at Tokyo that a ship in Japan costs anywhere between 670-720 yen a ton to build. This is contrasted with the alleged fixed charter rates of 252 yen for D class ships; 253 yen for C class types and a stated 365 yen for those of D class. The Government allegedly is granting subsidies toward the cost of construction as a method of overcoming this obstacle and the Industrial Equipment Corporation, for whom all commercial vessels are built, also is stated to employ the method of buying the ships at their construction cost value and selling them to shipping companies at terms in accordance with the Government fixed charter rates.

The system of coordinating the placement of contracts is stated to be receiving the most careful attention, but still is subject to great difficulties, since with the scarcity of materials and parts reportedly prevalent in the shipbuilding industry, it is absolutely necessary to have shipping orders unified, in order not to have a serious stoppage of production. Shipbuilding orders are claimed to be placed at periodic intervals by the Industrial Equipment Corporation. There was said to be one group of orders released on August 1st, 1942, and a second group of orders was expected to be released between December 1942 and March 1943.

## Shipbuilding:

The total annual rate of Japanese shipbuilding is of vital importance and the Soviet Naval Attache in Tokyo, Captain M. Kulikoff, is reported to have given his opinion that in the year 1942 Japan reached a maximum total construction of 800 thousand tons, including all naval and commercial vessels. This would appear not to be any different from the pre-war construction estimates, when it was reliably reported that commercial shipbuilding alone did not exceed 400 thousand tons a year. This would place Japanese shipbuilding into two equally divided tonnage classes of naval and commercial construction.

Private information received by this office's informant from Hong Kong indicates that the shipyards in that occupied British colony are building some large freighters, which are expected to be launched early this year, and also new small vessels are said to be launched there in rapid succession.

A dockyard in Shanghai, allegedly transferred to Italian ownership in August 1937, has now been acquired by Japanese interests and has been named the Showa Dockyard, with an announced capital of 5 million yen. This dockyard reportedly will construct merchant ships of the B class.

## Shipbuilding Materials:

Although several Japanese publicists have recently written about Japan's pre-war "clever policy in constructing superior boats by using the materials of two old type vessels to build one fine ship", it is now everywhere reported to be emphasized in the newspapers and magazines that in view of Japan's acute need for more ships, quality must be sacrificed and first importance must be attached "to the quantitative aspect and the rapid increase of tonnage, more or less disregarding other factors.

Economy of shipbuilding materials is stated by the Secretary of the Shipping Control Association to be one method of achieving quantitative production. In the NIPPON SANGYO of December 15, 1942, he allegedly stated that the quantity of steel required in shipping construction must be reduced and that Japan must contrive to do with two tons of steel what it previously accomplished with three tons. The article is stated to have frankly admitted the fact that this would result in lower ship quality, but it was considered a lesser evil to that of smaller numbers of ships. Not only are materials short under the shipbuilding program, but the Soviet Naval Attaché reportedly claims that there is a serious lack of machinery and that there are quantities of ships that have been launched and cannot be put into active service because the necessary internal machinery is unavailable.

## Shipbuilding Difficulties:

The attempt to designate shipyards to construct standardized types is stated to be bringing many difficulties to Japanese shipbuilders and to involve too many factors of rigidity in operation that the Japanese shipyards are as yet unadapted to. Mass production of ships is most new to Japan and some experts are claimed to be very doubtful of its success in that country, in view of the fact that the quantity of ships that it is possible for Japan to build cannot be adapted to mass production technique, in view of the lack of materials for a large scale program, which is the essential prerequisite for "factory" built ships.

**Substitutes for Steel Cargo Ships:**

Japanese ingenuity is being taxed to the limit in trying every means to augment its sea transportation capacity and experimentation with all kinds of wooden vessels, schooners, junks, and rafts is being made in an attempt partly to solve this apparently insoluble problem. Examples of Japanese methods to approach the transportation shortage may be seen in the newspaper illustrations which are attached to this report.

**Salvage:**

Salvaging sunken vessels in the southern regions is one method whereby Japan is gaining additional tonnage. The merger of all Japanese salvage concerns, which had been mooted for some time previously, was apparently announced in July by the Communications Ministry with the news that a consolidated salvage firm, the Maritime Development Company, had been organized with a capital of 10 million yen for the purpose of raising sunken ships in the south.

Salvage operations in Singapore also are frequently reported in the Japanese press and many scuttled vessels there are said to have been raised. In August 1942 a report from Singapore claimed that out of a total of 94 refloated vessels in that area, 20 were steamers, 35 were ferry-boats and 39 were heavy junks. Most of these ships were said to be again operating in nearby waters. Again it was reported on December 2nd, 1942, that a 10,250 ton British freighter TALTHYBIUS that reportedly had been sunk at the entrance to the Empire Dock by Japanese aircraft before the surrender of Singapore, had been successfully raised. A 1400 ton vessel, said to have been scuttled on the Sarawak river, was reported to have been refloated by the middle of September 1942 and the ship taken to Singapore for repairs and renamed the KUCHIMA MARU.

The YOMIURI-HOCHI reported on December 17th that 30 vessels of all types had been refloated in the neighborhood of Surabaya by a special salvage corps. Dutch submarine chasers and speed boats, some of which allegedly took part in the Macassar sea battle, were the first to be refloated and repaired. Some passenger ships and freighters also were said once again to be in service and by the end of 1942, according to the article, another six vessels were to be recommissioned. Operations on the giant floating dock at Surabaya were said to be continuing in an effort to refloat it, since the dock was considered

**Use of Motor Boats:**

On June 20th the JAPAN TIMES AND ADVERTISER published a small item stating that the Cabinet Planning Board was considering the utilization of motor boats for the transportation of steel and iron materials from the southern regions. It may be considered, however, that the motor boats which are at present utilized in Japan, mostly for the coastal trade of coal, lumber and kindred items, are built for lighter service than the one mentioned in the newspaper article and that consequently newer and stronger motor boats must be built. This has resulted in the governmental authorities apparently having decided to build a large number of motor boats of six different types, which will be capable of transporting heavy cargo and to be used for other purposes.

**Sail Boats:**

Japan is seeking to overcome the shortage of ships among the southern islands by resorting to the construction of large numbers of wooden sail boats to ply between the various island ports. In connection with this they are said to be resorting to many measures of forced native labor. With a headquarters allegedly at Macassar, Japanese newspapers are reported to claim that all the Indonesian sail boats in the islands are being organized for this inter-island traffic. A newspaper estimate gives 15,000 craft between one and sixty tons as being subjected to this supervision and it is claimed that they will be of inestimable service in keeping coastal communications open and freight traffic between the islands moving in a satisfactory manner. The number of these small craft now entering the port of Macassar, according to Japanese sources, is said to constitute 60 percent of the former average when the port was under Dutch administration.

On December 31st, stated Japanese newspapers, a large ceremony was held in Java on the occasion of the launching of several large type wooden vessels using Javanese teakwood. Japanese carpenters and engineers are claimed to be supervising the construction of these ships, with native carpenters being recruited for special training as technicians, and a seamen's training school is being opened to increase the number of native sailors.

## Junks:

In August 1942 it was announced that the first experimental use of Chinese junks for sea transportation on a large scale was undertaken between Dairen and Hong Kong. At that time 20 junks manned by Japanese seamen reportedly carried cargoes of soy-bean sacks without incident and it was said to be planned to employ them further in sea transport between "Manchoukuo" and Hong Kong.

The Mutankiang Lumber Company of Korea is claimed to be constructing 100 junks of the Chinese type after the reported success of the above experimental runs. These craft reportedly are 100 feet in length and 25 feet in width, weigh between 150 to 200 tons, are to be set with sails, and each is to be capable of carrying about 2,000 cu.ft. of lumber. To increase cargo capacity it is said the lumber will be carried in bamboo nets hung along the sides of the junks.

## Rafts:

Of all the experimental trials made at seeking substitutes for regular ocean-going vessels, one of the most interesting is that of an ocean-going raft with a stated load of 60,000 cu.ft. of lumber. Rafts of this type reportedly were devised by the War Ministry, and one raft completed a voyage of over 800 nautical miles between a port in Hokkido and Tokyo in fourteen days. This raft was towed by a small tugboat and it was made up by binding the lumber into a shape resembling a submarine. Several trial runs were made before the rafts were formally approved on September 30, 1942 by Premier Tojo, and the Army was said to be considering the operation of such ocean-going rafts between Japan and the southern regions.

## Coordination of Sea and Land Transport:

In addition to the use of every possible substitute for regular ocean vessels, the Japanese Government is attempting to use land transport wherever possible to lessen the heavy strain upon its shipping. A policy along these lines is reported to have been adopted in October by the Japanese Government, whereby ship cargoes were to be switched over to land traffic along the coast of Japan wherever this was feasible. Conversely, to relieve the railroads of some of their present burdens, in order to assume the greater one of handling normally sea borne freight, a drastic curtailment in passenger traffic in Japan reportedly was decided upon. Coal shipments are said to be one of the main concerns of the Government, since there are reported to be an insufficient number of ships available to transport this material from China and "Manchoukuo". On November 20 it is stated that the Communications and Railways Ministries published strict regulations for the control of shipping agents and the

the unification of small land forwarding agents. ## Harbor Works:

It is stated that in July a new wharf was completed on the western bank of the Whangpoo, some distance to the northeast of Lunghwa. Its construction is said to have taken 4 months and to have cost 150 thousand yen.

Construction work on the port facilities of Taku, the port for Tientsin, allegedly by the end of July was one third completed under a general plan for a complete harbor there. A large-sized Japanese vessel was said to have been berthed there for the first time and it is expected with the improvement of the harbor that there will be a consequent increase of shipments for Japan.

## Shipping Companies:

According to CHUGAI of an unknown date, an agreement has been made by the Communications Ministry for the various private Japanese shipping companies to increase the capital of the South Sea Shipping Company, in which all are presently interested. This increase reportedly will amount to 39 million yen, and the N.Y.K. allegedly will investigate 8 million yen in ships and equipment, while the remaining 31 million yen will be apportioned for investment by the O.S.K., other shipping companies and the Ishahara Industry Company.

An item given by this office's informant shows that in the first week of October there was a great advance in the value of shipping stocks and shares of the Kawanami Shipbuilding Company advanced 9 yen in one day, to be quoted at 148 yen. The same day the Hakodate Shipbuilding Company registered a gain of 4.30 yen, the Hitachi Works jumped 3.30 yen and Osaka Shoshen Kaisha shares increased 2.20 yen.

## Small Boat Insurance:

Because of the increasing importance of small boats, such as wooden schooners and motor boats, in national sea transport policy, it has been announced that insurance guilds will be established at all important points in Japan and the Government will hold itself responsible for any losses incurred by these shipowners during the course of their operations. These vessels can now be insured for their full value, state reports, whereas previously they were said to be only insured up to 65 percent of their value.

## Labor:

The Welfare Ministry in July 1942 is stated to have submitted a plan to the Cabinet, which was later approved, outlining new measures needed to overcome the "noticeable shortage of labor" in shipbuilding and the shipping industry generally. One of the parts of this plan is said to have been the encouragement of Korean laborers to come to Japan and work in the shipbuilding industry.

Reportedly on November 9th, 1942, the Japanese Cabinet approved a proposal to speed up ship loading and unloading operations in the country's ports upon a program which would extend over a 4-month period from December 1st 1942 to March 31st, 1943. During that time it was stated to be planned to improve all port facilities, to further the unification of longshoreman's unions, to pay special subsidies to them, and to grant special rewards to those whose efficiency standards measured up to the qualifications made by the Cabinet Planning Board.

The shortage of seamen is also another aspect of the general labor shortage. Several official announcements have indicated the earnest desire of the Government to increase seamen's training schools. Enrollment in existing schools is stated to have been increased to full capacity and, as previously reported to the Department, the minimum age for seamen has been lowered to include boys. Concrete plans for seamen training were stressed in the Japanese diet during the middle of December 1942, when a plan was approved for quicker training of officers and seamen. The ASAHI in reporting partially upon the plan stated that a one-year course was envisaged at the sailors' training center at Miyazaki for stokers and sailors, with 250 students training at one time. The Osaka training center would have a 3-month course for seamen, with 600 students at a time. For officers a short term course was to be started in the Tokyo Higher Merchant Marine School and a similar course was planned for the Merchant Marine School at Kobe. The training courses allegedly would extend over 2 years. At the Hanao Seamen's Training School, a one-year seaman's course said to be planned to include 250 students.

## Observations of Mr. Lavalle:

It may be of interest to report in closing, the observations of Mr. Lavalle upon the Japanese shipping situation, since he has talked extensively with all foreigners remaining in Japan acquainted with the subject and with some Japanese. He states that all foreigners in Japan of pro-Ally sympathies believe that one of the most effective ways to defeat Japan is by the destruction of its overseas communications, since the Japanese cannot replace the vessels that are being sunk. He states that it is universally realized by foreigners in Japan that this

=== Page 19 === (confidence 0.97)
-12-

this is Japan's weakest point and that if it is successfully attacked, it would most deeply effect the morale of the nation's leaders, although it is impossible for the Japanese public to know anything regarding the true circumstances. Allied successes in destroying Japanese shipping reportedly would have a most damaging effect upon the upper classes, especially the industrialists, who are said to have many qualms about the entire war.

An interesting suggestion made by Mr. Lavalle is that with the increased use by Japan of small vessels, such as junks and wooden schooners to supplement its regular shipping, it might be possible for the Allies to utilize craft along the lines of naval motor torpedo boats to attack these small ships, which it would not be worthwhile for submarines to destroy. He stresses that these boats, with high speed, mobility and long range, might prove very successful in this function.

The following is from the circular issued by the Nankai University Committee on Social and Economic Research, on February 3, 1930:

"The statistics on Japanese business investments in China, as shown in the table below have been compiled from data furnished by the Japanese banks and corporations and from other authoritative sources, by Mr. Masnoske Odagiri in his 1929 report to the Kyoto Conference of the Institute of Pacific Relations. It is believed that these statistics represent the most dependable figures yet available. The total amount of such investments reaches 1,809,154,000 yen, of which nearly seventy-five per cent is apportioned to the following four groups of enterprises:

(1) railways, transportation, warehousing;
(2) banking and trust;
(3) spinning and weaving;
(4) agriculture, mining, forestry.

In the first group, by far the most important is the South Manchurian Railway. This company operates 600 miles of railways involving a capital of Y.500 million of which sum Y.150 million is invested in subsidiary enterprises. In the second group are banks engaged in general banking, foreign exchange and industrial investment. The third group, spinning and weaving, has a total investment of Y.250 million; Y.228 million for cotton spinning and weaving, and Y.22 million for silk and flax spinning. In the fourth group, namely agriculture, mining and forestry, the largest percentage of the investment goes

## CHIEF INDUSTRIAL CENTRES

Of the total Japanese business investments in China, 67.5 per cent is concentrated in a few leading commercial and industrial centres. Dairen is at the top of the list, where a major part of the Japanese investments are for the South Manchurian Railway Company and its subsidiaries, while the rest is distributed among steamship, oil pressing, gas, and other enterprises. Shanghai takes the second place, where cotton spinning is the most important among the Japanese undertakings, followed by carrying trade and banking business. In Tsingtao the most heavily invested industries are cotton spinning and weaving and silk reeling, next to which are match making, oil pressing, and flour milling. In Tientsin, cotton spinning is responsible for 55 per cent of the total investment, while tobacco and match manufacturing are responsible for another 34 per cent. Of the investments in Hankow, 60 per cent is assigned to foreign trade and navigation concerns, 15 per cent to cotton spinning, and 25 per cent to banks and other minor enterprises.

The receipt is acknowledged of the Department's instruction No. 35 of July 12, 1932 (no file number), requesting a brief up-to-date report on discernible effects of the Chinese boycott of Japan. Promptness and accuracy, it is stated, should be given precedence over exhaustiveness.

Two factors render it impossible to calculate accurately the effect of the Chinese boycott on Japan's foreign trade, industries, carrying trade, and investments in China. One factor is the world-wide economic depression, which would

It will be observed that during the last three months of 1931 and the first two months of 1932 the Japanese exports to China and to territories where the trade is largely in Chinese hands decreased very considerably, although the exports recovered from March, 1932, after the fighting around Shanghai had been stopped. The decrease during the five months from October, 1931, to February, 1932, as compared with the trade during 1930, however, was partly due to the general economic depression. In 1931 the export trade of Japan to countries other than those under discussion decreased by 16.85 per cent, as compared with 1930, while with the countries under discussion it decreased by 31.16 per cent. The decrease in exports to China and territories where Chinese control the trade, therefore, was 14.33 per cent, or about Yen 75,000,000. During the first half of 1932 (using the customs yen figures) the exports to China and territories where the trade is controlled by Chinese merchants decreased, as compared with the corresponding period of 1930, by 37.11 per cent, while with other countries it decreased by 17.80 per cent, indicating a fall due to the boycott of 19.31 per cent, or approximately Yen 52,000,000. Using the statistics as compensated for the fall in the exchange, the decrease amounts to 56.75 per cent for China and territories where the trade is Chinese-

The total loss in foreign trade because of the Chinese boycott from the beginning of 1931 to the middle of 1932, therefore, is around $73,000,000.

The decrease in the export trade because of the boycott has naturally affected the industries of Japan, but it is difficult to state what proportion of the decreased activity is due to the Chinese boycott and what to the general world depression.

The cotton spinning industry has been operating with a curtailment rate of over 30 per cent of capacity for some months. While some curtailment would have been necessary in any case, the decrease in exports of cotton piece goods to China has undoubtedly made a larger curtailment rate advisable. Approximately 50 per cent of the Japanese output of cotton piece goods is exported, and of the exports of this line 57 or 58 per cent goes to China or countries where the trade is controlled by Chinese merchants. Some 20 per cent of the total production of cotton piece goods therefore goes to countries where the boycott is or was in force. The exports of cotton piece goods to these countries in the first half of 1932 decreased, as compared with the corresponding period of 1930, by 29% in volume. (The exports to other parts of the world remained constant, and consequently no adjustment is necessary because of the depression.) 29 per cent of the 28 per cent of output normally exported to China gives 8.12 per cent, being the proportion of the total output of cotton fabrics lost because of the boycott. This small proportion of the total output of the industry is not sufficient to bankrupt the industry but undoubtedly has caused a decrease in profits.

More serious, however, was the loss caused to the Japanese sugar industry because of the boycott. Some 94 per cent of the exports of sugar normally go to China or to countries where the trade is controlled by Chinese. During the first half of 1932, as compared with the corresponding

period of 1930, this trade decreased by 36 per cent in volume. The boycott therefore caused a decrease of 33.8 per cent of the total exports of this line, and as a consequence the sugar refining industry in Japan has had to close some of the refineries.

Other industries have been affected in the same way to a greater or less extent, but the above two examples will serve to illustrate the effect of the boycott on Japanese industries.

3. Steamship Earnings.

Japan's two largest steamship companies, the Nippon Yusen Kaisha and the Osaka Shosen Kaisha, were compelled, because of operating losses, to pass dividends for the last business term, which included the months when the Chinese boycott was at its height. The operating deficits were undoubtedly partly caused by the world-wide depression in trade, but the larger part is believed to have been due to the Chinese boycott. The trans-Pacific, Australian and European steamship runs have been fairly profitable for the Japanese lines during the past year, because of the unusually large quantities of raw cotton and raw wool imported into Japan and the large amount of raw silk exported from Japan. Moreover, ocean freights are, in practically all cases, quoted in gold dollars or pounds, and the fall in the exchange value of the yen since December, 1931, has been of great assistance to the Japanese steamship companies, as it gives them an increase in their yen income of about one-third. The carrying trade with China, however, has been extremely poor. The China-bound near-seas steamers of the two firms mentioned (or of the Kinkai Yusen Kaisha, a subsidiary of the Nippon Yusen Kaisha), during the height of the boycott, were leaving

-7-

leaving Osaka and Kobe for China ports with only 40 or 50 tons of cargo, instead of the 2000 or 3000 tons usually carried. This was not entirely due to the stoppage of trade, but was partly due to the boycotting of Japanese steamers by the Chinese stevedores, making unloading a difficult and costly process. Such cargo as was available from Japan to China ports, or vice versa, was therefore usually carried by non-Japanese vessels, which were not subject to the stevedores' boycott. This condition, however, did not continue for long, and after the Shanghai armistice the Japanese carrying trade with China returned practically to normal.

The steamship company which was worst hit by the boycott, however, was the Nisshin Kisen Kaisha, a Japanese company engaged entirely in coastwise and river transportation in China. At one time, in the height of the boycott, nearly all of this firm's vessels were reported to be tied up, owing to the impossibility of operation. Although the boycott subsided after the Shanghai armistice, the firm's vessels are still reported to be finding difficulty in operating at a profit.

4. Japanese Investments in China.

No information is available regarding the losses suffered because of the boycott by the many small Japanese firms in China, although in the aggregate these losses must have amounted to an enormous sum. It is known, however, that the Japanese cotton mills in the Shanghai district were compelled to suspend operations entirely during the Japanese occupation of Shanghai and that since the armistice they have been able to operate only at around 50 per cent of capacity, owing to the lack of demand among the Chinese for their products. No data is available at present

The Chinese boycott subsided to a considerable extent after the settlement of the Shanghai affair and the withdrawal of the Japanese troops, but recent reports from Shanghai indicate that the boycott is being resumed.

The principal effect of the Japanese policy toward China is not found in the discernible losses to industry and commerce caused by boycotts, but in the loss of potential trade. If, from around 1890, Japan had adopted a friendly, helpful attitude toward her western neighbor, it is safe to assume that today Japan, because of cheap labor costs and propinquity to the market, would have almost a monopoly of the Chinese trade, with the resultant enormous profits to Japanese industrialists and merchants.

B. Relations with Japan

1. Japanese Capital Appears Reluctant to Invest in "Manchukuo":

In an interview with newspapermen upon his return from Japan to Dairen on July 29, President Matsuoka of the South Manchuria Railway declared that the Japanese public understood the realities of the present Manchurian situation. However, Mr. Matsuoka went on to explain, some capitalists were hesitant over investing in Manchuria, chiefly because of Kwantung Army control over present economic ventures and the Army's program for future developments. The South Manchuria Railway President concluded that the unimpeded flow of Japanese capital into Manchuria could be realized only when "misapprehensions" concerning the Army's role in "Manchukuo" were corrected. He is credited with having announced in Kobe on July 26, that "there is absolutely no risk involved in the economic development of North China and Manchoukuo... The developing of North China and the new empire through Japanese investment and enterprise is the work and duty of Kwansai financiers".

A possible indication of Japanese capital's mistrust of investments in Manchuria is that although during his forty days sojourn in Japan Mr. Matsuoka was able to elicit promises from Japanese financiers to absorb the South Manchuria Railway's Yen 80,000,000 debenture flotation, only Yen 50,000,000 of the issue was actually taken up. It is significant that South Manchuria Railway issues are still regarded as the soundest Manchurian investments.

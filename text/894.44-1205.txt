June 27, 1940.
Mr. Secretary:
Your letter of February 27 to
Mrs. Charles Burnett in reply to hers of
February 25, in regard to the illness of
Count Kaneko, was evidently forwarded by
Mrs. Burnett to Count Kaneko. Hence his
letter to you.
Count Kaneko is a member of the
Japanese Privy Council and although now
very old still enjoys considerable in-
fluence in Japan. He was a friend of
President Theodore Roosevelt and was
active in bringing about American media-
tion in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-
1905. He has always been regarded as
the leader of the pro-American group in
Japan.
In view of Count Kaneko's position
it is thought that you may wish to send
a brief acknowledgment to him of his
letter to you. A draft of a letter is
attached.

Frivolous Melodies by N. B. C.
Spoil Pious Atmosphere of State
Funeral Day.
The Pacific Information, June 6, 1934.
Though undoubtedly without any ill-will, the
NBC of America greatly spoiled the pious atmosphere
of the first night following the burial of the late
Fleet Admiral Togo by broadcasting immediately after
the message of sympathy to Japan by Admiral W. H.
Standley, Chief of the Naval Staff, over our loss of
the great man, such frivolous songs as "Echigo-Jishi",
"Kappore" and "Umegaye", which may be sung with im-
punity at a party among intimate friends where geisha
girls might be invited to serve the guests.
Indeed, tens of thousands of listeners to radio,
including the families of the late Admiral Togo him-
self, were startled to hear the frivolous melodies
on the night of nation-wide sorrow. In this respect,
Captain Taketomi, Chief of the Propaganda Department
of the Naval Office, Lieutenant-Colonel Mikuni of
the Newspaper Section of the War Office, Mr. Keikichi
Tanomogi, M. P., etc. are very indignant, but not so
much with NBC as JOAK, which, they say, should have
turned off the switch as soon as the unrespectable songs
were started. The authorities concerned are, however,
of the opinion that they had better avail themselves
of the present opportunity to tell the NBC and the
American public of the bad taste used on this occasion.

Statement to the Press
The profoundly sad news of the death of Prince Toku-
gawa has inexpressibly shocked my wife and myself. During
the past eight years I have had the privilege of knowing
the Prince well, not only through our contacts in the
America-Japan Society but in many other activities, and
my admiration, respect and affection for him were in the
highest degree heartfelt and complete and will always
continue. As President of the America-Japan Society, a
position to which he devoted constant thought anā effort,
he contributed much toward the relations between our two
countries for which I have always felt deeply grateful.
His passing means a great and personal loss to all of us
who knew him well and we respectfully extend to his ramily
our profound condolence and sympathy no less than to his
country which he served with the highest distinction.
JOSEPH C. GREW

America-Japan Society, has passed away in the fullness of
years;

was a mirror of loyalty, as a citizen of the world he
was acclaimed and revered for his unswerving faith in
the principle of friendship among the nations, and as a
man he was loved for his character and honored for his
good deeds;
AND WHEREAS, as President he directed the America-Japan
Society for sixteen years; toward serving the Society in
achieving its purpose of promoting friendship between the
peoples of the United States and Japan, he gave unstintingly
of himself by wise counsel, indefatigable energy and in-
domitable confidence in the mutual well-being of nations
predestined to be neighbors; and by precept and example
he infused the Society with that vigor which alone made
possible its contributions in the field of constructive
and helpful international relations;

abiding affection and veneration for Prince Tokugawa, its
sense of gratitude for the long and inspiring association
which it was privileged to have with him, and its deep sor-

SIR:

I have the honor to state that I have just
received notice that a subsidy for advertising
expenses has been granted by the Taiwan Tea Merchants'
Association to shippers of Taiwan teas to the United
States in 1939. The Taiwan Tea Merchants' Association
is technically an unofficial organization comprising
all tea manufacturers, middlemen and exporters in
Taiwan, including foreign firms which however have
no voice in the affairs of the association. The
Association also embraces an Association of Exporters
of Tea to "Manchukuo", and an Association of Exporters
of Tea to Other Third Countries, these three inner
associations having been formed in September, 1939.

In the summer of 1939 the Japanese Government
realized that high Yen prices for Taiwan teas, caused
by the abnormal demand in "Manchukuo", would, if
continued, virtually render it impossible for local
exporters to buy Taiwan teas for shipment to third
countries, to obtain foreign exchange so

by Japan. The Government therefore decided that methods of control were needed, and through the medium of the Taiwan Tea Merchants' Association, effected as of August 3, 1939, a policy of collecting Yen 20 per picul (132.277 lbs.) on tea shipped thereafter to "Manchukuo" (mainly) and China on a strictly limited quota basis, and applied the amount collected to a bounty on shipments to third countries. Local authorities and tea merchants feared that an outright bounty on shipments to the United States would raise the question of countervailing duties under the provisions of the Anti-Dumping Act of 1921, and section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930, and for a time it was decided that no bounties would be paid in any form on tea shipments to the United States. However subsequently, the Anglo American Direct Tea Trading Company (American) received a letter dated October 26, 1939 through its New York office (99 Wall St.) from Frank Shaw, Acting Commissioner of Customs, Treasury Department, Washington, stating:

"Reference is made to your letter of October 19, 1939, in which you state that it has been intimated that the Formosa authorities may be paying exporters of tea an advertising allowance to stimulate exports to this country and request to be advised whether in that event such tea would become subject to a countervailing duty equal to such bounty under the provisions of section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930.

The provisions of section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930 apply only to merchandise which is dutiable under the tariff act. It has no application to tea which is free of duty under that act."

On the basis of that letter the four foreign tea
exporters in Taiwan, namely Anglo American, Carter
Nacy Company Inc (American), Tait &amp; Company (British)

While the subsidies are nominally granted for advertising purposes, Carter Macy &amp; Company is reportedly the only firm which is endeavoring to send its grant abroad, but does not expect to meet with much success in obtaining foreign exchange for this purpose. As far as is known other firms will use the amounts granted for ordinary business expenses, there reportedly being no known requirement that the sum granted must actually be used for advertising purposes, and in fact the Japanese government would probably be loath to convert the grants to a sum of some $100,000 to go to the United States to advertise Taiwan teas.

Beginning with January, 1940 a levy of Yen 5 per picul is being made on shipments to "Manchukuo" and China and at the end of the year on the basis of the amount collected together with whatever subsidy may be granted by the Taiwan Government General, it is at present expected that a similar subsidy will be granted to tea shippers on the basis of actual shipments made in 1940.

Inasmuch as the amount to be granted is indefinite, shippers are now being required by this office to make the following statement on invoices:

"No rebates, drawbacks, or bounties have been received for the tea included in this invoice, but may or may not be received in the future, no definite information in regard thereto being available at this time."

The problem naturally arises as to what action if

if any the United States Government might wish to take in connection with duty free subsidized exports. As Mr. Shaw pointed out in his letter, Section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930 applies only to dutiable merchandise, however there may be other regulations with which this office is not familiar, covering subsidized merchandise which is duty free. However that may be, it is pointed out that by counting on a subsidy of some Yen 5 per picul, it will be possible for Taiwan tea shippers to undersell by nearly one cent per pound, shipments of tea to the United States from countries which do not grant a subsidy on tea shipments. Thus in the world tea market a situation similar to that which arose here last fall may well come up, namely that some Taiwan shippers who counted on eventually receiving a subsidy undersold other Taiwan shippers who did not count on a subsidy, causing consternation in the local as well as in the New York tea markets until assurances were given at that time that no subsidies would be granted on shipments of Taiwan teas to the United States.

This office will appreciate being informed of any comments or action which the Government may make in regard to the situation described.

Respectfully yours,

Gerald Warner
American Consul.

The contents of this report have been discussed with officials of the Customs Bureau, who confirm the letter of the Acting Commissioner of Customs dated October 26, 1939 to which reference is made in the despatch, to the effect that items on the free list are not subject to countervailing duties under the provisions of Section 308 of the Tariff Act of 1939. Furthermore, it does not appear that there are any other provisions of the American customs laws which are contravened by the subsidy in question. The Anti-Dumping Act of 1921 provides, in the appropriate circumstances, for the imposition of additional duties when commodities are imported into the United States at less than their "fair value" as defined in the Act. It does not appear, however, that this Act is likely to be applicable in imports of tea in as much as three weeks ago, as in no likelihood of an American subsidy which has passed or extended during free food imports.

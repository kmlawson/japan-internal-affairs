During the last half of 1931 the purchases of
United States gold dollars by Japanese interests were
extraordinarily heavy. There was a general feeling
that Japan would not be able to maintain the gold
standard much longer, and business generally took
care to protect its interests in expectation of a
possible fall in the exchange value of the yen. The
cotton importers bought unusually heavily of American
cotton (reported purchases so far this cotton year
being nearly 1,000,000 bales) and made forward contracts
for dollars to cover their purchases. The large
Japanese banks, trust companies and insurance
companies had surplus funds which they were unable to
utilize to advantage in Japan in the present depressed
condition of industry and commerce, and these funds
were sent abroad to purchase Japanese dollar and
sterling securities issued abroad, or were placed in
the London short-term money market. The newspapers
report that funds to the amount of Yen 100,000,000
were in the London money market when Britain abandoned
the gold standard in September. While a large part of
the dollar purchases were caused by actual needs, a
considerable part also undoubtedly consisted of a
flight of capital from Japan in anticipation of a lower
value of the yen. There was also some speculation in
dollars, but the flight of capital was more important
than the speculation.

Great Britain is worried over the Japanese
abandonment of the gold standard, which is attri-
buted to the loss of t Japan's gold holdings and
a 60% decline of Japanese exports to China. De-
preciation of the yen cuts short a new boom in
British textile exports to Asia and the Chinese
boycott is reported as weakening with the depletion
of stocks. Japanese competition in cotton and
woolen textiles in both India and China will,
therefore, be renewed and the British will have
lost a temporary advantage. Also Japan bought
cotton before going off gold, thereby gaining an
advantage over British spinners who have to buy
cotton with depreciated currency. The result is
that the British spinners are urging their workers
to consent to longer hours and lower wages.

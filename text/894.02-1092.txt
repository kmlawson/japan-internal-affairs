# Government Readjustments:

A number of administrative changes have been recommended by the Administrative and Financial Readjustment Commission, which has been working on a plan of changes in line with financial retrenchment. The proposals put forward by the commission have been submitted to the various departments of the government for revision and amendment, and will be brought up for final decision at a meeting of the Cabinet in October. A number of the changes recommended are regarded as certain of accomplishment. Among these is the abolition of the Department of Overseas Affairs, the newest of the Ministries, which seems to have encountered much difficulty in discharging its supposed function of supervising the colonial governments. A Board of Overseas Affairs will be created to take over the functions of the abolished ministry, and will be attached directly to the Cabinet and under the supervision of the Premier.

The Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry and the Ministry of Commerce and Industry will be merged and renamed the Ministry of Industry. Merger and abolition of various sections and Bureaus of several Departments will be carried out. The Minister of Finance, Mr. Inouye, who is the moving figure behind all these changes, hopes by them to effect a saving of Yen 100,000,000.

According to the press reports the new Ministry will probably be established during the month of December, 1937, and will include five bureaus and one board, as follows:

1. Labor Bureau, supervising the improvement of working conditions, labor hygiene and sanitation, and the adjustment of labor supply and demand;

4. Bureau of Hygiene, handling all problems related to environmental hygiene, adaptability to environment, improvement of housing facilities, nutrition investigations, and control of foodstuffs;

Compromising on the divergent plans submitted by
the Legislative Bureau and the Foreign Office for the
establishment of machinery to govern China affairs, the
Japanese Government decided on March 15 to create the
China Economic Affairs Bureau under the direction of the
Cabinet.

Originally

Originally, the Legislative Bureau proposed the establishment of an organ under the Premier to be called the Cabinet China Affairs Board and to be entrusted with political, economic, and cultural matters relating to China, independently of the Foreign Office. Opposing this plan, Mr. Hirota and the Foreign Office stood out for the establishment of a bureau under the direction of the Minister for Foreign Affairs but not necessarily an integral part of the Foreign Office, to handle economic exploitation of China but without any authority to deal with political or cultural matters, which would be controlled as hitherto by the Foreign Office. According to the Japanese press, the Minister for Foreign Affairs insisted that Japan's policy toward China was entirely different from that toward Manchuria, that the political, economic, and cultural body projected by the Legislative Bureau would possibly be accused of infringing upon China's sovereignty and would hence be displeasing to third countries, and that, aside from relations with the Powers, for technical reasons alone administrative affairs relating to China should be left to the Foreign Office.

Apparently unable to reconcile the conflicting points of view between the Legislative Bureau and the Ministry for Foreign Affairs, the Cabinet arrived at the compromise plan mentioned above, namely, to create the China Economic Affairs Bureau which would have no voice in diplomatic matters and whose principle duty would be to supervise the North China Development Company and the Central China Rehabilitation Company. It is understood that there will also be set up a

a provisional body to be called the China Economic Inquiry Commission, headed by the Premier, composed of members chosen from the Cabinet, the Cabinet's Advisory Counsel, and financial circles, and entrusted with examination of matters relating to economic exploitation of China.

There may be a substantial amount of truth in the report that Mr. Hirota opposed the establishment of a political, economic, and cultural board to control China affairs on the ground that such a step would unnecessarily antagonize countries possessed of treaty rights with China and would lay Japan open to the accusation that it contemplated a definite infringement of China's sovereignty and administrative integrity. While the respect by Japan of China's sovereignty and administrative integrity is only a fiction advanced by the Japanese Government, the placing of China affairs under an organization akin to the Manchurian Affairs Bureau would undoubtedly hamper Japan's well-known dialectics on its actions in China.

It has been remarked by observers in Tokyo that Mr. Hirota's antagonism toward the setting up of a body, distinct from the Foreign Office, to deal with Chinese matters was due to a desire to check the ambitions of the militarists and of the more nationalistic members of the Cabinet. However, it is more likely that the Minister for Foreign Affairs is loath to relinquish a power which he has wielded firmly and is unwilling to turn over the direction of China affairs to a group of men who might take a more moderate view toward the Chinese that that assumed by the Foreign Office itself.

May 5, 1939.

JWB:
MMH:

Tokyo's despatch no. 3814 of April 8, 1939, entitled "Reorganization of the Ministry of Commerce and Industry", reports an extensive reorganization of the Ministry of Commerce and Industry which in practical effect gives relative permanence to government bureaus originally of a temporary character designed to meet an emergency situation. The Embassy states that the newly-reorganized Ministry is to be vested with the authority to regulate commodity prices and to control their demand and supply and may be envisaged as the keystone of the arch of regimentation in Japan. The Embassy points out the resemblance to German and Italian prototypes but does not hazard a guess as to the success of the reorganization. The changes await the submission to the Legislative Bureau of the Cabinet of the necessary drafts of Imperial Ordinances designed to make the changes effective. Physically the changes consist primarily of the expansion of seven existing bureaus within the Ministry and the creation of three independent bureaus under the Supervision of the Ministry, the creation of a new Price Bureau and the establishment of a Deliberative Council for the Adjustment of Demand and Supply. This Council will act as liaison office with War and Navy on matters relating to war-time economic administration.

This is the third of three interesting despatches received in the current pouch from Tokyo competently prepared by Frank Schuler.

The practical effect of the changes described above

originally of a temporary character and designed in large

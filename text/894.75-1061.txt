The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to inform the Department that considerable interest is being shown in a suggestion made by the Minister of Communications to have the telephone system, which is now owned and operated by the State, transferred to private hands.

The principal aim of the plan is to increase the revenues of the Government, which would be the principal stockholder in the proposed company, and its secondary aim

=== Page 2 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

aim is to effect a general improvement and extension of the telephone system, which is so restricted by bureaucratic methods of management that it is a despair to the subscribing public.

Unlike the railways, which are also state-owned but which are operated under a separate account, the finances of the telephone system are a part of the general budget of the Japanese Government. This is an important difference; for while the revenues of the railways can be applied to the construction of new lines, the purchase of equipment, et cetera, the revenues of the telephone system revert to the national treasury and become a part of the general income of the State, such moneys as may be necessary for the extension and improvement of the telephone system being allotted in the light of other estimated expenses of the Government. The telephone system has thus always been hampered by a lack of funds, so that the demand for new telephones and new lines is far beyond the capacity of the telephone authorities to meet; there is a long waiting list of applicants for telephones, with the result that the right to have a telephone in any large city can be sold for a considerable sum of money. In Tokyo, for example, a telephone is worth about Yen 2,000, although at one time it commanded as much as three or four thousand yen. This has transformed what is elsewhere considered to be a necessary method of communication into a luxury which can be afforded only by the comparatively well-to-do.

The general outline of Mr. Koizumi's plan, which he has not apparently worked out in detail, is to organize

a

=== Page 3 === (confidence 1.00)
-3-

a private corporation with a capital of eight hundred million yen; the Government will transfer the telephone system to the corporation in exchange for four hundred million yen of stock, and the remaining stock will be sold to the public in such a way as to secure the widest possible distribution.

It is too early to speculate upon the results which may be obtained by the Minister of Communications, but there are certain political or social aspects of the discussion centering around the transfer of the telephone system to private interests which may be well worthy of mention.

It may seem to be a paradox but it is nevertheless true that, while Japan has a political system based upon extremely conservative principles, it is perhaps the most highly socialized country in the world, with the exception, of course, of Russia. In addition to the railways and telephones, the Government maintains a monopoly of the telegraph system and of the manufacture and sale of tobacco, salt, and crude camphor. The tendency a few years ago was to nationalize other industries, as there was some serious thought of the Government taking over the match industry, and at the present moment there is no little possibility of the manufacture and sale of gasoline being taken over by the Government. In a nation predominantly agricultural and with but three or four industries organized on a very large scale, the enterprises which the Government has already monopolized represent a considerable portion of the industry of the entire country. Each of them yields a fairly

=== Page 4 === (confidence 1.00)
-4-

fairly satisfactory return, but the prices charged for services or for commodities range from high to exorbitant. The profits of the revenues represent from 25 to 30 per cent. of the total ordinary revenues of the Government, but the prices charged are several times higher than would be charged by private corporations under a system of free competition. Although the statement cannot be supported by evidence, the Government monopolies appear to be greatly overstaffed and inefficiently operated, so that while the Government can always be sure of making profits by the simple expedient of raising prices, an unnecessarily heavy burden seems to rest upon the consumer. The country has now labored for ten years under an economic depression, which has lately become more severe rather than showing promise of improvement, and complaints are general against the rates of all the Government monopolies.

Perhaps the desire to take the telephone system out of the Government's hands may be purely a measure of expediency, but I am somewhat disposed to think that it is an unconscious reaction against the tendency towards nationalization. The Minister of Communications must overcome definite opposition as well as great inertia in official quarters before his project is realized. If, however, the operation of the telephone system by private enterprise should be attended by beneficial results, I should look forward to the abandonment by the State of its monopolies rather than its

its absorption of additional industries.

E. Fullam and the Japanese Minister of Communications and a memorandum dated November 4, 1938, in regard to radio telephone and telegraph connections between Japan and South America.

From the viewpoint of the Division of Far Eastern Affairs the significant part of the letter is that the question of participation by the International Telephone and Telegraph Company in the development of telephone communications in China in cooperation with the Central China Telecommunications Company was mentioned between Mr. Fullam and the Japanese Minister of Communications. # MEMORANDUM OF TALK WITH JAPANESE MINISTER OF COMMUNICATIONS.

Mr. Kajii, who has been assisting in the negotiations for radio telephone and telegraph connections between Japan and South America, suggested that it would be advisable for me to see the Minister of Communications in that connection. Accordingly, he arranged a meeting for October 26th. After the usual preliminaries, I explained what Mr. Chapman has been endeavoring to accomplish and I gave the Minister a brief history of our operations in South America to offset some of the misinformation which has been planted around the Department by some of our competitors. The Minister discussed the propositions at some length and then asked me to see the two Director Generals and some of the Engineers.

This second meeting was arranged for the same afternoon and Mr. Kajii accompanied Mr. Chapman and me. Most of the individuals present were quite familiar with our propositions, as a result of Mr. Chapman's conversations, but one or two misconceptions which had grown up were corrected.

The Minister also invited Mrs. Fullam, Mr. Chapman and me to have lunch with him on Monday, October 31st. At the luncheon, which was attended by the Vice Minister and a large number of the Staff, the Minister made a speech in which he indicated that favorable action would be taken on the Japan - Lima telegraph circuit. There will probably be further delay but this is significant because the Japanese

=== Page 10 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

officials seldom go that far until their minds are pretty well made up.

At the October 26th meeting, the Minister brought up the question of Intelco participation in the development of the telephone system in China. He did not put forward any concrete plan and I was unable to obtain any clear indication of what he may have in mind or whether any basis of cooperation has actually been developed.

He did ask me to study the situation during my stay in China and to have another talk with him on my way back.

It is clear that they are looking for foreign financial cooperation in the development of their plans for China. The Minister is one of the leaders of one of the two principal political parties here and, I am advised, is one of the strong men in the Cabinet. Mr. Inada informed me that it is most unusual for one of the Cabinet Members to give such a luncheon for a representative of a foreign company.

J. E. FULLAM

November 4, 1938

I.S.E.C. - SUMITOMO - NIPPON CONTRACT
OF JANUARY 1938

Since our discussions in Osaka early in October, an extended series of negotiations has been carried on between Sumitomo and Nippon officials and the Finance Ministry. On Wednesday, November 2nd, Mr. Obata was here from Osaka and reviewed the negotiations to date with Mr. Ishikawa and me. They feel that the Minister, Vice Minister and other higher officials have now been convinced of the fairness of our position and will try to work out a solution. The next step under Japanese procedure is to convince the sub-officials directly responsible as apparently the higher officials, though willing to indicate their approval of recommendations from below for a fair solution of this matter, will not themselves issue instructions to subordinates to carry out the contract. These subordinates have agreed to see our representatives within the next few days.

Apparently, the difficulty developed in the following manner: Nippon secured preliminary approval of the contract sometime late last year. Regulations were tightened considerably about January 1st but the Minister of Finance was rather lenient in applying these new regulations and applications for exchange, totaling some ¥30,000,000, including our own case and those rated by the Authorities as similar to our case, built up very quickly. As a result the situation became so serious that the Minister was forced out and a new man came in before our transaction could be completed. The new Minister, who is still in power, is a capable business man who was formerly one of the chief Mitsui officials. He and his staff went through this ¥30,000,000 of applications with a fine tooth comb and found many of

=== Page 12 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

highly questionable character. On its face, our case appeared most questionable to the new group because of the large Yen loans to us coupled with applications to buy dollars against accounts owed to us, including the large item of a special 15% dividend. This all placed our people here under considerable suspicion, particularly in view of the pressure they were then exerting and their attempts to explain all the facts were pretty completely discounted. Of course, I stressed again that so far as the January contract was concerned they had a message from us as late as September 7, 1937 suggesting that the matter be dropped until conditions became more settled, so it should be clear that we did not initiate this contract as a scheme to get money out of Japan. Further, with reference to the amounts payable under the General Relations Contract, I continued to stress the position we had been placed in by accepting orders in good faith for goods in the necessity class which we could not dispose of elsewhere, or even stop manufacturing without serious financial loss, when exchange regulations were made retroactive. There is, of course, a substantial difference as compared with cancelling orders for or holding up in customs staple merchandise which the supplier has some chance to take back and realize on.

The most effective argument seems to be that they might just as well start general repudiation of obligations to foreigners, including the Japanese Government U.S. Dollar Bonds, as to force our Japanese associates to default on the January contract after it has been urged by one Government department and informally approved by the Finance Authorities. They still have strong hopes of securing capital from America after settlement of the China incident, either in the form of direct loans or indirectly through associated foreign companies. Particularly on this score, our Embassy representatives,

=== Page 13 === (confidence 0.99)
-3-

who are thoroughly familiar with our negotiations to date, think we have a strong case.

I believe the matter is now fully understood by the principals of the Department of Finance and that they will endeavor to arrive at some fair solution, though it is clear that they are up against a financial situation which is making the supply of essential foreign exchange an exceedingly difficult problem so the question of making good on the contract is one of physical ability as well as of willingness.

J. E. FULLAM.

November 4, 1938.

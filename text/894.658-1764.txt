# Canning Industry in Japan
## INFORMATION ON EXPORT INDUSTRIES—No. 7

## Introductory Remarks.

The canning industry of Japan was founded by a man named Matsuda, who was initiated into the art of preserving sardines in oil by a Frenchman at Nagasaki in 1871. During the experimental period, a mushroom increase was witnessed in the number of enterprisers, most of whom were engaged in the canning of fish. By the time of the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese War, the products of the industry had become indispensable commodities for the armed forces.

The expansion of the trade, however, was due to later cultivation of overseas markets. Beginning in 1907, when our canned crabs first crossed the Pacific Ocean to America, the exports steadily increased year after year. In 1929 canned oranges were for the first time exported to England, and the good reputation that they gained there stimulated the trade to further activities, until at length it developed into an industry having the second largest capacity in the world, i.e. next to the U.S.A. The unique quality and flavour of the products were widely appreciated.

In 1939 it registered an output of 18,290,000 cases, of which 8,670,000 (about ¥150 million) were sent abroad. Thus the canning industry took the third place among our export industries.

After 1939, the year of peak production, however, the industry gradually declined under a multiplicity of unfavorable conditions. In 1941, as the result of the forced consolidation of unit enterprises, there came into existence in each prefecture a federated canning company. In the same year, every agricultural association came to have its own cannery. All these produced exclusively for army requirements, but always with poor results throughout the duration of the War. Japan's loss of Saghalien, Korea and Formosa as the result of the defeat in the War dealt an almost fatal blow to such branches of the industry as the salmon, trout and crab canning. This has placed the trade under the necessity to shift its weight to canning for domestic use.

The post-war extreme depression is the result of these circumstances. However, it has recently begun to show signs of regeneration as an export industry.

## 2. Bottling.

The bottling industry reached its peak in the period 1940—42, producing 4 to 5 million cases. The post-war figures are as follows:—

|  Year | Output  |
| --- | --- |
|  1945 | 291,791 cases.  |
|  1946 | 289,575  |
|  1947 | 732,477  |

The last year’s output represents a 16.8% operation, which is higher than that of the canning industry. Whereas, in case of the latter, the 1947 output showed a decrease against the previous year, the bottling industry more than doubled its output. This is considered due chiefly to the fact that the making of bottles was in more favorable condition than that of cans.

## 3. Causes for the depression of production.

On the whole, the manufacture of both canned foods and bottled foods is in depression. It may be attributed to (1) the difficulty in obtaining materials, especially tin-plates and condiments, (2) the lack of balance between the production cost and the selling price and (3) the shortage of funds. Such bad conditions have hampered production and brought about the stagnancy of the trade.

The Production Programme for the current year calls for an output of 1,500,000 and 750,000 cases in canned and bottled foods respectively.

The attainment of this target is considered not so hard a task for the bottling branch, which showed last year an output of about 730,000 cases. But in the case of the canning branch, for which the production target is three times as high as last year’s production, we shall perhaps have to content ourselves with some 6–700,000 cases.

## III. Business Condition.

### 1. Scale of Enterprises.

The enterprises in our canning and bottling industry may be divided into two groups. One includes those enterprises which are engaged exclusively in the manufacture of one kind of products or another, such as canned salmons, trouts or crabs. And the other group is composed of those enterprises whose products vary according to the change of seasons.

The enterprises falling under the former class are necessitated to have large plants as a natural result of the fact that, in case of failure to treat necessary raw material in as large quantities as possible during a short period of the year when they are found in abundance, they cannot keep up their business at a profit; whereas for the latter, the problem lies in how to keep their plants in operation throughout the year; because they cannot depend for their existence upon manufacture of one single kind of products, and therefore, plants larger than a certain limit will make the enterprise out of profit.

After the War, by the loss of Korea, Formosa, and the fisheries in the North Sea, the sole-product enterprises which had been dependent for their raw materials on salmons, trouts, crabs, or pine-apples, suffered an almost fatal blow. Thus the centre of the trade has now shifted to the domestic canning, which carries on an all-year operation.

An illustration of the all-year operation is afforded by the canneries in the Shizuoka area. From May to August they manufacture oiled tunnies. The flakes, which are produced in the course of the process, are seasoned and made into canned foods. Canned oranges are made from the end of November to March or April of the next year. In the slack season from August to November, they are engaged in the manufacture of canned bamboo-shoots, medlars, peaches, and various fish, or make preparations for the next busy season. The canneries for domestic consumption have always been of a small scale. Although consolidations took place during the War, most of the existing enterprises are still of a medium or small size. Even the largest corporation’s capital does not exceed the ¥3 million mark. Most of the canneries employ 50 to 100 hands, covering an area ranging from 100 to 300 tsubo.

Hokkaido is the area where the canneries are most densely located. Shizuoka follows Hokkaido, and among the rest, the prefectures

## 2. Profit of Business.

It does not pay to put out only 500,000 or 600,000 cases with the present production capacity. So the manufacturers are compelled to produce other goods, such as “tsukudani” (fish or sea-weeds boiled down in soy), boiled or dried sardines, substitute for soy, and pickles. Such state of affairs did not commence after the end of the war, but dates from the early stage of the Sino-Japanese Incident. However, due to a strong support given by the military authorities, the enterprisers could manage to keep on operation during the war. This tendency became more conspicuous with the termination of the war. In the case of “A” Company, for instance, the comparison between the output of canned and bottled goods and that of other goods shows the fact that it is unable to carry on operation without manufacturing other goods, while “B” Company, which is making efforts to keep up the management mainly by the manufacture of canned and bottled goods, is also making other goods in order to eke out its income.

To explain more concretely, we may classify the enterprises into two groups. One is of those enterprises which are engaged in the production of raw materials, the processing of the same, and the sale of finished foodstuffs. The other is of those which are confined to processing. The former, besides manufacture of canned and bottled goods, is carrying on ice-manufacturing, refrigeration, warehousing, agricultural, live stock and aquatic industries. The latter, chiefly looking forward to the future of the canned and bottled industry, is only carrying on the manufacture of “tsukudani” and seasonings as a subsidiary business in order to overcome the difficult situation. Owing to their fund condition, however, those who are able to convert their enterprises into such a large-scale industry as the former group are few, and most of the enterprisers belong to the latter group carrying on a subsidiary business up to the extent consistent with their financial condition.

## V. Demand and Supply Condition of Raw Materials.

This industry, which showed a brilliant prosperity in pre-war years producing as much as ten and several millions of cases and exporting half of them, is now managing to produce only one million cases per annum. The main reasons for this depression are the decreased production, caused by the war, of the raw materials in general—agricultural, stock-breeding and aquatic products—and the loss of the places of their origin, i.e., the North Sea fishing grounds, Korea and Formosa.

The North Sea fishing grounds, which included Kamchatka, Saghaline and North Kurile Islands, and produced each year 2,000,000 cases of canned salmons and trouts and 300,000 cases of canned crabs, have been almost wholly lost, and our fishing grounds are confined to a part of Hokkaido, where 10% of the former output can hardly be maintained. The loss of Korea, in which canned sardines preserved in tomato ketchup was originated and which was one of the principal producing districts, is also a heavy blow.

Furthermore, by the loss of Formosa, the resources of sugar and pine-apples are lost. The highest output of pine-apples in pre-war years reached 1,800,000 cases, ranking third in the world and next to Hawaii and Malaya. The ratio of the lost districts of resources in the sphere of canned-goods production is roughly 40-50%.

A great bottle-neck is also formed by the facts that materials cannot be acquired regularly, and that, as the selling prices are officially fixed, the makers are unable to buy materials as freely as they wish.

The greatest bottle-neck concerning raw materials lies in cans. It is no exaggeration to say that here is the fundamental cause for the stagnation of production. The can manufacturing industry was, for the first time, separated from the canning industry in 1917, when the Toyo Seikan Kabushiki Kaisha (The Oriental Can Mfg. Co., Ltd.) was established. This separation gave rise to a remarkable growth of canning industry. The highest output of cans in pre-war times was roughly from 1,400,000 to 1,500,000 cases (more than 60,000 tons). As the result of the war, the Takao Factory of the Oriental Can Mfg. Co. located at Takao, Formosa, and the Korean Can Mfg. Co. located at Fuzan, Korea, were lost. But no damage was done to the factories in Japan proper, which still retain the capacity to digest 60,000 tons of tin-plate per annum. The shortage of tin-plate, however, is rendering the production of cans stagnant. The betterment of coal and electric power supply and the increased production of iron and steel will improve tin-plate supply. On the part of the can manufacturers, they are also looking forward to import from the U.S.A. Already from the U.S.A., 200,000 cases in April, and 104,160 cases in May (304,160 cases in total) were imported this year, and 80,000 cases more are expected to be imported. Bottles are made in this country, but they also are dependent upon the supply of coal. The production capacity of bottles per annum is roughly 1,500,000—2,000,000 cases.

The lack of seasoing materials—sugar, saccharine, durchin, table salt, table oil, soy, etc.—is also a great obstacle to the production, and we have to depend upon imports from abroad.

VI. Fund situation.

This industry is not free from the tightened fund situation prevailing in the industrial circles. It is essential in producing foods for export that products be sanitary. For this purpose, sanitary facilities must be prepared. The demand for working capital also being large, credit demand amounts to a considerably great volume. In the production programme set for this year, this industry demands ¥150,000,000 as equipment funds. The breakdown is ¥45,000,000 (30%) for expenses to improve and enlarge equipments, ¥90,000,000 (60%) for expenses to repair factories and ¥15,000,000 for cost of raw materials. Thus the utmost importance is attached to the repair of worn-out factories. As for working funds, the demand of this industry totals ¥675,000,000, which is necessary for 1,500,000 cases of canned goods. (¥450 per case).

Adding the working funds of ¥675,000,000 to the equipment funds of ¥150,000,000, the total funds reach the immense amount of ¥825,000,000. It is intended that equipment funds be advanced by the Reconversion Finance Bank, and funds for purchasing raw materials be accommodated principally by the ordinary commercial banks.

According to the present loan priority order of canning industry, equipment funds are B and working funds are A-2. In the bottling industry, equipment funds are C. and working funds are B. It is desired to raise the loan priority order of the funds for the production of canned goods to A, and to make special accommodation, beforehand, of them according to the production programme of canned goods for export of this year.

VII. Conclusion.

The canning and bottling industry, which was at the height of its prosperity in former days, is now stagnant in its production, notwithstanding its seemingly excessive equipments and capacity. Comparing the operation condition of canning industry with that of bottling industry, the former is less active than the latter. Although various reasons may be counted for this, the fundamental one seems to be the lack of cans. The future of this industry may, therefore, be said to depend upon whether or not tin-plates for cans may be imported from abroad. We are obliged to admit the fact mentioned before, whether this tendency is right or wrong, that the production of 50,000—60,000 cases under the present production capacity is not profitable,

and this fact is causing, in this industry, a tendency to operate additionally other food processing industries.

As for the demand, the domestic one is larger at present than that in pre-war years, and export will also increase. Now that, however, the vast districts of resources have been lost as the result of the war, it may be hard for this industry to recover its pre-war prosperity.

At present, the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry has a programme to allocate canned goods for export and bottled goods for domestic use. Production of canned goods for export necessities, naturally, improvement of quality and high degree of sanitariness and this renders it absolutely essential for this industry to adjust and consolidate enterprises. As the result of the abrogation, however, of the Enterprise License Ordinance and Controlling Company Ordinance, many new enterprises have appeared, and the Japan Canning Industry Co., Ltd., the control organ of federated companies, has been reorganized into the Public Corporation for Foodstuff Distribution and federated companies everywhere are tending to split themselves, accelerating the dwindling of enterprises. For the purpose of making the most rational and efficient use of raw materials, and of raising the output to the highest possible level, it is required that the dispersed factories should be concentrated, according to a definite plan, and reorganized into factories having superior equipment and technique. Then the production will increase by such means as using by-products, and full use will be made of funds and raw materials.

As will be seen from the foregoing remarks, the prospect of this industry is not rosy but rather full of difficulties. However, the rationalization and consolidation of enterprises and the import of can materials, together with the past experience and superior techique, will enable the industry to overcome its difficulties and recover its former prosperity to some extent.

The following information is provided in the original document image:

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to forward the following statistical tables as of possible interest to the Department of Commerce:

- (1) Production of Canned Marine Products in Japan;
- (2) Products of Canned Market Products from Japan;
- (3) Production of Canned Fruits in Japan;
- (4) Products of Canned Fruits from Japan;
- (5) Production of Canned Vegetables in Japan;
- (6) Canned Vegetables from Japan;
- (7) Production of Canned Meat and Dairy Products in Japan;
- (8) Department of Commerce and Dairy Products from Japan.

The enclosed tables were prepared by the Canned and Bottled Food Investigator of Japan (Kambagama Kyokai), 567 Marunouchi Building, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo, in response to a letter received by the Bureau of Scientific Section, General Headquarters, SCAP, 10-21 National Office, International Tin Study Group, 7 10-21, Tokyo, in response to a letter from the 1937 through 1947, supplementary information not being available according to the Association.

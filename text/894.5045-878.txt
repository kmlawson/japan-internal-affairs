The strike in Tokyo of more than 10,000 street-car and bus operators was said to be the largest transportation strike that has yet occurred in Japan*. The capital's transportation was not, however, hopelessly tied up, for volunteers ran both street cars and buses and suffered no violence unless collision with one another through inexperience is not excluded. At one of the busiest car crossings of the city, one volunteer motorman struck a pedestrian. Sympathetically he hailed a taxi, put the injured man in it, and escorted him to a hospital. Meanwhile his street-car remained unattended for forty minutes tying up traffic. The sympathy of the people seemed not to be with the strikers, while the officials were well-prepared to oppose them. Within a few days the strike collapsed. Three months previously the same workers had gone on strike in protest to a suggestion of bonus reduction and postponement of wage

With respect to labor, one matter ceased to interest the public during the month under review: the labor troubles at three mills of the Kanegafuchi Spinning Company, reported in the two previous monthly reports, came to an end. On June 2nd the dispute at the Hyogo Mill was concluded after a period of 53 days, the principal terms of the agreement being reported in the press as follows:

The thirty per cent. war-time allowance to be included in the wages during the second half of this year;

Reduction to be made by a partial lowering in the rent of the company's houses, bath, gas, medical and barber's charges;

The strikers' headquarters to be dissolved

No branch of a workers' union to be established at the Hyogo Mill.

The disputes at the Osaka and Kyoto mills were settled on June 4th after a period of 53 days, the chief points of the settlement being reported as follows:

The reduction in income of workers, resulting from reduction in allowances, shall be made good out of the prosperity promotion funds and other ways.

The thirty per cent. allowance shall be included in the wages from the month of July this year.

No further reductions in wages shall be made in the near future.

Strikes.

Endurance chimney sitting as a method of settling strikes has become popular in Japan since a striker of the Fuji Cotton Spinning Company at Kawasaki succeeded in obtaining settlement of a strike last November by remaining aloft on a chimney for one hundred and thirty hours. This record was broken during May by one Hiroshi Chiba, who broke the previous record by remaining on top of a stack of the Japan Dying Company's plant in Asakusa for 12 days, 4 hours and 22 minutes, until the strike at the plant had been settled. It was feared that the man would succeed before the strike could be settled as he was in a very serious condition due to exposure and lack of food. As usual in such cases in Japan, Chiba was regarded as a hero and attempts have been made to follow his example. The Sumitomo Steel works in Osaka, which has been having labor troubles and which has ten chimneys at the works, has cut the ladders twenty feet from the ground.

A compromise was reached in a miners' strike in Fukuoka after two hundred men remained below the surface for 125 hours, until the strike was settled.

I have the honor to refer to despatch No. 908 of September 21, 1934, "Tokyo Tram and Bus Strike", and to submit the following in connection with the progress and conclusion of the strike of the Tokyo Traffic Labor Union which was called on September 5, 1934, involving 10,200 tram and bus workers.

On September 23 an arbitration committee was formed in accordance with the Labor Disputes Arbitration Law which called for compulsory arbitration. The committee was represented by three officials of the Municipality, three leaders of the strikers and three neutral members. Pending the outcome of the arbitration the majority of strikers returned to work on September 21.

As a first condition to a compromise, the Union representatives insisted that:

(1) The Tokyo City Electric Bureau withdraw its readjustment plan involving the wholesale dismissal of all workers and

their re-employment at wages reduced by 20 to 48 percent.

(2) The 99 workers who had been dismissed during the beginning of the strike should be reinstated.

The opinion of the neutral members of the committee was that such a readjustment plan as proposed would be most undesirable as a precedent to general employment.

On the part of the Electric Bureau, the chief difficulty involved in the withdrawal of the readjustment plan appeared to be in the question of "saving face".

After many sessions the following compromise plan was formulated, which was approved by five out of nine members of the arbitration committee:

(1) The readjustment plan to be withdrawn.
(2) Wage-cut of 20 percent to be instituted.
(3) Allowances to be given to workers who volunteered to resign before October 14.
(4) Discharged workers to be re-employed after a disciplinary period.

The Union representatives objected to the compromise, contending that the 20 percent wage-cut would in actuality amount to a 35 percent reduction, when all factors were considered. They objected also to the proposed disciplining of strike leaders by suspension from employment. Since their objections were overridden by a plurality of the members of the arbitration committee, they rejected the compromise plan. On October 7 the strike was resumed, and the struggle became a direct test between the Municipal Electric Bureau and the Tokyo Traffic Labor Union.

When it became apparent that the trend of the dispute was definitely against the strikers, an offer of mediation was made to them by the Superintendent of the Police Board, who counselled them to leave the whole matter without reservation in his hands, and advised that further continuance of the strike might compel settlement on less favorable terms. His offer was finally accepted by the strikers, and he was entrusted to act as their representative in negotiating with the Electric Bureau. The Union made two demands:

(1) A wage-cut lower than 20 percent.

(2) Reinstatement of the 99 workers who had been dismissed.

IX. LABOR AND EMIGRATION

(a) Labor

One of the largest labor disputes in Japan's history occurred on September 5 when some 10,200 Tokyo tramway and bus employees went on strike in protest against the decision of the Tokyo City Electric Bureau to dismiss permanently 380 employees, and dismiss the remaining 9,822 employees and re-employ them the next day with an average pay-cut of 10%. The Tokyo City Electric Bureau had hoped to save $1,500,000 annually in running expenses by means of this curtailment of wages. Although the strike lasted 12 days, it was on the whole extremely orderly. Following the decision of the Metropolitan Police Board to order compulsory arbitration of the controversy under authority of the Labor Dispute Arbitration Law, the employees announced that the strike would be suspended on September 17 because of the order of compulsory arbitration and because the Tokyo City Electric Bureau had resolved to reserve enforcement of its wage-cut plan. A large part of the strikers returned to work, and on September 2 the arbitration proceedings were begun. The arbitration committee met with a firm stand on the part of the strikers' representatives, who were reported by the press to be determined in their demand for a substantial reduction in the announced 40% wage cut, and at the end of the month it appeared likely that another strike would be called unless the employees' demands were complied with.

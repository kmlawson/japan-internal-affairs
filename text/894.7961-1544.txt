INTERNATIONAL AIR TRAFFIC IN JAPAN

1. Information contained herein is published in accordance with
paragraph 11b of Circular 8, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for
the Allied Powers, 30 August 1946, and shall be brought to the attention
of all countries, agencies and aircrews flying aircraft into Japan. All
previous instructions on this subject are rescinded.

2. The purpose of this circular is to establish standard procedures
to be followed by all aircraft cleared into or out of Japan, in accordance
with Circular 8, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied
Powers, 30 August 1946.

3. Due to the unpredictable character of weather conditions normally
encountered over Japan and the extremely rugged nature of the terrain
along available approach routes between the Asiatic Mainland, the Southern
Islands, and Japan, the continued safe conduct of flights is becoming a
matter of increasing concern to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

4. With the foregoing objects in mind, the following procedures will
apply to all international air traffic cleared into and out of the Tokyo
Area, Japan, with the exception of regularly scheduled air traffic along
organized air routes as approved by the Supreme Commander for the Allied
Powers.

a. Approach Routes and Check Points. To aid in the control of
air traffic and to facilitate search for missing aircraft, all flights
into Japan shall be conducted as follows:

(1) Any aircraft approaching Japanese territory from the
north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan shall conduct
their flight so as to pass directly over the town of
Ryozu (38°04'N, 138°20'E) on Sado Island at an altitude
of 10,000 feet, or at a higher or lower altitude if
weather conditions so require. This point is designated
"Check Point Sado." From check point Sado, all flights
shall proceed to Haneda Airfield (35°34'N, 139°46'E) via
one of the following airway routes (see inclosures 2 and
3):

(a) North Route. Check point Sado to check point Niigata (37°56'N, 139°02'E), thence to check point Koriyama (37°24'N, 140°23'E), thence southward generally along railroad to check point Koga (36°12'N, 139°42'E), thence south to Haneda Airfield.

(b) South Route. Check point Sado to check point Niigata (37°56'N, 139°02'E), thence to check point Nagaoka (37°26'N, 138°50'E), thence generally via railroad to check point Maebashi (36°24'N, 139°04'E), thence to check point Koga (36°12'N, 139°42'E), thence south to Haneda Airfield.

(2) Any aircraft approaching Japanese territory from the west, south, and east of Honshu shall conduct their flight so as to enter Japan by means of established airways as indicated in Inclosures 2 and 3, and shall proceed to Haneda Airfield (35°34'N, 139°46'E) as follows:

(a) West. Enter Japan by passing over check point Fukuoka (33°36'N, 130°24'E), thence to check point Osaka (34°40'N, 135°30'E), thence to check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(b) South. Enter Japan by passing over check point Kanoya (31°23'N, 130°52'E), thence to check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(c) East. Enter Japan by passing over check point O-Shima (34°44'N, 139°24'E), thence to Haneda Airfield.

(3) The above air routes normally encompass the airspace within twenty-five (25) miles over water and ten (10) miles over land, on either side of the rhumb lines connecting the check points listed.

(4) Inclosures 2 and 3 outline air routes to be used in entering Tokyo Area, Japan.

b. Entry Procedure.

(1) Escort aircraft under control of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers may meet inbound flights at any point along the outlined air routes over Japan. Detailed

escort instructions may be provided at the time the flight is cleared for entry.

(2) If escort aircraft do not meet inbound flights approaching from north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan, the aircraft cleared for entry will proceed from Niigata to Haneda Airfield along the northern route as indicated in paragraph 4a (1).

(3) If escort aircraft do meet inbound flights approaching from north of Honshu over the Sea of Japan, the aircraft cleared for entry will follow the decision of the escort commander as to choice of northern or southern routes from Niigata to Haneda Airfield as indicated in paragraph 4a (1).

(4) Entrance to Japan from the east, south and west is confined to one established air route over Japan with no alternate so that only the outlined route will be used.

(5) In event of interception by escort aircraft, all instructions given by the escort commander will be followed implicitly.

(6) Aircraft proceeding along above established air routes will avoid flying over airfields, remaining at least three miles from them, and will use caution in avoiding local air traffic which may be in the vicinity of the airfield.

c. Arrival Procedure.

(1) Upon arrival in the Tokyo Area, aircraft will circle Haneda Airfield within a five mile radius, flying not lower than 1,500 feet over the city areas and vicinity, and will request landing instructions from Haneda tower.

(2) Upon landing at Haneda, Airplane or Flight Commander shall present in person to the Base Operations Officer or his representative a copy of his load manifest, with complete list in English of passengers and crew members. List of passengers and crew members shall show full name of each person and military rank (if applicable). The Operations Officer at Haneda Airfield will then give the Airplane or Flight Commander the necessary instructions regarding parking of airplane and any other pertinent instructions required under the circumstances.

d. Departure Procedure.

The foregoing entry and arrival procedures shall be followed in reverse by aircraft leaving the Tokyo Area. Notice of time of intended departure and outbound passenger lists shall be filed with the Operations Officer at Haneda Airfield at least 24 hours prior to projected takeoff. Detailed instructions as given at aircrew briefing will be followed.

e. Communications Procedure. The following communications procedures are announced for arriving and departing aircraft.

(1) An English speaking radio operator or other crew member will accompany each airplane or flight of aircraft into Japan in order to conduct English language communications as indicated below.

(2) The call word "New Comer" shall be used to identify all aircraft cleared into or out of Japan, in accordance with Circular 8, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, 30 August 1946. When two or more aircraft are in a flight, each shall be identified as "New Comer One," "New Comer Two," etc., followed by its radio call sign.

(3) Any time that inbound or outbound flights proceeding along the designated air routes over Japan are met by escort aircraft, "New Comer" aircraft will be prepared to transmit and receive on 116.10 megacycles. The escort aircraft will use the identifying call word "Escort."

(4) Initial contact will be made with "Tokyo Control" upon arrival in Tokyo Control Area (100-mile radius around Tokyo). "Tokyo Control" will then grant permission to continue on course to Haneda Airfield, or will issue pertinent flight instructions. "Tokyo Control" will be contacted by "New Comer" Flight Commander using voice communication in English. "Tokyo Control" will transmit and receive on 5055 kilocycles during the day and 2975 kilocycles at night. In addition, "Tokyo Control" is prepared to transmit and receive using voice communication on 116.10 and 126.18 megacycles. "Tokyo Control" also transmits and receives on 6910 kilocycles, continuous wave (CW), during the day and night.

(4) When aircraft land at airfields other than Haneda, the nearest United States Army Commander will be contacted for instructions.

(5) Air-sea rescue facilities are available and standing by for immediate action. Contact "Tokyo Control" on voice or continuous wave frequencies, or "Haneda Tower" on voice frequencies, and briefly explain the situation. Use the international call "Hayday" to attract attention to the emergency.

g. Miscellaneous.

(1) Aircraft will not fly lower than 1,500 feet over the city area of Tokyo and vicinity. Radio towers approximately 1,100 feet high are located in two areas, north and east of the city.

(2) In order to prevent unnecessary accidents and to insure the safety of all personnel in flight and on the ground, no aircraft operating in Japan under the provisions of this circular will carry mounted weapons.

(3) Agencies designated to implement the provisions of Circular 8, General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, will take every precaution within capabilities to insure flying safety, safe passage, and compliance with these procedures, including the providing of communications facilities, aids to air navigation, airfield weather service, aircrew briefing, and air escort when deemed necessary, for international air traffic entering Japan.

5. Careful compliance with the foregoing procedures is necessary in order to avoid possible embarrassment since Airfield Commanders have been instructed to detain all aircraft and occupants not properly cleared or which violate the terms of clearance. In such cases of detention, all personnel will be accorded every courtesy and facility until their status has been properly cleared with the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

(5) When "New Comer" aircraft have been granted permission to proceed to Haneda Airfield by "Tokyo Control," "New Comer" Flight Commander shall call "Haneda Tower" using voice communication in English on 4495 kilocycles and request landing instructions. "Haneda Tower" will respond on 280 kilocycles. In the event of instrument flying conditions or other emergency, either "Tokyo Control" or "Haneda Tower" will give definite instructions to "New Comer" aircraft. In addition to 4495 kilocycles and 280 kilocycles, "Haneda Tower" is prepared to transmit and receive on 126.18 and 116.10 megacycles.

(6) Whenever there is a radio communication failure between "Haneda Tower" and "New Comer" aircraft, the portable traffic control light will be used to direct the movement of aircraft in the air, on landings, on takeoffs, and on the ground as outlined in inclosure 1.

(7) Inclosure 4 indicates radio navigational aids currently in use.

f. Emergency Procedure.

(1) "Haneda Tower" is prepared to transmit and receive in English on 140.58 megacycles (secondary frequency).

(2) Haneda Airfield is equipped with special instrument let-down equipment called "Ground Controlled Approach" (GCA). With this equipment specially trained operators on the ground transmit verbal instructions to aircraft which, if followed implicitly, will enable the pilot to effect a safe landing under practically any weather conditions. No special equipment is required in the airplane other than radio receiving equipment capable of reception on Control Tower frequencies mentioned above. Under instrument weather conditions, the tower operator will pass control of aircraft to the Ground Controlled Approach operator (voice call "Zippo"), who will verbally direct the pilot of the aircraft to perform the necessary maneuvers to effect a safe landing. In view of this service, incoming flights need not be diverted to other airfields under inclement weather conditions except in case of radio or mechanical failure.

(3) In event of a bona fide emergency, landings may be made at any airfield in Japan. (See paragraph 5.)

STANDARD LIGHT SIGNALS

1. General. The portable traffic control light is a small, easily handled directive light, which emits an intense, narrow beam of light. The color of the light (white, green, or red) is controlled by the operator through a system of levers and triggers in the two handles. The normal range of the signal in ordinary weather is ten (10) miles at day time and fifteen (15) miles at night. Signals are readily discernible to the pilot of any aircraft visible to the operator. This light shall be used to control the movement of personnel and vehicles on the landing area and the landings and take offs of any aircraft not equipped with radio.

2. Signals from a portable traffic control light shall mean the following:

a. To Aircraft in Flight.

(1) A green light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED TO LAND."

(2) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "GIVE WAY TO OTHER AIRCRAFT AND CONTINUE CIRCLING."

(3) A series of red flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a recall signal to indicate to the pilot that it is desired that he return and land. (Light signal to indicate clearance to land must also be given when aircraft is in proper position.)

(4) A series of alternating red and green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a general warning signal to advise a pilot to be on the alert for hazardous or unusual conditions.

b. To Aircraft on the Airfield.

(1) When a pilot is taxiing:

(a) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "STOP."

(b) A series of red flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean that a pilot is to taxi back to the hangar line.

(c) A series of green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED TO CONTINUE TAXING."

(2) When a pilot is in position for take off:
(a) A red light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEAR THE RUNWAY IMMEDIATELY AND WAIT."
(b) A green light from a directed traffic control light shall be used to mean, "CLEARED FOR TAKE-OFF."

(3) General Warning Signal. A series of alternating red and green flashes from a directed traffic control light shall be used as a general warning signal to advise a pilot or driver of a vehicle or personnel on the landing area to be on the alert for hazardous or unusual conditions.

(4) Attention is directed to the fact that the warning signal is not a prohibitive signal and may be followed by either a red or green light as circumstances warrant.

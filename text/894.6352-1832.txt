Gen. MacArthur's headquarters has launched an
official investigation of reports that a group of
American traders are secretly selling war-potential
Japanese copper to countries behind the Iron Curtain.

SCAP officials told the United Press that reports
from a number of sources alleged that the copper pur-
chased in Japan ostensibly for use in Holland, Belgium
and France, was transshipped to Poland and Czechoslovakia.

"We recognize the seriousness of the reports," one
official said. "If true, the least that could happen to
the traders involved would be to have their licenses re-
voked. They would be told to leave Japan."

Another official said that three contracts under investigation involved some 4,000 tons of electrolytic copper valued at approximately $1,000,000.

Copper is on the list of strategic war materials whose export to Russia and satellite countries is banned by the United States.

The shipments were alleged to have been made since July 1 of this year when the first offerings of Japanese copper were made to foreign buyers.

Apparently this report was published in the United States, where the United Press called it to the Department's attention. On September 25, 1949, the Nippon Times published the following United Press report from Washington under the heading "U. S. State Dept. to Probe Nippon Copper Sale Issue".

WASHINGTON, Sept 24 State Department officials said they intended to conduct an "intensive investigation" into charges that certain American traders, who purchased Japanese copper, have been selling it to Soviet satellite countries instead of Western European nations for which it ostensibly was destined.

The officials said they received full reports from Tokyo but could not make further comment until they looked into the matter more closely.

Publication of Mr. Kalischer's report, together with that of the brief United Press report from Washington, is reliably reported to have aroused considerable attention in highest SCAP echelons. According to Colonel Ralph J. MITCHELL, official in the Foreign Trade and Commerce Division, Economic and Scientific Section (ESS), General Headquarters, a complete and detailed report on all copper exports from Japan has been ordered to be prepared by Major General W. F. MARQUAT, Chief, ESS. Colonel Mitchell, who supervises Japan's trade with the USSR and with Communist China, stated to an officer of this Mission on September 27, 1949, that he had been directed to prepare this report and hoped to have it completed within four or five days.

It remains to be seen whether this report will be prepared within the time stated, and whether General Headquarters will then decide to refer a copy thereof to this Mission for transmittal to the Department with the request that investigations of the allegations be conducted by the Embassies at London, Brussels, and possibly also The Hague.

The Department may wish to note that this matter was first called to the attention of an officer of this Mission on August 29, 1949, when four American metal traders voiced their apprehensions over possible transshipment of Japanese copper from London and Antwerp to Iron Curtain areas. A copy of a memorandum of this conversation is enclosed. It will be noted further that a copy of this memorandum was made available on the following day to Colonel Mitchell, who stated that the allegations made by these traders were serious and that ESS should take immediate steps to investigate them "before the whole mess blows up in our faces." In the latter view, the officer of this Mission concurred, he has since discussed this subject on some eight or 10 occasions with Colonel Mitchell, who frankly indicated that he had not been able to arouse the interest of other ESS officials in the need for immediate investigation. In fairness, however, it should be stated that those officials have been preoccupied with the visits to Tokyo of Under Secretary of the Army Tracy S. VOORHEES, the Congressional Mission, and, more recently, with the problems caused by sterling devaluation.

There is also enclosed a copy of a memorandum dated September 26, 1949, prepared by Colonel Mitchell for General Marquet on the subject "Shipments of Electrolytic Copper." This memorandum, a copy of which was made available by Colonel Mitchell to an officer of this Mission on a strictly personal basis, contains the basic data regarding the shipments of copper which presumably will require investigation.

What now appears most probable is that General Headquarters may, within one week, despatch an urgent telegram to the Department of the Army in this regard, possibly asking that Department to take up this matter with the Department. We feel, however, that the Department will welcome the advanced information contained in the enclosures to this despatch and may wish to instruct the Embassies concerned to undertake investigations prior to the receipt of the telegram from General Headquarters.

This Mission will expect to report further on this subject in the near future.

Respectfully yours,

# MEMORANDUM OF CONVERSATION

**PARTICIPANTS:**
Mr. Carl D. ROSS, World Commerce Corp., New York City.
Mr. Hans A. RIES, Philipp Bros., Inc., New York City.
Mr. P. RIESS, Metal Traders, Inc., New York City.
Mr. S. A. NEWIRTH, Associated Metals &amp; Minerals Corp., New York City.
Mr. Carl H. BOEHRINGER, Economic Counselor, USPOLAD

**SUBJECT:**
Possible Shipments of Japanese Copper to Satellite Areas

**DATE:**
August 30, 1949

**PLACE:**
Office of the United States Political Adviser, GHQ, SCAP

Mr. Ross called by appointment made August 29 accompanied by those indicated above. In making the appointment, Mr. Ross said he wished to discuss a subject which he thought was of considerable importance to Americans generally and to the State Department particularly. He said he wished to call upon me in the company of a number of his competitors in the minerals and metals trade, this, he said, being an indication as to the seriousness of the subject to be discussed.

Upon arrival at my office on August 29, Mr. Ross stated that he and his associates wished to discuss recent shipments of electrolytic copper to Europe, which copper he said had been purchased at prices above world market prices for even better grades of copper, and their suspicions that some of the copper bought in Japan for export to London and Antwerp had been transshipped to countries behind the Iron Curtain.

Mr. Ross stated that Japan, with ESS approval, was now offering for export 5,500 metric tons of copper, with tenders scheduled to be received on or before August 29, 1949, but with validations to be made later. He referred to two lots of copper which had been put up for sale, and largely shipped, in June and July, 1949, in the aggregate of 10,000 metric tons. He said that he and his associates present had placed bids on that copper but that it had been sold at prices one to one and one-half cents higher than they were prepared to pay on the basis of world market prices. Mr. Ross went on to say that he and his associates were "almost positive" that much of the copper

Mr. Ries stated at this point that he had been so concerned

Mr. Newirth stated that the finest American copper could not

by those presumably interested in transshipping that copper from
certain European ports to Iron Curtain areas. One of the metal
traders then said that GHQ, SCAP apparently was closing its eyes
to shipments of copper to Iron Curtain countries; that all con-
tracts were supposed to designate final destinations; and that the
question of violating "sworn statements" in that connection was being
raised.

The discussion brought out the following: of 3,000 metric tons
of copper shipped during June and July from Japan to London, the en-
tire amount was believed to have been transshipped from London to
Poland. In this connection, Mr. Ross exhibited the following report
circulated locally as a Reuter's despatch from London dated August 8,
1949:

"Metal circles here report that a British firm has
bought about 3,000 tons of Japanese copper for shipment
to the United Kingdom. The metal, the bulk of which is
in cathode form, is believed to have been purchased on
the basis of 15 cents per pound free on board Japanese
ports. In view of the fact that private imports into
the United Kingdom have not yet been permitted, trade
circles here believe that on arrival here, it will be
transshipped to Europe, possibly to Poland."

It was then stated that of some 7,000 metric tons of copper
shipped in June and July from Japan to Antwerp about 5,000 tons was
believed to have been shipped to countries behind the Iron Curtain,
especially to Poland and Hungary but possibly in smaller amounts to
Rumania and Czechoslovakia.

Upon being questioned as to what firms or individuals might be
making these shipments, my callers stated that most shipments had
been made by the Felix Kramarsky Corporation, New York City, repre-
sented here by Mr. Bruce AITCHISON, an American lawyer. The second
company, shipping mainly to London while the former firm shipped
mainly to Antwerp, was Mr. S. L. Barnett, a name which they con-
sidered was a dummy name of an individual supposedly living in London
or in the United States. They said that the said "Barnett" was con-
nected with several firms in the United States, including the Nago

My informants stated that, as American citizens, they were primarily concerned over possible transshipments of Japanese copper to Iron Curtain countries; however, as businessmen, they were interested in knowing whether such transshipments were actually allowed by General Headquarters, and if so, they desired to participate in that business. They raised the question that if copper was being permitted exportation to countries from which it would be transshipped to Iron Curtain areas, there might be a distinct possibility that shipments of other metals might also be made from Japan, specific reference being made to aluminum and lead.

In closing, my informants stated that their suspicions regarding the possible exportation of copper to satellite countries had been aroused by statements made to them by Japanese copper refiners who assertedly had told them that, in their opinion, it was strange that their former American customers had not been buying copper from Japan and that some new buyers had entered the market presumably for the purpose of over-bidding on price for the avowed purpose of buying copper for the account of certain satellite countries.

MEMORANDUM FOR: Chief Economic and Scientific Section

SUBJECT: Shipments of Electrolytic Copper

1. During the latter part of June 1949 the Foreign Trade and Commerce Division of the Economic and Scientific Section released for sale and export to Japan of all related tons of electrolytic copper in cathode and cars.

2. Copper being a source of metal and subject to certain restrictions as to shipping destination, all contracts executed in connection with these transactions cannot specify clauses as to destination and final convention. These contracts were signed by the Tokyo representative of the purchasing trading firms.

3. Certain allegations have been made by competing traders and information has been presented that raises the question as to whether the terms of these contracts were based upon or not. It has been further alleged that such a time copper has been reshipped to USSR satellite contracts in Eastern Europe.

4. In the 9 August 1949 issue of UNITED Economic Service under an 8 August London note that the following statement appeared:

"Metal firms here report that a British firm has bought about 2,000 tons of Japanese copper for shipment to the United Kingdom. The metal, the bulk of which is in cathode form, is believed to have been purchased on the basis of 10 cents per pound free on board Japanese ports. In view of the fact that private imports into the United Kingdom have not been permitted, trade circles here believe that on arrival here it will be transshipped to Europe, possibly to Poland."

5. Immediately following the publication of the foregoing certain of the Tokyo representatives of trading firms made an appointment with the Division of Economic Liaison of the Diplomatic Section to discuss

The Acting Political Adviser has the honor to refer to this mission's despatch no. 676 dated September 27, 1949, entitled "Investigation of Possible Shipments of Japanese Copper to Satellite Areas," and to report on the present status of the investigations in this regard currently being conducted by the Economic and Scientific Section (ESS), General Headquarters, SCAP.

The Economic Counselor on September 30, 1949, called upon Colonel Ralph J. MITCHELL, official in the ESS Foreign Trade and Commerce Division under instructions to conduct the investigation and to prepare a detailed report on all copper exported from Japan during 1949 in order to ascertain the present status of the investigation in question.

Colonel Mitchell stated that his report had been "practically completed" but that it was being held no owing to an unexpected development. He said that Mr. Bruce AITCHISON, American lawyer in Tokyo who acts as representative for the Felix Kramarsky Corporation, New York City, the firm mentioned in the despatch under reference as having shipped 5,010 metric tons of Japanese copper to "Antwerp or Rotterdam for final consumption in France, Belgium or Netherlands," had, within the past week, flown to New York in order to confer with the Felix Kramarsky Corporation. Colonel Mitchell said that Mr. Aitchison, upon his return, had called on him on September 29 and 30; that Mr. Aitchison apparently was gravely concerned over the possible transshipments of Japanese copper to satellite countries; that he had stated in positive terms that he had received assurances from the Felix Kramarsky Corporation that the latter had no knowledge of any such transshipments, and that he (Mr. Aitchison) on September 30 had presented a letter elaborating upon the foregoing statements.

Colonel Mitchell

Colonel Mitchell, who was appointed a few days ago to be chief of a special SCAP trade mission to Korea and who plans to leave Tokyo within four or five days, said he wished to have further conversations with Mr. Aitchison before completing his report. For the Department's information, it may be noted that the shipments made by Mr. Aitchison's clients, namely the Felix Kramarsky Corporation, are under special investigation, together with those made by the S. J. Barnett Co., Ltd.

Some remarks regarding Mr. Aitchison are contained in item (3) of this Mission's A-249, September 29, 1949. Mr. Aitchison is an influential member of the American Chamber of Commerce in Japan, in which he has been chairman of a special committee on arbitration; the successful work of this committee is at least entirely to Mr. Aitchison's efforts, will presumably seek results in the establishment of a Japan-United States Arbitration Committee. A despatch on which development is now under preparation by this Mission. Mr. Aitchison was a candidate for president of the Chamber, but was defeated at the annual general meeting held on September 13, 1949. However, he was subsequently appointed chairman of a special committee on taxation, and has given every indication of being willing to devote a great deal of attention to Japan's taxation problems, particularly as related to foreigners, which have come to increased public attention following release a few weeks ago of the Report on Japanese taxation by the Shoup Mission.

In his conversation on September 30 with Colonel Mitchell, the Economic Counselor expressed the opinion that, in his opinion, it was inconceivable that Mr. Aitchison would knowingly have acted as the representative of the Felix Kramarsky Corporation if he had any question whatever as to eventual disposition of the copper purchased in Japan ostensibly for use in France, Belgium and the Netherlands. Colonel Mitchell replied to the effect that he would have to withhold comment until he had completed his investigation.

Colonel Mitchell concluded his remarks by stating that he had again expressed his viewpoint, presumably to Mr. Frank E. PICKELLE, Chief, ESS Foreign Trade and Commerce Division, that, upon completion of the investigation, the report resulting therefrom should be referred to this Mission with the request to refer it to the Department for transmittal to the Embassies at London, Brussels and The Hague. The Economic Counselor received the impression that Colonel Mitchell may have encountered a difference in viewpoint in this regard.

## MEMORANDUM OF CONVERSATION

**PARTICIPANTS:**
Mr. Hans A. RIESS, representative of Philipp Bros., Inc., New York City.
Mr. Ernest J. SIMON, of same firm.
Mr. Carl H. BOEHRINGER, Economic Counselor, USPOLAD, Tokyo.

**SUBJECT:**
Information Regarding Companies Which Bought Japanese Copper for Shipment to England.

**DATE:**
November 3, 1949.

**PLACE:**
Office of the United States Political Adviser, GHQ, SCAP.

Mr. Riess called by appointment made on November 1, 1949, for the purpose of introducing Mr. Simon, who, Mr. Riess stated, wished to pass on certain information regarding several of the companies which bought Japanese copper several months ago for shipment to Europe.

Mr. Simon stated that until September 1949, when he had joined the firm of Philipp Bros., Inc., he had represented the Western Steel and Metals Corporation, San Francisco, and also the Hugo Neu Corporation, New York City. He said that he had been hired by Mr. Neu in New York City for the express purpose of representing Mr. Neu and a Mr. Popper, head of Western Steel and Metals Corporation, for the purchase in Japan of steel scrap; Mr. Simon added that, at that time he had not known much of Mr. Neu and had never met Mr. Popper. He said further that Mr. Neu had visited Japan in May and June, 1949, for the purpose of developing business, but that full details of Mr. Neu's operations were not known to him. Mr. Simon expressed the view that Messrs. Neu and Popper operated jointly in regard to the steel scrap business but confessed that he did not have the facts regarding any other working agreements which they might have had.

Mr. Simon went on to say that Mr. Neu, on the occasion of his visit to Japan, had been "very anxious" to buy Japanese copper.

(In response to my question, Mr. Simon stated that he considered Mr. Neu to be an "extremely shrewd business man;" Mr. Simon declined to voice an opinion as to whether Mr. Neu was an American citizen. Mr. Riess interjected a statement to the effect that Mr. Neu was regarded in American metal trading circles as a "very shrewd and speculative operator".)

Mr. Simon continued by stating that Mr. Neu would "most likely not violate the law in an obvious manner," but that the attitudes of Mr. Neu and Mr. Popper in the matter of the copper contracts had caused him to believe that Messrs. Neu and Popper had acted solely

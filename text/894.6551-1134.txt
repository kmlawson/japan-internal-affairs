MEMORANDUM.
American Consulate, Kobe, Japan,
November 17, 1933.
On November 15, 1933, I called on Mr. Akira
Yamauchi, a Director of the Kurashiki Kenshoku
Kabushiki Kaisha, in an effort to get the information
concerning staple fiber, or artificial cotton, requested
by the Department's Instruction of October 4, l933.
Mr. Yamauchi is reputed to be the leading authority
in Osaka on the subject of this new yarn, and his
company is considered the leading prospective
commercial producer of it.
Mr. Yamauchi parried or evaded each of my
questions, or answered it with an irrelevance. He
stated only that the fiber was still on an experimental
basis, and that at least a year would elapse before
it could be produced commercially. He in turn
manifested curiosity as to the reason for the
interest of American governmental agencies in the
new material.
After several minutes of conversation, Mr.
Yamauchi stated that he would furnish samples of the
yarn under discussion. Several bunches of yarn were
brought in for examination. Upon inspecting them I
found that they appeared to be nothing more than
ordinary rayon fibers, of 100, 150 and 200 denier
counts. I asked Mr. Yamauchi how the samples
differed from ordinary rayon fibers, whereupon he
admitted that the samples were nothing more than
ordinary

=== Page 10 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-
ordinary rayon yarns, and not what he had held them
out to be.
The step was either a subtle way of indicating
to me that I was wasting my time, or an effort to
dupe this Consulate into appearing ridiculous by
sending plain rayon to Washington as a sample of an
important new material.
I terminated the interview at once. It is
evident that the facts concerning the new material
are being jealously guarded, in the hope that it
will give the Japanese textile industry an advantage
over all foreign competitors. I consider that further
efforts to obtain the information desired at the
present time will be worse than useless.
Walter P. McConaughy,
American Vice Consul.

TABLES No. 1 (A to E) and CHARTS Nos. I to VI.
COTTON IMPORTS INTO JAPAN DURING 25 YEARS.
These Tables show detailed imports of actual bales and of quantities converted into
500 lbs. bales (nett, based en customary average weights. Running American bales to
Japan average about 5o0 lbs. nett, and round bales were taken as the equivalent of
half a square bale). They show not only the respective Growths, but also Japan Ports.
The heavy figures indicate the growth mestly imported during the respective season
and the port which did the largest turnover. Corresponding percentages are also
given.
Charts No. I and II show, arranged in columns, a survey of these imports accord-
ing to growth, in actual bales and in converted bales. The constant tendeney for the
Total to inerease is clearly visible—the two extremes are enoimous. The columns also
effectively show the proportion of imports of cach of the main varieties within one
season : the preponderance of Indians over Americans in the carlier years, with a gra-
dual but constant reversal later on.
Comments as to a few of the mest salient points are :—
1920-21 : 'The sharp set-back in imports was due to the " crash " after the war-hoom,
and financial panic reigning. The crisis came in the spring of 1920: the
imports for 1919-20 were excessively large, due to the still pravailing opti-
mism until the sudden collapse.
1923-24 : The carthquake in the Kwanto district, containing numerous mills in and
around Tokyo and Yokohama, disabled a great many of them for months.
The prevailing financial distress in the affected area also influenced con-
sumption. The carthquake happening at the beginning of the season, the
season itself shows an abrupt drop of imports ; but with reconstruction pro-
gressing rapidly during the next years there was again a marked increase.
1926-27 : 'The sharp rise in imports of American cotton was due to cheap low
grades partly replacing Indian and other staples.
1927-28 : Second financial crisis culminating in the Suzuki failure and a two months'
moratorium in the spring of 1928. Imports were correspendingly affected.
1929-30 : Some markets having been glutted during previous years, there was over-
production, and yarn prices being unremunerative, a voluntary curtailment
was adopted.
1931-32 : The enormous increase in the imports of American cotton was principally
due to speculative purchases in the expectation that the Yen would go off gold.

Our figures exclude some 150,000 bales of American cotton which were tempo-
rarily landed in Japan owing to the state of war at Shanghai, early in 1932, and
re-expórted during the same season. Another 30,() bales (about) are shown and were
liquidated during the following season.
1932-35 : Large exports were maintained owing to the depreciated yen which opened
up many new markets. The "boycott" of Indian cotton during 1933-31 did
not affect the quantity imported.
1934-35 : A sudden increase in imports of Egyptian cotton, due to low prices, is
noted but does not promise to be kept up during the next seasons.
Since for the sake of comparison a standard bale is essential, further tables and
charts only consider the converted 50o lbs. quantities.
Chart No. III gives the combined Imports into Japan according to various Ports.
It clearly shows the preponderance of Kobe, but it also makes one realize the advance
the port of Osaka has made during the past five seasons. The most important mills
are located in that district. Formerly large shipments were transferred from Kobe by
lighter—the distance by sea being less than 20 miles—but the pride of the Industrial
Metropolis demanded that their own harbour be enlarged, dredged and modernized so
as to accommodate large steamers and their cargoes. U'nfortunately the result of much
work at a very high expenditure is still unsatisfactory, Osaka harbour being too exposed
to winds which at times make unloading difficult if not impossible.
The other ports fall back considerably from these two Kwansai cities, and, gene-
rally speaking, have remained steady throughout the 25 years. Yokohama had a bad
set-back in 1923-24 owing to the disastrous carthquake on the 1st of September,
which destroyed warehouses and mills at a time when the new season's arrivals should

COTTON YARN PRODUCTION IN JAPAN.
The progressive spinning of finer counts is clearly expressed in these two sheets.
For convenience's sake three main sections have been taken : coarse, medium and fine
yarns. The following facts stand out from the Table, as to Production, when compar-
ing the years 1910 and 1931 (for 1935 only the half-yearly figures could be obtained) :—
Coarse Yarns have far less than doubled, in total output. But from a pereentage
view the S7ào, of 25 years ago have now gone down to 17o, of the entire
production.
Medium Yarns have gone up 11-fold in output, and from 1o', they now stand
at 47o, of the total. While 25 vears ago the production of medium yarns was only a
good eighth of that of coarse yarns, the two figures now practically stand on a level.
Fine Yarns are seven times as high now as in 1910. The pereentage has gone up
from 2! to 6.
The entire output has about trebled within the period, and corresponds to the
increase in total imports between first and last seasons.
The extraordinary decline in the Export of Yarns points to the larger consumption
at home, with consequent increase in export of picce-goods and finished goods. In
1910 the exports of about B50,000 bales of yarn were almost & rd of the production.
In 1931 exports of only 65 000 bales—less than a fifth in quantity--formed a bare 20
of the production. And while Imports of Yarns (mostly Chinese) were negligible at
first, they now almost equal the exported quantity, so that we may say that Japan
weaves its entire yarn output. Domestic Consumption of yarn has increased from
800 000 bales to 3! million bales.
With a production inerease of about three times the original figure, and a change
to finer yarns, the Spindles at work had to be increased over four times. But the
number of Operatives cmployed has gone up only by one-half! Twenty-five years ago
cach hand averaged less than 20 spindles; now they manage over fifty. In spite of
the output per spindle naturally declining with finer yarns (from 26 bales to about 18
bales a year), each operative increased the individual output from 12 bales to exactly
twice as many.
It is by continually exchanging old machinery for more perfected new kinds that
these results could be obtained. The operatives work fewer hours now than they did a
quarter of a century ago, and average working hours combined with the figures
presented would decidedly influence the picture in the same direction.
Chart No. IX is most descriptive. For better comparison the workers employed
are represented in units of 50. This line closely follows the trend of spindle-increase
and also of total production--for many years. After 1927 there is a sudden change ;
spindles soar sky-ward, production continues its old upward-curve and the number of
workers engaged goes downward. . . With the " sealing-up " process devised to curtail
over-production many mills were placed in the enviable position of having old and
ineffective machinery sealed, and installing new spindles which could produce faster and
cheaper. The lines for production are eloquent in themselves.

THE COTTON TRADE IN JAPAN.
While imported cotton is unloaded at all principal Ports in Japan Kobe, Osaka,
Yokohama, Moji, Nagasaki and Nagoya (with its main harbour of Yokkaichi)-it may
be said that the trade itself is restricted to Osaka.
In Osaka, the business and manufacturing metropolis of Japan, a city of almost
three million inhabitants, with uncountable smoke-stacks and brisk traffic on wide streets
and numerous rivers and canals, all the cotton agents, cotton merehants and mills have
their head-offices. The larger mill-concerns may have important factories in a dozen
or more different localities : their purchases are concentrated in Osaka. The merchants
have their delivery departments at all ports, but instructions are issued from Osaka,
while Shippers' representatives have their one local Osaka office.
It will casily be seen how such concentration makes for reduction of overhead
expense. A few men in cach firm are responsible managers ; there is no overlapping
of work, there is more fficient purchasing and an casier survey of the situation.
The yarn and piece-goods trades are equally centralized at Osaka. Partly they are
in the hands of firms dealing in raw cotton themselves or through affiliated houses.
Transactions in the nature of barter are a natural consequence, advantageous to both
parties.
About 40 firms of merchants* supply some 71 mill companics with the three-and-a
quarter million bales (a 5oo lbs. of raw cotton of all descriptiens, yearly consumed on
an average during the past five seasons. They do it without a multitude of sub-agents,
salesmen and clerks in every mill district.
The trade is organized like an army. Headquarters decide, and there are few
generals drawing large emoluments. The co-operation among the staff, with the old
ideas of feudality and family-system still strong in their veins, is exemplary.
The union of identical trades, be it manufacturing or merchandising, is again
highly organized. As long ago as 1ss2 the Mills combined to form the Boseki
Rengokai (the name of which was changed in 1SSS to Dainippon Menshi Boseki Dogyo
Rengokai, or Japan Cotton Yarn Spinners' Federation) which still functions, since 1902
again as the DAINIPPON BOSEKI RENGOKAI, or Japan Cotton Spinners' Asso-
ciation. At their regular meetings they decide on questions of policy, and representing
practically all the Mills, wield an enormous power.
The Merchants and Brokers in raw cotton formed the NIPPON MENKWA
DOGYOKAI, or Japan Cotton Merchants' Union, in 1S98. This Association attends

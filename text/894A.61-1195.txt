# MEMORANDUM

## General Conditions Encountered on Official Trip to Taiwan

I hereby report that the special trip to Taiwan has been completed and material gathered for an extensive report on the importance of the agriculture of that island which will be submitted in the next fiscal year. Some material obtained is in Japanese which will take some time to translate and analyze along with our current work before the report can be completed.

It is noted that the Consul at Taihoku has submitted a despatch reciting in some detail difficulties in obtaining information and carrying out investigations incidental thereto. Few further comments in that connection therefore need be made except chiefly with regard to the emphasis.

The trip was of value in bringing together a considerable volume of information on the agriculture of Taiwan not hitherto available to our Government despite some important deficiencies in the data obtained.

Some valuable contacts were made and it is believed a basis established for increased future exchange of agricultural and allied economic information. It seems that hitherto the Consul has had little information to give such contacts in exchange for information he was requesting. It is of course true that many of the contacts or potential contacts are now getting considerable information from the Department of Agriculture in Washington but it is much better if the Consul can have publications to furnish direct to such contacts. I am now undertaking to supply the Consul with some publications for that purpose.

Without exception the specialists with whom I had a chance to talk in Taiwan were cordial and seemed inclined to give me what information they could and I believe they may continue to be helpful. The greatest difficulty they encounter in their endeavor to be helpful is the ruling by military authorities that no figures for the last two years, and in many cases basic data for former years, shall be given out without express permission. In many cases this permission is not forthcoming.

In purposing a trip to Taiwan I was aware that many difficulties would be encountered in obtaining data but did not anticipate such an undue delay in arrangements after arrival and inability to get late experimental data due to the above mentioned regulation. While recognizing that war conditions have imposed certain restrictions on giving out of data, they are obviously carried too far in the instance of Taiwan which continues to benefit to a

degree in carrying on agricultural improvement work due to benefits of foreign information and research particularly that from America. Furthermore, every man I contacted on the staff of the University has either travelled widely to observe or collect specimens or studied abroad while but few if any specialists from other countries are encouraged to come to Taiwan for similar purposes due apparently to the objection of the military. I understand that this condition is not altogether of recent origin but has been more or less existent for several years.

I am happy to say I have not encountered to any great extent obstacles in Japan Proper similar to those met in Taiwan. I have so far been able to obtain current data desired quite freely with possible exception of rice. Only very recently is there an indication that anything like similar restrictions to those in Taiwan may be imposed upon releases of crop, agricultural and stock data generally for Japan Proper.

Future developments in the situation with respect to restrictions upon the release of agricultural information for Taiwan and the Japanese Empire as a whole may indicate the advisability of a request to Japanese authorities to grant us freer access to results of their research in agriculture in exchange for what they obtain from us in the States and in the Philippines where they have studied and travelled extensively.

My investigations in Taiwan were greatly aided by the Consul and the helpful material found in the consular files in agricultural reports of the past three years. I was very favorably impressed with the services of the interpreter Matsuo of the Consulate at Taihoku who is unusually capable in assisting the Consul in the difficult task of obtaining agricultural and other data under present conditions.

Owen L. Dawson

I have the honor to report that while especial
efforts have been made to increase agricultural
production in Taiwan this year, particularly rice
production, both the exceptionally bad typhoon weather
and lack of fertilizers and presence of insects have
militated against any increase, and it is quite
probable that 1940 will be one of the poorest years
agriculturally that Taiwan has had for some years
past. It is not likely that official statistics
for public perusal will be permitted to show the
true state of affairs, in view of the times.

Rice

There has not yet been any official announcement
of rice production for the first crop of 1940, and
as it is long overdue it is quite possible that no
announcement will be made. On August 1, 1940, it
was

was announced in the officially controlled press that Expert S. Shikayama of the Agricultural Section of the Bureau of Productive Industries had completed an inspection trip of the various rice growing districts of Taiwan to estimate rice production for the first crop of 1940. It was officially announced that while the expert had wondered if considerable damage had not been done by the typhoon of July 7, 1940 which came just at the time of harvest, he found that there had been surprisingly little damage and estimated that the first rice crop of 1940 would perhaps not be less than 4,200,000 koku (21,500,000 bushels) compared with production of 4,300,000 koku (22,000,000 bushels) (sic.) for the first rice crop of 1939. (Actual previously officially announced results for the first crop of 1939 were but 4,000,000 koku (20,600,000 bushels)).

It is recalled that Taiwan was ordered by Tokyo to produce an additional 500,000 koku of rice in the 1940 rice year (second crop of 1939 and 1st crop of 1940) over and above announced plans for production of 9,570,000 koku. Actual results for the second rice crop of 1939 were reportedly 5,060,000 koku (25,900,000 bushels), so that 5,000,000 koku of rice remained to be produced in the first rice crop of 1940 to attain the officially required 1940 rice year total production figure of 10,070,000 koku. Even if Expert Shikayama's estimate of 4,200,000 koku was realized, it would have fallen 800,000 koku short of

of the goal. The average first rice crop for the previous five years had been approximately 4,500,000 koku yearly, so that the first rice crop of 1940 was probably the poorest in many years, not excepting the first rice crop of 1939 which was poorer than average by some 500,000 koku. It is quite likely that the second crop of 1940 will also be the poorest in years.

## Sugar

In regard to the 1940-41 sugar season, preliminary estimates made during August, 1940, before the big typhoon of August 30, 1940, placed sugar production at some 18,300,000 piculs compared with an average of 16,000,000 piculs for four seasons of 1934-35, 35-36, 36-37 and 37-38, and a bumper crop of 23,600,000 piculs for 1938-39, and a reported production of 18,300,000 piculs for the 1939-40 season. Incidentally it is recalled that one year ago it was estimated that the 1939-40 season would yield 22,400,000 piculs, and it is doubtful whether actual production over the final official did reach figure of 18,300,000 piculs. After the August disaster the preliminary estimate for the coming season was revised downwards to the figure of 17,300,000 piculs. The even larger typhoon of September 29-30, 1940 will doubtless cause a further downward revision, and it would not be surprising to find final actual results by far the poorest for a number of seasons.

## Other crops

While rice and sugar are Taiwan's staple crops, the program, started in 1938, of planting various national policy crops, has received a severe set-back due to adverse weather conditions, lack of fertilizer and the prevalence of insect pests. It may safely be considered that this year's cotton crop was virtually ruined, as it has been in past years by even less adverse weather. After the August 30 typhoon, investigation of cotton planted on some 1500 acres in Taichu Province alone revealed a reported 73% loss. At the same time it was reported that 1/4 of the cassava plants had been ruined, 1/2 of the banana trees blown down, 1/5 of the sweet potatoes ruined, 1/4 of the fruit trees blown down, 15% of the sugar cane damaged, and 4% of the rice destroyed. Tobacco, jute, ramie, castor beans and other crops as well were damaged. The September 29-30 typhoon doubtless did even more damage, especially to rice and sugar cane.

Thus have natural calamities added to the agricultural problems which have increasingly beset the officials in Taiwan with the prolongation of the China Incident, and instead of increasing agricultural production to fulfill plans and expectations, it is likely that 1940 will be one of the worst Taiwan has experienced agriculturally for years past. It is probable that the rationing of

Tokyo's No. 2131 of November 14, 1936, discusses a proposal, emanating from military sources, for restriction of the powers of the Diet. This proposal was met with such pronounced opposition from the public that the Minister of War was constrained to issue a denial that the army had any intention of reducing the powers of the Diet.

Despite this denial by the War Minister, it is clear that there was a demand in certain military quarters for just such limitation on the powers of the Diet. Subsequently, a joint army-navy committee issued a statement which included a declaration that the Diet system should be renovated by a revision of the election law and the laws of the houses of the Diet. This statement appears to be a compromise admitting on one hand possible support for the younger army element which continues to demand Diet reform, and on the other hand not contradicting the War Minister's statement.

The despatch makes no prediction as to the eventual outcome of the proposal.

It should be noted that reorganization of the House of Peers, which is being formally considered at the present time, is not within the scope of the present despatch but will be reported separately.

The Embassy's earlier despatch noted the fact that no definitive statement of the proposal for limitation of Diet powers had been printed. The press reports merely attributed to the "military" a desire to prohibit members of the Diet from becoming cabinet ministers and entirely separating the political parties from participation in the administration of government. If there are men in the Army who expected that this proposal would be acceptable to the nation they must have been greatly surprised by the huge volume of adverse criticism which the proposal excited throughout the country. It is unnecessary to go into detail with regard to that opposition.

On November 6, at a meeting of the cabinet, the War Minister took occasion to deny that the Army was any intention of reducing the powers of the Diet. In response to questions, he stated that the policy of the Army of giving expression to political views only through the War Minister would go on without change, and he denied knowledge of the source of the proposal for Diet restrictions recurrently attributed in the press to the Army.

Following the War Minister's statement there were visible evidences

evidences of resentment among some of the younger and more politically minded officers of the Japanese Army. It is clear to the Embassy that the proposal for limitation of the powers of the Diet did in fact originate with army men but without the approval of the higher authorities in the War Ministry. It is also probable that the Minister of War deliberately left undefined his attitude on the subject until after sounding out the reaction of the public. The reaction of the public was so markedly opposed to the proposal that it was impossible for the War Minister to refrain from committing himself against the plan.

On November 9, the Vice Minister of War and the Vice Minister of the Navy presented a joint statement to the conference of four cabinet ministers considering the subject of administrative reform. After the conference the statement was given to the press. In the course of its recommendations the joint statement included the brief assertion that in the light of the nation's progress and of conditions prevailing in the Diet the law of the houses of the Diet and the election law should be revised for the purpose of the renovation of the diet system. This appears to be a compromise statement admitting on one hand possible support for the younger army element which remains interested in diet reform, and on the other hand not contradicting the War Minister's statement that the Army does not intend to reduce the powers of the Diet.

In reference to the Embassy's despatch No. 2131, November 14, 1936, on the subject of proposals of reform of the Japanese Diet, I have the honor to report that the most important development in the matter since that date has been friction over the request for the appearance of the Minister of War before the commission which is investigating the Diet system. The political parties desire his appearance and the military are generally opposed to it, and the issue has served to focus attention

on the cleavage between these groups.

On November 16 a representative of the commission made known to the Vice Minister of War the commission's desire that the War Minister appear before the commission and explain his ideas on Diet reform. At first it appeared that the War Minister was favorably disposed, but a meeting of other officers in the War Ministry recommended positively that the Minister and Vice Minister both refuse to attend. This attitude of the officers of the War Ministry is probably inspired by an apprehension that politicians on the commission would by crafty questioning at such an appearance, be able to embarrass the army's program of political reform, particularly since the War Minister is looked upon by many of his subordinates as too much of a moderate. The political parties, on the other hand, seeing political capital in the matter, have pressed the question.

The Prime Minister will no doubt seek a compromise settlement, probably inviting both the War Minister and members of the commission to a meeting under his own initiative. This meeting is expected by the press in early December. For the time being the question is in abeyance.

The 70th session of the Imperial Diet was convened on
December 24, last, and the opening ceremony was held in
the presence of His Majesty the Emperor in the new Diet
building on the 26th. After a recess for the year end
and the new year, the Diet resumed business on January 21,
last, but it was prorogued for the following two days
because of the troubles which occurred in the House of
Representatives on the day it reconvened. On the last
day of the prorogation, the previous Cabinet headed by
Mr. Koki Hirota resigned en bloc, and the session went
into adjournment.

The new Cabinet was organized on February 2 with General
Senjuro Hayashi as the Premier. In order to enable the
new Cabinet to work out preparations, the Diet was pro-
rogued two times, the first for ten days from February
4 to 10, and the second for another four days from
February 11 to 14. It was February 15 that the two
Houses resumed sittings. On March 25 which was the last
day of the session, the Diet was extended for six days,
or until the last day of the month. But on the last
day, the House of Representatives was dissolved suddenly
and the session of the House of Peers was suspended.

The budget bill for the 1937-8 fiscal year, which was
compiled by the previous Cabinet, was withdrawn on

The Cabinet withdrew the 51 legislative bills introduced by its predecessor altogether, and introduced 83 bills during the session of the Diet. The legislative bills passed by the two Houses and published as laws are summarized briefly.

**Enforcement of Municipal System in Saghalien (Law No. 1, 1937)**

During the past 30 years since it was placed under Japanese administration, Saghalien has witnessed a remarkable progress in all directions and towns and other autonomous bodies therein have so developed that they may be compared with municipalities in Japan Proper. In order to accelerate the trend and to promote further development, the Government decided to enforce the municipal system in the island. Provisions of the new law are not much different from those of the law enforced at home, and minor details will be regulated by an Imperial Ordinance.

**Revision of the Postal Law (Law No. 2, 1937).**

The purpose of this law is to raise postal charges, proceeds from which are to be spent for improving the postal service. Under the revised law, indemnification will be made

**Provisional Tax Increase Law (Law No. 3. 1937).**

Because of the considerably increased sum to be appropriated for national defense and other important policies, the Government found it inevitable to increase its income by raising tax scales and worked out its provisional tax increase plan in which the principles considered by the previous Cabinet were respected as much as possible. The plan was prepared along three major principles, namely; first, that a substantial increase should be made in the income; second, that tax bearing ability of different strata of the nation should be taken into account and that the major portion of the increase should be derived from direct taxes, particularly from income tax, in addition to capital interest and inheritance taxes, while avoiding the increase in indirect taxes except in some cases; and third, that economic circumstances of the nation should be considered so as not to cause radical change.

The increase will be effected for a temporary period, and permanent increase will be considered later for the whole list of the State and provincial taxes. Provincial governments are not authorized to levy surtax upon the increased portion.

Income taxes were made the principal item for the tax increase. Among various income taxes, the first class, that is, on the income of corporations, was raised by 100 per cent, or from 5 to 10 per cent; the third on the income of individuals by 50 per cent on the average, ranging from 20 to 70 per cent depending upon the amount of income; and the second on the interest on bonds, debentures, bank deposits, etc. by 50 per cent.

The business profit tax remains intact in the case of individuals, but in the case of corporations the rate was raised from 3.4 to 4 per cent. The capital interest tax was raised by 100 per cent, or from 2 to 4 per cent. The inheritance tax was revised extensively, and the rates were raised by from 20 to 100 per cent depending upon the value of property inherited, and in case immovable property accounts for more than acertain ratio, the tax may be collected within 10 years at the longest.

Among the list of mining taxes, the tax on mining output was raised by 20 per cent, or from 0.5 to 0.6 per cent, and a new levy will be imposed on the output of gold and silver ores which have so far been exempted. The temporary excess profit tax was made to remain in force for one more year, and its rates were raised from 10 to 15 per cent in the case of corporations and from 8 to 10 per cent in the case of individuals, in view of the enormous profits being earned by some industries under the present special conditions of the country.

**Tax on Corporate Capital (Law No. 4, 1937)**

The Government deems that capital payment in corporations constitutes a tax paying ability, and decided to impose a new tax, 0.1 per cent in its rate, on the total of paid-up capital and the funds in reserve. In the event of net loss for certain terms, the tax will be assessed on the balance of such a loss and the paid-up capital. This will yield ¥ 15,400,000 for the initial year.

**Special Tax on Securities in Foreign Currency (Law No. 5, 1937)**

This new tax is assessable on the bonds and debentures in foreign currency held within the country by those who reside within the country. Its rates are 70 per cent of the interest income in excess of 5 per cent per annum in the case of national bonds, and the same per cent of the interest in excess of 5.5 per cent per annum in the case of other bonds and debentures. The tax is not applicable to the national bonds which bear an interest rate less than 5 per cent per annum, other bonds and debentures less than 5.5 per cent, and those which are held by those who are immune to income tax.

**Gasoline Tax (Law No. 6, 1937).**

This tax, being intended to encourage the production of artificial fuels which may be burned as substitute for gasoline, is to be levied on gasoline when it is shipped out of refining factories or bonded houses at a rate of ¥13.20 per 1,000 litres, or ¥0.05 per gallon. It is not imposed on the artificial gasoline extracted from coal, shale rock and other kindred materials.

## Tax on the Transfer of Securities (Law No. 7, 1937)

This is another new tax, with the rates ranging from 0.01 to 0.08 per cent, assessable on the transaction, exchange, donation and transfer of national bonds, provincial and municipal bonds, corporation debentures and share stocks. It is to be paid, as the general rule, by means of revenue stamps by those who acquired these securities, but, in case these are transferred by a broker, it may be paid monthly by such a broker.

## Law for Flotation of Bonds for Ordinary Account (Law No. 8, 1937)

In order to meet the deficits in the general account for the 1937-8 fiscal year and a portion of expenditure carried forward to the 1938-9 fiscal year, the law authorizes the Treasury to float bonds, or make borrowings, to a total of $487,500,000 during the 1937-8 and 1938-9 fiscal years, in addition to the bonds to be issued under other laws.

## Law for Transferring Funds from Special Accounts to General Account (Law No. 9, 1937)

To provide funds for the expenditure in the general account, the Treasury is authorized, for some time in the future and in view of the positions of income and expenditure in each year, to transfer surplus funds into the ordinary account from special accounts of the Imperial Communications and Railways, the Kwantung Government, The Governments-General of Formosa and Korea and the Governments of Saghalien and South Seas.

## Law for Transferring Funds from Deposit Bureau to General Account (Law No. 10, 1937)

The reduction in the interest rate on postal savings by 0.24 per cent per annum, which was enforced April 1, will yield a net surplus income of about ¥8,000,000 annually in the special account of the Deposit Bureau of the Finance Ministry. The surplus thus available should be spent for social welfare facilities because the principal yielding such a surplus will have been saved by the people at large, and the law authorizes the Government to transfer the surplus, not exceeding ¥6,000,000 annually from the 1937-8 fiscal year, besides the other transfers as provided under other laws, into the general account to be spent for the purpose stated above.

## Revision of Law No. 1, 1932 (Law No. 11, 1937)

This revises the maximum annual limit for issuing bonds for Manchurian operations and increased the limit by a further ¥265,900,000 per annum.

Revision of Special Account for Cultural Enterprises in China.
(Law No. 12, 1937)

It was provided in the original law, Law No. 36 of 1923, that the funds in the special account for cultural enterprises in China should be held in the form of national bonds or to be deposited with the Deposit Bureau of the Finance Ministry, but this revised law widens the scope of the utilization of the funds so that they may be invested in other mediums which are sound and profitable, on the recommendations of the Investigation Commission for Cultural Enterprises in China, and in accordance with the Imperial Ordinance.

Law Concerning Special Accounts of Tokyo Imperial University
(Law No. 13, 1937)

The institute for training agricultural teachers, attached to the college of agriculture of Tokyo Imperial University, will be founded as an independent college to be named as the Tokyo College of Agricultural Education during the present fiscal year. ## Revision of Relief and Protection Law (Law No. 18, 1937)

The number of sufferers seeking the Government aid under the Relief and Protection Law is increasing and the State grant per capita is decreasing. Hence the Government found it necessary to formulate definite rates for its aids and thereby employ the original law more effectively. Revising the conventional rate which was stipulated "not exceeding one half the expenses to be given", the revised law provides that the Government shall pay one half the amount to be granted to the sufferers from the Hokkaido and Prefectural Governments, Municipalities and private welfare facilities, and seven-twelfths of the amount given by towns and villages. The date of enforcement will be promulgated by an Imperial Ordinance.

## Mothers and Children Protection Law (Law No. 19, 1937)

This is an important legislation from the standpoint of social well-being. It consists of 15 articles providing those who are eligible to the protection, the organ to determine the eligibility, the kinds and methods of support, the facilities for protecting mothers and children, and the State grants for such support and facilities.

Being intended to support the mothers who are unable to support their infants, this law is applicable to the mothers who have children younger than 13 years of age, cannot support their children because of poverty, and have no husbands or have such husbands who are unable to work. The mayors or the masters of towns and villages, in cooperation with the committee taking care of the slum quarters, will determine who are eligible to the protection.

To those who are eligible, the municipalities, towns or villages will supply the materials or funds necessary for livelihood of the mothers and children and the children educated in school, rent or give the tools or other utensils necessary for the mothers to continue their household industry, and extend medical aid or expenses when they are ill. The State shall bear one half of such expenses in the case of the Hokkaido, prefectures and municipalities, and seven-twelfths in the case of towns and villages. All the expenses for the protection of mothers and children will total ¥4,730,000 per annum, of which ¥2,590,000 will be defrayed from the Treasury. The law will be enforced from January 1, 1938.

## Revision of Soldiers Relief and Protection Law (Law No. 20, 1937)

The Soldiers Relief and Protection Law, which was enforced January 1, 1932, was made applicable to a wider scope by this law, and the State support will be extended to the four cases which were not covered by the original law, including: first,

Revision of the Law for Protection of the Hokkaido Aborigines.
(Law No. 21, 1937)

This law extensively revised the law for the protection of the Ainu in the Hokkaido which was promulgated 30 years ago, and affords better and positive protection for their livelihood. This law will be enforced by an Imperial Ordinance to be promulgated later.

Revision of Export Compensation Law (Law No. 22, 1937)

The original law was revised so that it may operate more positively and encourage export trade more effectively than ever, and the maximum limit of the Government compensation was raised by about 10 per cent and the scope of compensation was greatly expanded. This law will be enforced by an Imperial Ordinance to be promulgated later.

Fishing Vessel Insurance Law (Law No. 23, 1937)
Special Account Law for Fishing Vessel Insurance (Law No. 24, 1937)

These laws enacted in order to make compensation for the damages inflicted upon fishing vessels and utensils, to facilitate the financing to fishing industry, to improve fishing business and thereby to secure the life of fishermen. Under the laws, fishing insurance associations will be founded in all centers and will be authorized to insure their properties on mutual account basis, and the Government will supervise and re-insure and the date of enforcement will be named by an Imperial Ordinance to be promulgated later.

Forest Fire National Insurance Law (Law No. 25, 1937)
Special Account Law for Forest Insurance (Law No. 26, 1937)

Forest fires cause a tremendous amount of damages every year, particularly affecting young trees. In order to compensate for the damages, facilitate reforestation and preserve forest resources, the Government enacted the two laws by which forests with trees 20 years or younger will be insured on the special national account to be inaugurated by the latter law. The amount of insurance and other details will be promulgated by an Imperial Ordinance.

Revision of Subsidy Law to Private Railways (Law No. 27, 1937)

This legislation gives more subsidiary aid to private railways, and the Government will grant subsidy of the amount equal to the balance of 4 per cent of building cost and the annual profit earned by any private railways.

Revision of the Board of Audit (Law No. 28, 1937)

The Board of Audit is to be added, by this law, by a new bureau to the existing three bureaus, because of the increasing pressure on its business.

Law Concerning Funds for Kobe University of Commerce (Law No. 30, 1937)

Out of the funds for Government universities, a total of ¥1,327,453 will be transferred into the General Account during the 1937-8 and 1938-9 fiscal years, and such a total will be defrayed to make good for the deficits caused by the removal and reconstruction of the buildings of the Kobe University of Commerce.

Revision of Tax Law No. 21 of 1907 (Law No. 31, 1937)

This law enforces the inheritance, capital interest and corporate capital taxes and the special tax on securities in foreign currencies in Saghalien which has so far been exempted from these levies.

Alcohol Monopoly Law (Law No. 32, 1937)

This law authorizes the Government to monopolize the manufacture and distribution of the alcohol to be mixed into gasoline and also industrial alcohol. Under the law, the term of alcohol implies that which contains 90 per cent or more of alcohol, and the purity shall be determined by the quantity of alcohol, in per cent, whose specific gravity is 0.7947 at 15 deg. C. The law provides for the Government license to be granted to private producers as existed when the law is enforced, the restriction on the imports of alcohol, the Government supervision over the private producers, the purchase by the Monopoly Bureau of the output of private firms and other details. The law was enforced from April 1.

Abolition of Bank of Japan's Advisory Council (Law No. 33, 1937)
Revision of Law Governing Bank of Japan (Law No. 34, 1937)

This law creates seven council directors in the Bank of Japan, who will be selected by the general meeting of the shareholders from among the men of great experience and knowledge in financing and industrial circles and will then be appointed by the Minister of Finance. As a result, the board of council of the bank was abolished.

Revision of Law No. 12 of 1932 Concerning Mint Funds (Law No. 35, 1937)

According to the law No. 12 of 1932, the Government, out of the silver bullion holdings of the Imperial Mint, earmarked 50,000 kwan (one kwan is about 3.75 kg.) and the amount corresponding to the value of such quantity was loaned to the Japanese residents in China in aid of their

## Law for Issuing Bonds to Raise Funds for Grants to Retiring Officials in Manchurian Service (Law No. 36, 1937)

National bonds not exceeding ¥4,000,000 may be issued in order to raise funds to be granted as special gifts or allowances to the Japanese officials who are to be dismissed as the result of the relinquishment of extraterritoriality in Manchoukuo and the concession of the administrative authority in the South Manchuria Railway zone to the Manchoukuo Government.

## Law for Purchase of Private Railway Lines (Law No. 37, 1937)

The Government may purchase the railway facilities and business of three private railway companies during the 1937-8 fiscal year, and may issue national bonds to finance the purchase.

## Revision of Gold Purchase Law (Law No. 38, 1937)

With the increase in the quantity of gold purchase by the Bank of Japan, the debt obligation to be borne by the Government to the bank was increased from ¥200,000,000 to ¥400,000,000 by this law. The revision provides further that the Government may order the bank to dispose of the gold thus purchased.

## Alcohol and Gasoline Blending Law (Law No. 39, 1937)

With a view to saving gasoline consumption and attaining self-sufficiency as to liquid fuels, the law provides that "those who produce or import gasoline shall mix alcohol into gasoline in case the gasoline is shipped out of, consumed in, or delivered to other parties at, their respective factories or reservoirs," and that "the producers and importers of gasoline shall work out their respective programs for blending alcohol with gasoline."

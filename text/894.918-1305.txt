Secretary of State,
Washington.

21, January 15, 8 a. m.

Whereas the Japanese system of press censorship was formerly centered on drastic control of the domestic press and liberal with regard to despatches to foreign newspapers, there has now occurred a marked change in this respect. The local press is increasingly unrestrained while the despatches of foreign correspondents are drastically cut. Fleisher's daily telephoned despatches to the New York HERALD TRIBUNE are now communicated first to the censor who refuses to allow the transmission of many items and comments appearing in the local press. We assume that the same drastic curtailment applies to the despatches of other correspondents which are sent by cable, although they themselves may not be aware of it.

The Department will therefore probably find in future that the Tokyo despatches published in the American press fail to give an adequate picture of the situation and of current developments.

GREW

FROM Tokyo
Dated September 12, 1940
Rec'd 8:40 a. m.

Secretary of State,

824, September 12, 4 p. m.
CONFIDENTIAL.

One. Censorship in Japan has now become so strict that not only is the local press forbidden to touch upon a great variety of current subjects but the foreign correspondents are able to get only a small fraction of their news despatches through to their home papers. Many of the foreign correspondents are therefore hoping to be transferred to other posts. Morin of the Associated Press and Thompson of the United Press have asked for leave of absence in early October and I understand that they hope not to return. Byas states that he will not be here next winter.

Two. The JAPAN TIMES is bringing further pressure to bear on Mr. Fleisher, Sr., to sell the JAPAN ADVERTISER. Toshio, editor of the TIMES, has indicates that unless a sale is arranged the ADVERTISER will find increasing difficulties

=== Page 8 === (confidence 0.99)
hsm -2- No. 824, September 12, 4 p. m., from Tokyo

difficulties in publication. Fleisher has agreed in principle to an offer of $100,000 but he asks a further sum in yen to take care of bonuses for old Japanese employees and for passages home for foreign employees. This latter request has not been agreed to by the TIMES.

Three. I am informed that the information board, when set up soon, is considering the following plans for consolidating the press: the ADVERTISER and TIMES to be amalgamated with two daily editions; ASAHI and NICHI NICHI to be amalgamated with two daily editions and the English edition of the latter to be suppressed; all afternoon papers to be amalgamated.

September 17, 1940.

Major George Fielding Eliot called me on the telephone from New York and said that he had something that he thought might be of interest to us. He said that the New York Herald Tribune had had a telegram from its Tokyo correspondent, Mr. Fleisher, saying that he would call them on the telephone; the telephone call came through and Mr. Fleisher said that the censorship in Japan has become so strict that he finds it impossible to send out anything except contents of official communiqués; and he asked that the Tribune's disbursing officer be asked not to forward to him any checks [for funds] after the end of this month. Major Eliot added that "we" [persons on the Herald Tribune staff], looking upon Mr. Fleisher as a man very adroit in circumventing the censors, attach significance to this message.

Tokyo's despatch no. 5044, October 7, 1940, "Transmitting Translations From Vernacular Press Illustrating Press Surveillance in Japan".

To illustrate the degree of press censorship now in effect in Japan, the despatch encloses (1) a translation of an excerpt from an article in the Miyako which comments on the disappearance during the present European war of "special privileged classes, represented particularly by the nobility, who have held political power . . . in the old order"; (2) a translation of an article from the Bulletin of the Newspaper Research Bureau, which states that the authorities are irritated with the Miyako and that suspension of publication might result.

According to the despatch, the Miyako article quoted is believed to have caused the difficulty, and statement that privileged classes, especially nobility, are disappearing, is considered a "dangerous thought" on account of the possible implication with regard to the Japanese nobility.

The Embassy states that as far as is known, no action of suspension has yet been taken.

Sir:

I have the honor to enclose translations of two recent articles appearing in the vernacular press which illustrate the degree of press censorship now in effect in Japan, especially the rigid surveillance maintained over the expression of ideas which might conceivably be interpreted as liberalistic. One article, featured in the September 21 issue of a press trade paper Shimbun Kenkyu-jo Ho (Bulletin of the Newspaper Research Bureau), announced that the Miyako, a Tokyo daily newspaper, had incurred

=== Page 16 === (confidence 1.00)
-2-

incurred the grave displeasure of the authorities because of certain comment published in the morning edition of September 15 and that as a result publication might be suspended. The second translation is taken from a column of editorial comment appearing in the Miyako on September 15 and is believed to be the suspect passage in question. As far as is known, no action of suspension has yet been taken against the Miyako.

The column, which is headed "Political Sweepings", discussed on September 15 general developments in the European War, pointing out that a complete change in European economic and political structure was being brought about by the war, that in actual fact Germany had in five months swept away the old order from the continent of Europe. The writer then states that in all countries those special privileged classes who ruled the old order, represented particularly by the nobility, are disappearing completely from the stage in the present war. This is in contrast to the World War, he continues, in which Russia alone underwent such a transformation.

Although Japan is not mentioned, it is believed that the mere statement that privileged classes, especially nobility, are disappearing is regarded as a "dangerous thought" which might suggest to the reader that the future of such classes in Japan might be insecure. According to the report in the Shimbun Kenkyu-jo Ho, the editors of the Miyako are still under the influence of the liberalism which they supported in past years and which is in direct opposition to the spirit of the present times.

Respectfully yours,

Joseph C. Grew

Translation from the Bulletin of the Newspaper Research Bureau (Shimbun Kenkyu-jo Ho).

Authorities taking increasingly grave attitude.
Miyako Shimbun in danger of Suspension.
Decision in two or three days regarded significant.

Because of a certain important article appearing in the morning edition of the Miyako of September 15, 1940, the appropriate authorities are irritated with the editorial department of the journal and this irritation seems to be growing in dimensions in spite of earnest explanations being made by the editorial staff. The authorities are especially concerned over the sources for the article and take the stand that common sense would dictate to all persons engaged in journalism that the publication of such ideas should be completely suppressed from the standpoint of national policy. It was stated that the Miyako, influenced by ideas prevalent in newspaper circles in the old era of liberalism may have been fishing for a "scoop" but in any case the action is contrary to the present emergency.

The decision of the authorities as to action against the paper is expected in a few days and significance is attached to the fear that suspension in some form may materialize.

Tokyo's 5326, January 31, 1941, subject: "Imperial Ordinance For The Control of News Publications in Japan".

Transmits a copy of an Imperial "Ordinance Restricting Publication of Newspapers, etc.", which appeared in the Official Gazette January 11, 1941.

The ordinance prohibits the publication of secrets pertaining to mobilization, the military, and military resources, and it empowers the Prime Minister to prohibit the publication of news of diplomatic matters which it is necessary to keep secret from foreign countries, and of matters which might hinder national policies. The ordinance is based upon laws already in force.

The Embassy states that a new and severe law designed to prevent espionage is pending before the Lower House. As its applicability is not limited to Japan much apprehension is felt by foreign correspondents as to its effect upon their reporting. The Embassy will report further.

Sir:

1/ I have the honor to transmit the Embassy's translation of Imperial Ordinance no. 37 entitled "Ordinance Restricting Publication of Newspapers, etc." which appeared in the Japanese OFFICIAL GAZETTE of January 11, 1941.

This ordinance prohibits the publication of national general mobilization secrets, military secrets and military resources

=== Page 22 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

resources secrets, and it empowers the Prime Minister to restrict or prohibit the publication of news of diplomatic matters, of matters which it is necessary to keep secret from foreign countries and of matters which might hinder the execution of financial, economic or other national policies. As legal bases for the present ordinance, the national general mobilization law, the Military Secrets Protection Law and the Military Resources Secrets Protection Law are cited.

The present ordinance appears to contemplate the control of publications within Japan and is based upon laws already in force. On January 30, 1941, the Government introduced a new law, entitled the "National Defense Security Act" into the Lower House of the Japanese Diet. This projected legislation is designed to prevent espionage, is very comprehensive in its application and provides extremely severe penalties for violations. As its applicability is not limited to Japan, considerable apprehension is felt by foreign correspondents in Tokyo as to its effect upon their reporting activities. A more complete discussion of the law will be reserved for a later despatch.

Respectfully yours,

Joseph C. Grew.

DIVISION OF JAPANESE COMMUNIST AFFAIRS

October 17, 1944

FE
Mr. Grew:

The attached report from Mr. Service (transmitted by
Emmerson's despatch of September 30, 1944) contains a very
interesting résumé of the program of the Japanese Communist
Party as related to Mr. Service by Mr. Susumu Okano, pre-
sumably at Yenan.

The political program outlined by Okano, at least in
the first two stages, is remarkably moderate for a Communist
program, and, in fact, parallels fairly closely the general
thought in the United States on the subject. The program,
however, apparently contemplates the retention by Japan of
an army and a navy, which are not included in American
programs for post-war Japan. Okano also would abolish the
House of Peers, which seems unnecessary and perhaps un-
desirable, as the House of Peers in the past has often been
more liberal than the House of Representatives of the Diet.

The industrial and agrarian programs outlined by
Okano for the first two stages do not agree in many ways
with opinion in the United States. For one thing, the
programs contemplate the retention and expansion of Japan's
heavy industries, while opinion in the United States leans
toward their total abolition. Some of the agrarian reforms
suggested by Okano are undoubtedly desirable, but it is
difficult to see how collectivization and mechanization of
the farms can be carried into effect as long as Japanese
agriculture is engaged primarily in the growing of rice.

I cannot agree with Mr. Service's tentative suggestion
that we adopt toward the Japanese Communist Party an
"attitude of at least sympathetic support", as I believe
that any support whatsoever which might be given to Com-
munists at this stage or in the early stages of the recon-
struction of Japan's political structure would tend to
alienate from us many of the liberal elements of Japan with
whom we shall have to deal.

JA: Dickover: JAD

New Local System
Revision of City, Town, and Village Organization

Aim: Efficiently in Administration

Question: A revision of Municipal, Town, and Village Organization has been in effect since 1 June (1943). What is the aim of this revision?

Answer: Municipal, Town, and Village Organization being the most fundamental legislation for the self-governing organization of the nation, differs from legislation relating to general mobilization, etc. Therefore, it is not so frequently revised. Today, however, with the outbreak of the GEA War from the China Incident, the functions of municipalities, towns and villages have come to be more and more important. Whether it be air defense, increase in food production, or national savings, in none of these can we expect fine results without the efforts of local governments. In order to discharge such important local functions successfully, the city, town and village organization heretofore in effect has not only been inconvenient but also inefficient. The present revision has thus been put into effect for the purpose of enabling cities, towns, and villages to discharge their responsibilities fully and efficiently. Hence, a succinct answer to the question, "What is the aim of the present revision?" would well be, "Efficiency in city, town and village administration."

Method of Selecting City, Town and Village Leaders

Question: In the present revision, the method of selecting city, town and village leaders seems to have been the chief concern. In what way has this method been changed?

Answer: Heretofore, city, town and village leaders alike were selected by their respective assemblies, and once the man in question accepted, he immediately became leader. Under this system, however, a person of first-class character from within the community was not necessarily selected. Moreover, the leaders particularly the city mayors, always had to perform their work by guessing the intentions of the Municipal Assemblies, and if once ill-feeling arose between them and the assemblies, they were immediately in danger of losing their positions; therefore, they were very uneasy and it was difficult for them to proceed with their responsibilities. Moreover, a person of first-class character in a town or village was not altogether happy at becoming a leader, and there appeared a tendency to dislike assuming this troublesome post. Therefore, the present revision is an attempt to find the best candidates for both city mayors and town and village leaders, through the combined efforts of the cities, towns and villages, and of the state.

Let us explain the method specifically in the case of a mayor. First, the Home Minister designates a fixed period of about 30 days for the recommendation of a candidate by the Municipal Assembly. If the Home Minister thinks the candidate selected is most suitable, he requests Imperial sanction and the candidate is named Mayor. If the candidate is unsuitable, the Municipal Assembly again recommends the most suitable candidate possible. Further, if no such nomination is made before the expiration of the period fixed by the Home Minister, the Minister makes a thorough search for a candidate who can be considered most suitable. Imperial sanction is then requested and the candidate is named Mayor. In general, this is the method of selecting mayors.

Next, we shall consider Town and Village Headmen. As heretofore, these men are selected by the Town or Village Assemblies but they do not actually become Headmen until they further receive the necessary approval of the prefectural governors.

Question: Mayors, having received Imperial sanction, are named by the Home Minister, but Town and Village Headmen are approved by the prefectural governors. Why this difference?

Answer: Essentially, there is a considerable difference between the self-government of cities and that of towns and villages. In cities, administration is very complicated and extensive, while towns and villages are, after all, communities of plain and simple agricultural people. Consequently, even when it comes to the method of selecting its leaders, in the case of a city, it is necessary to take positive action, as in the case of the nation itself; but in towns and villages, on the contrary, it is possible to obtain satisfactory results by leaving matters to the opinions of the people. For that very reason, there are cases when things do not go smoothly, and hence it is well to have a plan whereby revision may be effected according to the circumstances. Furthermore, before 1926, three candidates for Mayor were selected by the Municipal Assemblies, the selection being determined by Imperial approval, while Headmen, though selected by the Town and Village Assemblies, were appointed on the approval of the prefectural governors. In the light of such historical facts, it is reasonable that there should also be a difference in method between the selection of Mayors and the selection of Headmen in the present revision.

Removal of Mayors and Headmen

Question: One of the main questions in the Diet was the question of the removal of Mayors and Headmen. Under what circumstances are they removed?

Answer: Mayors and Headmen are removed when it is recognized that they are conspicuously unsuited to remain in office, as in the following four cases:

1. Where they have incurred a punitive sentence and have lost the confidence of the people.
2. Where there is lack of harmony between Mayors and Headmen and it would be advantageous to have them retire from office.
3. Where there is lack of harmony between Mayors or Headmen and their Assemblies, and it would be advantageous to have them retire from office.
4. For reasons which are the equivalent of the preceding three cases.

However, since the right of removal is like an heirloom sword, it must not be wielded without good reason. In most cases, it is believed that good results can be obtained by practical supervision. If this is not possible, removal should be employed only as a last resort.

Question: What about the removal procedure?

Answer: Mayors are removed by the Home Minister with Imperial sanction; Headmen are removed by the prefectural governors.

Simplification of Election Procedure

Question: Efficiency and simplification are among the chief aims of the present revision. What does the revision contain along these lines?

Answer: The revision includes various examples of this sort: simplification of procedure in electing city, town and village assembly members, in lodging protests, and in judging petitions; readjustment of assembly duties; and reduction in the number of situations requiring the approval of the Home Minister or the prefectural governors.

Question: Please describe in some detail the simplification of procedure in electing city, town and village assembly members.

Answer: The main points in the revision of election procedure are: the decision to use the Register of Members of the House of Representatives in electing city, town and village assembly members; recognition of the system of electing town and village assembly members without ballot; and reduction in the number of re-electiones.

To speak first of the Register, previously, it was necessary to make separate entries, one in the Register of Members of the House of Representatives, one in the Register of City, Town and Village Assemblies; though there was little difference in their contents. As a result the labor, paper, costs, etc. amounted to a substantial burden upon the cities, towns and villages. This duplication of entries in registers has now been discontinued and it has been decided to make use of the Register of Members of the House of Representatives as the Register of City, Town and Village Assemblies. Consequently, as the Register will also be used in electing city, town and village assembly members, the same register will be used three times.

Next, the system of ballotless elections of Town Assembly members has been recognized. This system is one in which it is admitted that in cases where the number of candidates is either the same as or less than the number of vacancies, balloting is useless, and candidates are automatically made assembly members. Heretofore, this system existed only in City Assembly elections, but now it has also been accepted for Town and Village Assembly elections. For this reason, while heretofore it was permissible for candidates not to file notice of their candidacy, hereafter those who stand for election to Town and Village Assemblies must file such notices.

Next, is the reduction in the number of re-electiones; that is, heretofore, even if there was a deficiency of only one member-elect, it was necessary to hold a second election, as distinct from a by-election, within three months. Now, however, this takes place, as a rule, only when more than one-sixth of the number of municipal or Town Assembly members are lacking.

Lodging Protests and Judging Petitions

Question: What about the simplification of lodging protests and judging petitions?

Answer: Heretofore, when there was dissatisfaction, for example, with the levying of taxes by a Mayor, a protest was first lodged with the Mayor, who, within a fixed period, laid the protest before

-3-

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
the City assembly. The City assembly made a decision, but if there was dissatisfaction with its decision, the Prefectural Assembly was petitioned; and, if there was dissatisfaction with its ruling, the matter was further appealed to the Administrative Court. However, since both the settling of protests and the judging of petitions have become largely specialized, Mayors and Town and Village Heads, Prefectural Governors, and their assistants, who have simple duties, are better suited to handle them. Moreover, laying them before Municipal, Town, or Prefectural Assemblies sometimes entails errors in justice, delays in rendering judgments, and other disadvantages. By the present revision, the Mayor or Headman settles protests by himself, without referring them to the Municipal, Town, or Prefectural Assemblies and petitions are lodged with the Prefectural Governor, who judges them himself.

Question: That is clear. But a readjustment is also said to have been made in the duties of Municipal and Town Assemblies. What about this?

Answer: In this connection, there are various points, but we shall discuss only the principal matters. First, heretofore, all municipal and town affairs, whether great or small had to be settled in the municipal or town assemblies. However, this system is extremely inefficient today, and now it is better for the assemblies to decide only important matters which have been put in writing, while other general matters are better handled by the Mayor or Headman, completely on his own responsibility. Furthermore, it was hitherto recognized that the assembly might further increase or reduce the budget submitted to it by the Mayor or Headman within certain limits, but by the present revision, this is no longer recognized, in the light of abuses. Formerly, also, assemblies had the power of investigating municipal or town expenditures and revenues, but this too has been abolished, its necessity being no longer recognized. In connection with municipal assemblies, in addition to this decrease in duties, the well-named "Perpetual Municipal Assemblies", that is, those which are in session all year without adjournment, have also become no longer necessary and have been discontinued. A system of two assemblies has been recognized, the Regular Assembly which decides the following year's budget, and the Special Assembly, held when there is urgent business. The Regular Session lasts 20 days (30 days in the 5 great cities); the Special Session, 5 days.

## Matters Decided by the Assemblies

Question: City, Town and Village Assemblies, as stated, decide only specially written up matters. What sort of matters are these?

Answer: Matters pertaining, for instance, to ordinances, budgets, the settlement of accounts, rents, charges, entrance fees, municipal taxes, assessments, labor services, stocks on hand, basic assets, money and grain reserves, obligations, waivers of rights, and other regulations in connection with financial affairs.

Question: What about the reduction in approvals by the Home Minister and the prefectural governors?

Answer: Heretofore, matters pertaining to aqueducts, sewers, electricity, gas, railways, street-car lines, automobiles, and rents of central wholesale markets required the special approval of the Home Minister and the Minister of Finance; matters pertaining to other rents and to continuing expenditures provided for long-term projects extending over more than 2 years also

required, in principle, the approval of the prefectural governors. Now, however, this has been abandoned and no such approval is necessary at all. Moreover, local loans made to meet either obligations in connection with national projects or local projects subsidized from the national treasury, and local loans incurred to meet expenditures for increased or improved building of national schools formerly required, in principle, the approval of the Home Minister and the Minister of Finance. These, too, may hereafter be approved merely by the prefectural governors.

## Limitation of the Number of Assembly Members

**Question:** All this has been done with the aim of simplification, has it not? But the present revision also reduces in certain cases the number of assembly members. Are there really cities now which have decreased in size?

**Answer:** Heretofore, the plan has been that with the increase in population of a city, the number of Municipal Assembly members is also somewhat decreased. The minimum is kept at 30 members, while the maximum is represented by the present representation in Tokyo of 112 and in Osaka of 108. When the number of assembly members increases in this way, there easily arises an assembly hall psychology characteristic of a "Chairman Number So and So" assembly. It is difficult to engage in serious debate, efficiency of discussion is lowered, and there is even a tendency for the Assembly members' sense of responsibility to weaken. Now, therefore, the previous unlimited increase in the number of Assembly members has been checked, and a limit of 30 members has been set for municipal assemblies, with 100 members in the Tokyo Metropolis Assembly and a maximum of 90 members in the prefectural Assemblies. A limit has been set on the increase in the number of members. The five large cities which will be affected by this, aside from Tokyo, are: Osaka (a reduction of 28 members), Nagoya, (a reduction of 8 members) and Kyoto, Kobe, and Yokohama (each showing a reduction of 4 members).

**Question:** What, then, will be the present status of the members?

**Answer:** If the number of the present members is, let us say, more than 80, it may remain at that figure during the term of incumbency.

## The Directive Powers of Mayors and Headmen

**Question:** In the present revision, there is what is called, the synthesized planning of all types of policies. What is this?

**Answer:** This seems very complicated, but if we look back upon our daily living, it becomes clear immediately. Recently, whether it be food, savings, or the problems of labor, everything has been based upon a fixed plan which is centrally conceived, but the execution of which is transferred to the cities, towns, and villages. However, it is often the case that A's plan and B's plan are in disagreement. Thus, if we do not set up in the respective cities, towns and villages a basic plan on which the cities, towns and villages themselves truly and thoroughly agree, we cannot achieve real practical results. The planning and execution of a basic plan in this manner represents, in a sense, "The Planned Synthesis of All Types of Policies". However, if it is asked, "To whom is this work assigned?" the answer is not the agricultural associations, not the defense corps, but it must be, after all, the cities, towns, and villages, which have general powers. With this thought in mind, it has been recognized in the

- 5 -

=== Page 8 === (confidence 0.99)
present revision that the Mayor or Headman shall give necessary
direction to the organization of cities, towns and villages.
Furthermore, councillors have been set up to report their opinions
concerning the planned synthesis of all types of policies
which are carried out by the Mayors or Headmen.

**Question:** Giving Mayors and Headmen this directive power is
a splendid idea, but what happens in case their orders are
not followed?

**Answer:** If there is a local organization which does not follow
the order of the Mayor or Headman, the latter may report the
matter to the organization's supervisory authority and request
that proper measures be taken.

# Organization and Duties of Councillors

**Question:** How are the Councillors to be organized?

**Answer:** There are generally to be from 5 to 8 councillors. They
are to be selected from among local assembly members, representatives
of industrial and economic organizations and various other
bodies, men of learning and experience, such as national school
principals or authorities on agriculture, persons distinguished
in self-government, etc.

**Question:** There seems to be some duplication in the duties of
Councillors and those of the regular local assemblies and committees.
How about this?

**Answer:** Let us look first at their relationship to the regular
assembly. Briefly, the Councillors are the governing body of
the regular assembly. Consequently, matters which have been
first decided by the deliberation of the Councillors are then
submitted to the local assembly for completion and execution.
Therefore, there is not the slightest duplication. Moreover,
their relation to committees may be illustrated as follows; matters
drafted in the Economic Reconstruction Committee are submitted
to the Councillors, and on the basis of their opinion, the
committee makes the ultimate decision. Hence, there is not the
least conflict.

**Question:** It would seem that there is, more or less, a difference
between the duties of Councillors in cities and those in towns
and villages. Is that so?

**Answer:** That is so. If the city is a small one, the conditions
are the same as in towns and villages, but when it comes to a
large metropolis, the Councillors naturally become the highest
advisers to the Mayor. Therefore, it is deemed advisable that
their number be kept small. Councillors are required to be set
up in towns and villages, but are optional in cities. This is
because of the difference in conditions.

# Selection of Deputy Officials and Revenue Officers

**Question:** The method of selecting deputy officials has also been
changed, hasn't it?

**Answer:** Yes. Heretofore, the city, town and village assemblies
designated the deputy officials, in principle, upon the recommendation
of the mayor or leader, but now the mayor or leader appoints
them with the approval of the prefectural governor. The deputy-
officials are assistants of the mayor or leader and they should be
in complete agreement. Yet in spite of this, there have been
instances in the past of a lack of harmony between the two, and
it has been necessary to correct this evil.

Question: What about the method of selecting revenue officers?

Answer: This, too, was formerly the same as for the deputy officials but now these officers are appointed by the mayor or leaders with the concurrence of the assemblies. Since revenue officers hold an independent position vis-a-vis the mayors or leaders, the concurrence of the assembly is made requisite.

## Organization of Examiners and Committees

Question: What is done by the examiners who have now been organized?

Answer: They examine the administration of the city. Their principal duty is the examination of conditions in the management of municipally conducted business, income and expenditures, etc. Sometimes upon orders of the Supervisor's Office, they examine the municipal administration, and report upon the results.

Question: In what sort of cities are examiners to be set up?

Answer: Examiners are generally to be set up in cities which have a population of more than 300,000. Aside from the 5 great cities, this will include at present the 3 cities of Kawasaki, Fukuoka, and Hiroshima, as of 1 July (1943). Of course, officials are to be designated in other cities as well, to carry out the duties of examiners, and these persons must be assigned the same work as that of the actual examiners.

Question: How are examiners selected?

Answer: Examiners are appointed by the Mayor in consultation with the Municipal Assembly. The position of the examiners is not so independent of the mayor as that of the revenue officers, but to some extent they perform their duties independently. Therefore, special consultation with the assembly has been made a requisite.

Question: It seems that committee organization has also been changed.

Answer: Heretofore, Committees, rather than being specialized investigating bodies, were a means of assisting the Mayor or Headman, sharing the burden of a portion of the local duties. The aim was to accustom the local citizens to the administration of self-government. Now, however, the necessity for this being no longer recognized, committees have been made into bodies charged with special investigations in the cities, but in towns and villages, where such work could not very well be thoroughly accomplished, their position as assisting bodies to the headman has also been recognized.

Question: What is the Municipal Accounting Staff?

Answer: The Accounting Staff consists of assistants to the Revenue Officers. They are appointed by the Mayor from among the salaried officials. Their duties are to take charge, under orders of the revenue officers, of matters pertaining to receipts and expenditures, but they can also make revenue disbursements, a task allotted them by the revenue officers. Being independent, they are very useful in large cities, where there are many establishments requiring cash receipts and expenditures (such as zoos, municipal libraries, swimming pools, etc.).

# Delegation of National Government Affairs

**Question:** National Government affairs may now also be delegated to cities, town, and villages; isn't that so?

**Answer:** Yes. Heretofore, the delegation of such affairs to local governments or local officials could be done only by law or by Imperial Ordinance. This troublesome procedure was probably instituted because it was feared that, as delegated duties increased, the local share of the burden would also increase. Therefore, there being no suitable law to prevent this increase in the share of the burden, it was easy to delegate matters by ordinance. Now, in most cases where matters are delegated to local governments or officials by cabinet or ministerial order, the Prime Minister or the Department Minister concerned confers with the Home Minister. On the basis of their discussion, the Home Minister takes suitable financial measures to avoid creating a local burden. When done in this way, the delegation of matters by ordinance entails no inconvenience at all.

**Question:** What has happened to the problem of the legal recognition of neighborhood and community assemblies (chōnai-kai and buraku-kai).

**Question:** That has been an extremely troublesome problem, but the following three stipulations have finally been made:

1. If they have the permission of the Mayor or Headman, Neighborhood or Community Assemblies or joint assemblies may own property in their own name.
2. Local governments may take necessary measures in connection with the management of property and expenditures as well as changes in the area limits of Neighborhood or Community Assemblies or joint assemblies.
3. City, town and village leaders may call upon the presidents of Neighborhood or Community assemblies or joint assemblies to assist them in their duties.

These stipulations have been deemed extremely necessary for the better fostering of Neighborhood and Community Assemblies, and now they have been specially provided for by law.

**Question:** With the legalization of Neighborhood and Community Assemblies, will not the assembly presidents be flooded with orders to perform duties?

**Answer:** The fact that Mayors and Headmen may call upon the presidents of assemblies to assist them in their duties does not necessarily mean that they may thrust upon these persons any and all tasks. The intention is that they should receive assistance in duties for which help is absolutely necessary, and that Mayors and Headmen should exercise due care in employing them.

# Kokkaidō and Karafuto Cities, Towns and Villages

**Question:** What about the towns and villages of Kokkaidō?

**Answer:** In Kokkaidō there has hitherto been a distinction between first and second class towns and villages, and separate Imperial ordinances were issued for these. Now these Imperial Ordinances have been abolished, and local organizations has been made applicable to the towns and villages of Kokkaidō. That is because

=== Page 11 === (confidence 0.99)
Hokkaido' towns and villages have made in general the same progress as the prefectural towns and villages. Of course, for the time being those which have been second class towns and villages will continue to be designated as "Authorized" towns and villages. Though different in this respect more or less, from most other towns and villages, their organization is for the most part the same as that of the second class towns and villages, in existence heretofore. That is, Headmen are appointed or removed by the Governor of Hokkaido, Assistant Headmen are appointed or removed by the Chief of the Government Branch Office, and Revenue Officers are appointed by the Headman with the concurrence of the Town Assembly and the approval of the Chief of the Government Branch Office.

**Question:** As a concomitant of its incorporation into Japan proper, local organization in Karafuto also has been changed, hasn't it?

**Answer:** There have not been any special changes made in the cities, towns, and villages of Karafuto simply because it has been incorporated into Japan proper, but by the present revision of local organization, the separate laws for Karafuto municipal organization and Karafuto town and village organization have been abolished, and in their stead a general local organization system has been applied to Karafuto's cities, towns, and villages.

However, since the law governing the election of members of the House of Representatives does not operate in Karafuto, a special Register of Names will be prepared for the election of local assembly members. Moreover, since Karafuto is not a self-governing unit like the prefectures and since it has a special tax system, it differs more or less from the cities, towns, and villages of Hokkaido and the prefectures.

The revision of local organization in Karafuto is in general the same as that which we have seen in the case of Hokkaido.

**Prefectural Organization and the Hokkaido Assembly Laws**

**Question:** Along with the revision of local organization, there has also been effected a revision of Prefectural Organization and the Hokkaido Assembly Laws. What is the aim of this revision?

**Answer:** The Prefectural Organization and Hokkaido Assembly Laws are rather old, the former having been promulgated in 1890, the latter in 1901. These laws have now been revised not only because the present prefectural organization is by no means efficient enough for the prosecution of the present great war, but also because we know from past experience that they contain stipulations which are inconvenient and disadvantageous. Therefore, they have been revised to keep pace with the revision of municipal, town, and village organization.

**Question:** It is said that under the present revision, prefectures can be ordered to perform national tasks.

**Answer:** Prefectures, like cities, towns, and villages, operate buses (Hagano Prefecture) and collect gravel (Saitama Prefecture), but besides these, which are merely prefectural tasks, they also orbit schools, libraries, and agricultural experimental stations, and perform national tasks at the request of the state. Heretofore, the Prefectures could not very well be made to perform such national tasks without a law or Imperial Ordinance. Under the present revision, they can be made to perform national tasks by cabinet or ministerial Order, without

- 9 -

=== Page 12 === (confidence 0.99)
such coremonious procedure. Now, on the other hand, prefectural
expenditures necessitated by these tasks are the special concern
of the state.

Question: What is the situation now with regard to the number of
prefectural assembly members?

Answer: In keeping with the designation of 80 members for Municipal
Assembly and 100 members for the Tokyo Metropolitan
Assembly, the prefectural assembly has been set at 90 members, the
mean number between these two.

Question: The County (gun) Election Districts for Prefectural
Assembly members, it is said, have now been put under the jurisdiction
of the Regional office Chief or Branch Office Chief.
Is that so?

Answer: At present, counties are the election districts for
Prefectural Assembly members. However, last year (1942) there
were formed Regional Offices, which took over the duties of the
former County Chiefs, and in places where communication was
inconvenient, Branch Offices were also formed. Therefore, rural
districts outside the cities come under the jurisdiction of some
Regional Office or Branch Office Chief. Since both Regional
and Branch Offices are generally located in each County, there
is no great difference between the former and present situation.
Hence, just as the areas formerly under the jurisdiction of the
County Chiefs have been made election districts, the election
districts have been put under the jurisdiction of the Regional
or Branch Office Chiefs. As a result, the 819 Election Districts
throughout the country have been reduced by 84 and are now
735 in number.

Question: It is said that the election procedure for Prefectural
Assembly members has also been simplified. Is this true?

Answer: As in the case of city, town and village assembly elections,
by-elections and re-elections have been largely simplified.

Question: It is said that the right of Prefectural Assemblies
to increase or reduce the budget is no longer recognized. How
about this?

Answer: The budget is drawn up by the Prefectural Governor,
who has it introduced in and passed by the Prefectural Assembly.
At present, however, the Assembly may pass the budget with a
cut, of course, or even with an increase. Passing the budget with
an increase or cut in this way is called an Increasing or Decreasing
Amendment. However, an Increasing Amendment, as distinct
from a Decreasing Amendment, is apt to be accompanied by
abuses. The revision has been made in recognition of this fact.

Question: The right of Prefectural Councils to make substitute
decisions has been extended, while its right to make personal
revenue investigations has been abolished. How about this?

Answer: Yes. Let us first discuss their right to make substitute
decisions. The Prefectural Council acts as the substitute
for the Prefectural Assembly when the latter is not in
session, and its principal duty is to perform the Assembly's
task by substitution. At present, the Council handles simple
matters, substituting for the Assembly while it is not in
session. This is usually called the Prefectural Council's
Right of Substitute Decision. This right has now been extended
and the Council may now decide by substitution, in the absence
of the Assembly, all matters other than these important measures
in which it is specially stipulated that they must be decided by the
Assembly itself. This is calculated to expedite business.

-10-

=== Page 13 === (confidence 0.99)
Again, at present, the Prefectural Council elects Committees
and investigates the revenue of prefectural establishments one
by one. But both the sphere and the amount of this task have
now become vast, and investigation by amateurs is not very
effective. Therefore, this has been discontinued, and there
has been put into force a more specialized system of inspection
dependent upon the Administration Investigation System or
inspection by the Home Ministry Auditors.

Question: What other revisions have there been?

Answer: There have also been changes made in the procedure
of lodging protests and judging petitions, as well as in

Question: What is the nature of the revision of the Hokkaido
Assembly Law?

Answer: The regulations revising prefectural organization apply
in many ways also to the Hokkaido Assembly Law, as well as to
the Hokkaido Local Expenditure Law, upon which the organization
of Hokkaido is based. Therefore, it may be assumed that almost
all the points in the revision of prefectural organization apply
equally to the revision of Hokkaido

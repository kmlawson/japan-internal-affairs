74, September 15, 10 p.m. (SECTION ONE)

In compliance with the Department's telegram 37, September 5, 6 p.m., I beg to submit the following observations regarding Japanese activities in Afghanistan.

One. Treaty of Friendship was signed in Geneva (?) 1931 and first Japanese minister appointed 1933. As stated in paragraph eight of my telegram 59, August 27, 11 p.m., Afghanistan was chiefly interested in relations with Japan in order to play her off against the Soviets. Kabul was impressed with Japan's successes in 1931 in defying Great Britain, the United States and the League of Nations (?) so that in 1933 (?) King Nadir Shah told a prominent Indian he would not be surprised if Japan were to succeed Britain in the control of India. It is significant that when the Afghan Government in that same year ordered a new radio station to be erected at Kabul, it insisted that direct communications

communications with Tokyo must be part of the specifications. And in 1935 Afghan students were sent to Japan to finish their education.

Two. Afghanistan found this newly scholars very useful as a counterpoise in its trade negotiations with Russia which it had always handled with considerable candor although of rapprochement with Russia's potential enemies such as Japan, Germany and even (Poland?) the Afghan Government sought a form of reinsurance against the menace of Bolshevist political penetration in the guise of commercial (?). Japanese trade was therefore encouraged to such an extent that a few years before 1939 imports of Japanese (?) goods artificial silk and chinaware were said to have practically ousted all Russian and Indian goods.

(A) The Japanese have no quarrel with their Asiatic brothers and so long as they do not assist the British they will not be harmed. Pan-Asiatic doctrine as preached by Japan in India is based primarily on race prejudices while in Afghanistan it tries to represent Japan as the would be deliverer of oppressed Mohammedan countries. It appeals to all "proud Orientals" by picturing a bright future once British domination in Asia has been completely eliminated.

(B) Emphasizes irresistible tide of the new era which has been launched by Japan and which no country in Asia can escape. Therefore, all Moslems should join wholeheartedly in this new order which Japan is creating for their benefit.

(C) Poses as the champion engaged in stamping out Communism in Asia just as other Axis partners are determined to stamp out Communism in Europe.

The present regime in Afghanistan need, therefore, have no fear that the Axis would support Amanullah and his party for it could be coincided with a plot that as soon as the German armies are established on the Caspian Sea the Afghan Government will wish to cooperate with the Axis.

Six. Unfortunately the mere absurdity of such statements is not in itself sufficient to cause them to be discredited in a country like Afghanistan. However, as intimated in paragraph nine of my 59, August 27, the Afghan Government has never believed that Japan was genuinely interested in this country and there is no feeling of cultural unity with Japan. Subject: Japanese Engineer to work in the Provinces.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

In numbered paragraph 7 of my telegram No. 74 of September 15, 10 p.m. (1942) I mentioned the fact that several Japanese engineers employed in the Afghan Ministry of Public Works had been working in the interior, but had been recalled to Kabul in the spring of 1942.

I now have the honor to report that one of these engineers, Mr. Genji Kobayashi, left Kabul for Ghirishk (Province of Kandahar) on October 18, 1942, to continue work on the irrigation canal at Boghra. It is believed that he will not be back before next May.

I understand that the British Legation in Kabul does not deem his presence in the interior of sufficient importance to interpose objection. (Please see also, in this connection, numbered paragraph 8 of my above-mentioned telegram No. 74.)

Respectfully yours,

O. Van H. Engert

Subject: Three Afghan Students return from Japan.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

In confirmation of my telegram No. 82, April 15, 7 p.m., I have the honor to report that the following three Afghan students have recently returned from Japan via Siberia and Tashkent:

Abdullah Khan
Shulam Naqshband Khan
Abdul Hakim Khan

These students, with three others, had been sent to Japan by the Afghan Government in 1936 as a result of an offer of the Japanese Government to have all their expenses paid by the Cultural Relations Section of the Japanese Foreign Office.

At least one of these students, Abdullah Khan, has since his return been received by the King, when he is reported to have expressed the pro-Japanese sentiments mentioned in my telegram. He is said to have voiced similar views in conversation with other Afghans.

Abdullah Khan is the son of Mirza Mohamed Khan, who has twice been Minister in Moscow (1921 and 1926), was Under Secretary of Foreign Affairs in 1929 and Minister of Commerce (1930-37). His sister was one of the wives of the Amir Habibullah who was murdered in 1919. His reputation for honesty is not very good and he is also suspected of secretly supporting the cause of the ex-King Amanullah.

Respectfully yours,

LEGATION OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA
WASHINGTON 27, 1943
No. 166.

Subject: Comments of Afghan Student recently returned from Japan.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

With reference to my telegram No. 82, April 15, 7 p.m., and to despatch No. 153, April 16, 1943, concerning three Afghan students who recently returned from Tokyo, I have the honor to report that a reliable source has informed a member of the Legation that in a recent conversation with one of these students, Abdul Hakim Khan, the latter did not seem to be as impressed with the Japanese as Abdullah Khan. He said frankly that the mass of Japanese are being kept entirely ignorant of the progress of the war and that the "news" is concocted with utter disregard of fact, simply to meet the requirements of the populace. For example, when the Japanese radio announced the loss of a cruiser off Guadalcanal, the people became so despondent that it was necessary immediately to issue "news" of the sinking of three American cruisers. Furthermore, the majority of educated Japanese who have lived abroad or who have been active in the intellectual life of the country are said to be under no illusions as to the inevitable outcome of the war. According to this Afghan student many of these, especially professors, scientists, and writers, are being taken into custody.

Respectfully yours,

/s/ Wm H. Enger

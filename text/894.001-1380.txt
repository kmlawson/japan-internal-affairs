# STATUS OF THE JAPANESE EMPEROR
## Summary

The issue: The emperorship has been a dominant feature of the Japan which has risen rapidly and become an aggressive menace; should or should not the institution be tolerated after the defeat of Japan?

The facts: The emperorship has developed since 1868, the deliberate creation of able statesmen. Its asserted antiquity and continuity are gross exaggerations. The present emperor is a mild, earnest man inclined to peace, not capable of strong personal governing. By the constitution he is all-powerful. Through the practice of direct access and by other means the military makes use of his great authority. The psychological politico-religious power of the emperorship is tremendous. The United States has thus far left open the question of future treatment of the emperor by abstaining from implicating him in responsibility for the war.

The alternatives: The status quo would leave the emperorship vulnerable to the same forces and would probably lead again to the same result (war). Continuation of the emperorship with changes by the peace to end domination by the military would not in itself solve the problems inherent in the institution but might open the way to their solution by intelligent approach; the institution is capable of utility in holding together government and people in postwar Japan, and might be an effective instrument of later beneficial reform. The consequences of termination of the emperorship would apparently include difficulty of government and order, popular non-cooperation, undermining of morale, and active undercover movements toward renewed war and revenge, but would likewise involve such advantage as there may be in a decisive blow to Japanese nationalistic fanaticism and in the definite weakening of Japan as an organized state.

# STATUS OF THE JAPANESE EMPEROR

The problem of the overthrow of the militarists in Japan and the problem of changing the military mind of the country raise questions with regard to the Japanese emperorship and its future.

The emperorship has been a dominant and essential part of Japan in a period of rapid rise and aggression, ending in a climax of very wide aggression. The United Nations can not hereafter tolerate a government of Japan which continues to constitute the same menace. The question arises whether, in postwar Japan, the survival of the emperorship is something which the United Nations should make impossible.

# HISTORICAL

## Early

The earliest Japanese record to mention the emperor dates from the 7th century A.D. and was a product of the period immediately following the introduction into Japan of Chinese culture. Chinese Buddhist priests coming to Japan in the preceding century had found a people without written language. Two early products of Chinese influence were the devising of a more or less centralized government and of a method of writing, and the earliest known use to which writing was put was the preparation at the court of documents in the form of records. They show a transparent purpose to sustain authority and legitimacy through accounts of tenure from antiquity and of divine descent, and supernatural incidents abound.

At a very early stage in the historical period of the emperorship there developed a strong trend toward diverting actual power into hands other than the emperor's. By 769 a Buddhist priest (Dokyo) was in power; soon after, a family (Fujiwara) exercised control for a long time through a series of regencies; rival families disputed this control; in 1192 the shogunate was established, and thereafter for nearly seven hundred years the governing of Japan was carried on without reference to the emperors except that, during certain brief efforts to seize power, legitimist sanction was sought through (nominal) restoration of the, or an,

During the long shogunate era, government tended to center about military power and the emperorship so far fell into desuetude that the incumbent, living in seclusion away from the seat of government and often in poverty, came to embody little more than a religious significance, and that not an active one. It is recorded that one emperor begged in the streets, one supported himself by copying classic poetry, one lay dead a month unburied for lack of funds. There were instances of assassination of incumbents.

## Modern

The revolutionary change of government which brought the shogunate to an end, and in 1868 opened the modern era in Japan through the form of placing active control under the emperor "restored" to his authority, was the deliberate creation of political leaders who astutely understood the Japanese people. In the tottering final period of the shogunate there had already been heard a few scattered voices affirming the legitimate right of the emperor to rule and their slogans were in some degree picked up by political agitators; but the creation of the modern emperorship of Japan can be said, so far as practical developments are concerned, to have been the deliberate, whole-cloth creation of those gifted leaders who with great ability built Meiji Japan. To make the emperor chief of state under a restoration of government was rightly seen as the sure way to national unity and strength. It was, furthermore, a practical compromise solution to the most serious division which existed among the new rising groups in the country. One group, heirs to military government in feudal Japan, sought a continuing military dominance of the modern government of Japan. The other principal group saw promise of progress through representative institutions. The compromise of nominal authority in the emperor, who must in practice be controlled by someone, did not settle the issue but left both sides with hope of future influence, while at the same time setting up an institution which must strongly appeal to the emotions of the people at large and focus their activity. In that compromise was laid the basis of Japan's subsequent course: on the one hand clarity and unity in the purpose of developing strength, on the other hand confusion and dangerous deferment of decision in the matter of where control of her power should lie.

## Continuity

In Japanese eyes the imperial line has been unbroken.

Adoption and illegitimacy have figured conspicuously in the maintenance of the continuity. Even in modern Japan succession has been through blood other than the empress's: in 1912 the son of Meiji who succeeded to the throne was not a son of the empress but of one of the court ladies.

## Present Incumbent and Family

The present emperor, Hirohito, is a mild, earnest, family man. He was born in 1901 and came to the throne at the end of 1926. He has not been assertive in political decision, instead approving the recommendations of those entitled to offer them; but it has become known that his own definite preferences have been for peace if possible. He has a wide interest in knowledge but does not possess an outstanding intellect. He has accepted the passive, titular role which is expected of him under Japanese political practice.

The imperial family is large at the present time and possible heirs to the throne are numerous. The heir apparent, eldest son of the emperor, was born in 1933. A second son, born in 1935, stands second in the line of succession. In the absence of sons, grandsons are eligible to succeed; the present emperor has five daughters, the older among whom are approaching marriageable age. He also has three brothers, two of whom are married.

LEGAL

Constitutional

Under the constitution of Japan (which was promulgated February 11, 1889) the powers of the emperor are explicitly comprehensive. Article IV states (official translation): "The Emperor is the head of the Empire, combining in Himself the rights of sovereignty, and exercises them according to the provisions of the present Constitution." The severest limitation upon the scope of his powers is contained in Article V, which states: "The Emperor exercises the legislative power with the consent of the Imperial Diet." Other articles state that the emperor is sacred and inviolable; that he gives sanction to laws, and orders them to be promulgated and executed; that he convokes, opens, closes, and prorogues the diet, and dissolves the House of Representatives; that emergency ordinance power is vested in him; that he issues or causes to be issued the ordinances necessary for the carrying out of the laws or for public peace, order, or welfare, but no such ordinance shall in any way alter any of the existing laws; that he determines the organization of the different branches of the administration and the salaries of all civil and military officers, and appoints and dismisses them; that he has the supreme command of the army and navy, determines their organizations and peace standing, declares war, makes peace, concludes treaties, and declares a state

=== Page 6 === (confidence 1.00)
-5-

state of siege; that he confers titles of nobility, rank, orders, and other marks of honor; and that he orders amnesty, pardon, commutation of punishments, and rehabilitation.

When the constitution was promulgated (1889) special care was taken that the existence of a constitution should not be conceived as detracting from the emperorship. For the same reason the emperor himself was declared the sole source of the constitution, and initiative in possible future amendment was reserved to him alone. The constitution was set forth as an embodiment of already existing fundamental law. The oath taken at the time by the emperor at the sanctuary of the palace referred to the new constitution as being "an exposition of grand precepts for the conduct of the government, bequeathed by the Imperial Founder of Our House and by Our other Imperial Ancestors." Prince Ito, the actual principal author of the constitution, saw fit to assert, in his authoritative contemporary commentaries on the document: "That express provisions concerning the sovereign power are specially mentioned in the Articles of the Constitution, in no way implies that any newly settled opinion thereon is set forth by the Constitution; on the contrary, the original national polity is by no means changed by it, but is more strongly confirmed than ever."

On the other hand, the emperor accepted a solemn commitment, irrevocable in form, in the preamble to the constitution as follows: "Having, by virtue of the glories of Our Ancestors, ascended the Throne of a lineal succession unbroken for ages eternal;...We hereby promulgate...a fundamental law of State, to exhibit the principles, by which We are to be guided in Our conduct, and to point out to what Our descendants...are forever to conform." The preamble was duly signed by the emperor, sealed, and countersigned by high officers of state.

With regard to continuity, Article I of the constitution states: "The Empire of Japan shall be reigned over and governed by a line of Emperors unbroken for ages eternal." Article II states: "The Imperial Throne shall be succeeded to by Imperial male descendants, according to the provisions of the Imperial House Law."

## Extra-Constitutional

There are extra-constitutional provisions of law which vitally condition the legal status of the emperor. The constitution provides for the heading by the emperor of both civilian and military functions but does not lay down procedure for handling conflicts of jurisdiction between them. In this lack, and in view of the fact that the emperor is not a personal ruler who makes independent decisions, the right of certain officials and officers to enjoy access to the emperor assumes compelling importance. The right of direct access exists only for purposes within the responsibility of such officials or officers as enjoy the right. The highest officials of the imperial household department have the right of direct access to the emperor; they are, in fact, his closest advisers. Cabinet ministers have the right. Certain army and navy officers, including the army chief of staff and the navy chief of staff, have the right. In military affairs; therefore, advice is given direct to the emperor and he directly approves the recommendation. This direct access is jealously exercised by the army and the navy, and much is made, in developing morale among men in the army and navy, of their direct responsibility to and command by the emperor.

This is an obscure field of Japanese law, one in controversy among Japanese scholars. One of the articles of the 1889 regulations defining the organization of the cabinet specifically mentions army and navy direct access but is not its authorization. Subsequent regulations (1908) of the army general staff and of the navy general staff mention direct control by the emperor. Direct access was routine practice from much earlier, however. It appears that the incorporating in formal law of direct access by the army and navy was avoided, that from the promulgation of the constitution in 1889 it was an accepted practice, and that even today it has no source in law other than that which can be held to be implied in the articles of the constitution which state that the emperor has the supreme command of the army

## POLITICAL

The question of the status of the emperor has not in Japan been allowed, however, to rest on its legal definitions. An active and dangerous political issue, it has been pressed by a number of nationalist groups. In 1935 the leading living commentator on the constitution, Dr. Minobe, member of the house of peers and a retired professor of constitutional law, was the object of bitter and protracted attack by patriotic associations on the ground that in his books (published in 1906, 1921, and 1934) he had described the emperor as an organ of sovereignty. His attackers found this conception too institutional, not sufficiently personal. Military opposition to Dr. Minobe probably stemmed from a desire to keep imperial prerogatives beyond the possibility of questioning by the people: if the emperor were an organ of the nation, the nation might one day claim a right to determine the emperor's decisions. The attack attained such proportions that Dr. Minobe's career was ruined and the government was forced to put out an extravagant declaration on the emperor's powers.

The exercise in practice of the powers of the emperor through direct access of the military is illustrated by the occupation of Manchuria in 1931. Previous to September 18, 1931, the army chief of staff went to the emperor and obtained imperial sanction to take whatever measures might be necessary for protection of Japanese lives and property in event of Chinese aggression; the commander of the Japanese army in Manchuria was so informed; an incident at Mukden on September 18 was engineered by the Japanese military to give the appearance of Chinese aggression; and the consequent action found necessary by the military for the protection of Japanese lives and property was the occupation of Manchuria. Japanese forces occupied Manchuria under an authority granted in blank by the emperor to the army chief of staff availing himself of direct access, and there is evidence that the civilian government was uninformed of the plan.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL

The emperor as an institution in the life of the Japanese is a complex of great pervasiveness and effect. Its incidence covers a wide scope, ranging from the superstitious to the high ethical. In the schooling which is given to all Japanese for six years of their childhood, the subject given the greatest stress is called national morals, which is focused upon reverence for the emperor. He is symbol of the nation and symbol of the good. As illustrative of the tone taken in Japanese indoctrination there is quoted below the basic imperial rescript on education, which is ceremoniously revered as the fundamental pronouncement for all instruction in Japan. In translation it is as follows:

Know ye, Our subjects:

Our Imperial Ancestors have founded Our Empire on a basis broad and everlasting, and have deeply implanted virtue; Our subjects ever united in loyalty and filial piety have from generation to generation illustrated the beauty thereof. This is the glory of the fundamental character of Our Empire, and herein also lies the source of Our education. Ye, Our subjects, be filial to your parents, affectionate to your brothers and sisters; as husbands and wives be harmonious, as friends true; bear yourselves in modesty and moderation; extend your benevolence to all; pursue learning and cultivate arts, and thereby develop intellectual faculties and perfect moral powers; furthermore, advance public good and promote common interests; always respect the Constitution and observe the laws; should emergency arise, offer yourselves

yourselves courageously to the State; and thus guard and maintain the prosperity of Our Imperial Throne coeval with heaven and earth. So shall ye not only be Our good and faithful subjects but render illustrious the best traditions of your forefathers.

The Way here set forth is indeed the teaching bequeathed by Our Imperial Ancestors, to be observed alike by Their Desendants and the subjects, infallible in all ages and true in all places. Loyal obedience and devotion to the good are the admonitions which pervade the imperial build-up, but the popular response goes considerably farther. There can be little question that millions of Japanese look upon the emperor as a god and direct prayers to him in that capacity. He stands at the head of Shinto, the pervasive politico-religious worship to which all Japanese are required to adhere. In the field of practical politics, worship of the emperor is an international menace in that it lends to aggression an aspect of destiny. Military-minded leaders have been unchecked in their perversion of the institution to their own ends. Through emperor worship Japanese are filled with belief in their uniqueness and with determination to impose their institutions and rule upon alien peoples. In all the people's participation in military service the emperor is emphasized as the object and purpose of loyalty and service. As head of the national faith he is the means by which the Japanese community is unified; as heir of the unbroken line of deified imperial ancestors he is both guardian and motive force of the national destiny and of its timeless mandate to extend to every corner of the earth the imperial glory which is uniquely Japan's, incomparable and of universal validity.

## POSTWAR ALTERNATIVES

### Status Quo

The continuance after the war of the emperorship in the same form as it existed before the war would presumably lead to the same consequence, a renewal of the aggressive attitude and finally of war. The subservience of the emperorship to the military led to war, directly through control of governmental decision, indirectly through power over forming the mind of the people; unmodified continuance would likely have the same result again.

### Survival of the Emperorship

#### Modified by Termination of Dominance by Military

Even with the most effective safeguards against reassertion of dominance by the military, inherent qualities of the emperorship which would survive with its survival would include:

a. Superstitious belief deriving from the dogma of divine descent.

b. Strong nationalistic belief consequent upon the dogma of uniqueness of race and mission.

c. National strength through unity.

With a surviving emperorship in Japan there would remain serious problems for the United Nations; it would be shortsighted to expect that by returning actual governing power from the military to a civilian group

about

-11-

about the emperor an automatic solution to problems would be obtained. No ready-made group of liberals in Japan exists, competent to take over the governing of the country. If the emperorship is allowed to survive, subsequent policy would be under the necessity of seeking methods of reducing the risks which inhere in the institution. Superstition connected with it is susceptible of removal through more open educational practices, but the exaggerated nationalism of the Japanese can be expected to be more resistant. An adequate governing group for Japan can not at best be a matter of discovery; it is perforce a continuing problem susceptible only of gradual solution.

Japanese are peculiarly and sentimentally responsive to generosity and indications of respect (as illustrated by American relief at the time of the 1923 earthquake, return of Saito's ashes by the U. S. S. Astoria, and acceptance of the Panay settlement offered by Japan), and this is especially true in matters having to do with their emperor. There is no approach so completely calculated to carry Japanese approval as good treatment of the emperor.

The survival of the emperorship is a potential asset of great utility not only as a cohesive of domestic government and order but also as an instrument of future betterment and changes which the United Nations might wish to bring about. The very fact that the power to initiate amendments to the Japanese constitution is reserved to the emperor makes orderly constitutional change more feasible if the approach is through the emperor. A non-militaristic governing group would be in a better position to make reforms effective if in position to draw upon the authority of the emperor.

A principle set forth in the Atlantic Charter and confirmed by the declaration of the United Nations on January 1, 1942, is respect for the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live. There is no present indication that the Japanese, when given a choice, will choose any form other than the emperorship, whatever its constitutional limitations. Although another point stated in the Atlantic Charter and equally confirmed by the United Nations looks to the disarmament of nations which threaten, or may threaten,

-12-

threaten, aggression outside their frontiers, and this might well take priority over free choice of government; in the case of Japan there appears to be no inevitability of conflict between these two principles.

## Termination of the Emperorship

Any postwar system of government for Japan based on elimination of the emperor and termination of the institution would appear to involve the following consequences:

a. Difficulty in finding competent Japanese willing to participate in an emperorless administration.

b. Popular resentment, opposition, and non-cooperation.

c. Disintegration of basic incentive of order, law, and acceptance of authority.

d. Creation of a permanent incentive for insurgency and revenge.

e. Removal of obstacle to inculcation of more rational ideas among Japanese regarding national uniqueness and world mission.

f. Cheek to nationalistic fanaticism and the power which derives from it.

g. Weakening of Japan as an organized state (military or non-military, political or economic).

To me, it would seem that the making by this Government of this kind of a gesture at this time would be just plain "silly".

1. For the King of England to send such a message is, I believe, in line with established procedure: exchange of such messages in such connections is a common practice among ruling Houses, and, the British have done a lot of this with the Japanese ever since the consummation of the first Anglo-Japanese Alliance in 1902. But for the President of the United States to go out of his way to send such a message would be unusual.

2. It would in my opinion be easily inferable that, in taking this action now, we are "tagging after" the British.

3. I see no reason for assuming that receipt of such a message would give the Emperor a great emotional uplift; still less for assuming that it would gladden and soften the hearts of the real rulers of Japan.

4. I believe that, rather than having an effect in the direction of improving our relations with Japan, receipt by the Japanese of such a message would tend to strengthen an opinion which they entertain that we try and expect to use merely soft words as our tools for dealing with hard problems and would give encouragement to the views of that element in Japan who contend that fundamentally the attitude of the United States is an attitude of "appeasement".

5. In principle, I think we should send less rather than more of these congratulatory messages--

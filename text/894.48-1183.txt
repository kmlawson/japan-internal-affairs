ELIEF MEASURES IN NAGASAKI PREFECTURE NECESSITATED
BY DROUGHT DAMAGE TO RICE CROP.
During 1939 the total rainfall throughout the Island
of Kyushu, as in many other parts of Japan, was below that
for all but a very few of the years in which accurate re-
cords have been kept. Several cities with normally adequate
water storage facilities have been compelled to resort to
suspension of the water supply except during certain hours.
In the city of Nagasaki, water for residential consumption
was supplied for only one and one-half hours on alternate
days during a period of several weeks and the shortage has
not yet been overcome. All three of the reservoirs forming
the city water storage system are exhausted and current
supplies are being obtained from two rivers and from
artesian wells temporarily commandeered for public use.
In smaller cities and rural districts depending upon
surface water for normal needs the lack of rain has also
been acutely felt. The rice farmer, however, has suffered
most of all. Shortly after the transplanting of the shoots
in the spring the lack of rain tended to weaken the plants
and the continued drought throughout the growing season
drastically curtailed production, in some cases producing
complete crop failure. The prefectures of Nagasaki, Fukuoka
and Saga were among those most seriously affected.

Day Murseries
On account of the shortage of male labor on farms,
due to demands for manpower for military and industrial
purposes, an increased burden has fallen upon the female
members of farm households since the outbreak of hostilities
in China in l937. In order to enable mothers having small
children to work in the fields at the seasons for planting
and harvesting rice, day nurseries have been established
in farming communities in Nagasaki Prefecture under local
control but with the support of funds from both the pre-
fectural and national governments.
These day nurseries, although long talked about, first
began to operate in 1937 and their mumber has steadily in-
creased. The demand for this service is very much greater
during

=== Page 9 === (confidence 0.99)
5
during the spring planting season than in the fall, as
shown by the fact that during the Spring of 1939 there
were 230 nurseries in operation, serving 8,500 children,
while in the Fall there were only 20, serving 133 children.
It is planned to operate 266 nurseries in the Spring of
1940 and 30 in the Fall.
The day nurseries come under the general supervision
of the Department of Public welfare and are intended not
only to free the parents for agricultural activities but
also to provide a satisfactory diet and some medical care.
That this can be done only on a very small scale is evident
from the announcement that the funds now allotted for the
purpose are sufficient to grant a subsidy of only ¥50 per
nursery. Assistance by the national government and by the
prefectural government is in the ratio of two to one.
Army Furloughs
As a further measure to relieve the farm labor shortage
temporarily, soldiers in active service were granted fur-
loughs during the 1939 harvesting season to enable them to
assist their families with the work. In addition, middle
and high school students were organized into groups and
released from their studies for the same purpose.
Subsidy Purchases
It was announced in the local press on January 7 that
at a conference under the auspices of the Ministry of
Agriculture and Forestry it was decided that a subsidy of
¥793,900 would be granted for the purchase of rice straw
for cattle fodder in the ten prefectures most seriously
affected by the drought, Nagasaki and Saga Prefectures
among them. In addition, it was expected that an additional
subsidy of ¥530,000 would shortly be forthcoming. The
amounts to be allotted to the several prefectures were not
mentioned.

Compensatory Price Adjustment
At one of his first interviews with the press after
his appointment, the new Minister of Agriculture and Forestry
1s reported by the press as having said on January 16:
".....It must be made clear that Japan
is not lacking rice. The recent reported
anxiety was due to improper distribution.
In order to effect smoother distribution, it
will be necessary for the Government, I think,
to take proper steps for the protection of
rice producers. That is to say, the Govern-
ment must, at its own expense, try to supply
low priced rice to consumers without causing
loss on the part of the producers."
By prohibiting polishing of rice more than 70%, as
a compromise between maximum conservation of grain and the
preference of Japanese consumers for completely polished
rice, the government has indicated that the supply is far
from

=== Page 11 === (confidence 0.99)
from abundant. Active measures are under way at the
present time to take a census of the stocks stored not
only by dealers but by the public. It seems likely that
rice farmers may still possess considerable amounts of
grain retained for their own use, in view of the fixed
low price at which they would have to sell and the knowl-
edge that prices are irresistibly rising. It may be that,
without abandoning the principle of controlled low prices
for commodities of prime necessity, the government will
devise some means of directly subsidizing rice farmers.
There has already arisen a demand that producers who sold
their grain as a result of insistent government pressure
before the legal price was increased be reimbursed for
the amount of their paper loss.

Government Relief Well Organized
I endeavored without success to locate the ruins of
the houses where the Americans of Japanese race had been
living. I am satisfied that none of the Americans who
suffered damage are in any inmediate danger. As indicated
in the newspaper accounts, the casualties to the inhabitants
of the burnt area were remarkably few. Food, medical sup-
plies and other necessities were rushed to Shizuoka from all
directions with great speed. Prepared food was distributed
from trucks which made frequent trips during the day through-
out the burnt area for the benefit of the thousands of former
residents and relief workers who were digging in the ruins
and clearing the streets. Everyone was given shelter either
in schools, public buildings or in private houses.
No attempt was made to check on the whereabouts and
welfare of the mericans of Japanese race who were not
registered as living in the devastated area, as it was
assumed that they were safe.

Assistance of the American Red Cross to the Nationals of other

of a statement from the Red Cross Society of the country concerned
that such assistance is desired or will be acceptable. Furthermore,
it is not the general policy of the American Red Cross to extend as-

There are three principal causes for the
power shortage which has developed in Japan:
(1) The electric power industry in Japan
has been in danger for several years of becom-
ing state controlled and in April of l939 the
semi-official concern, the Japan Electric
Generation and Transmission Company, was estab-
lished to control and operate electric power
generation and transmission in Japan. The
apprehension connected with this development
had resulted in the reluctance of private com-
panies to undertake expansion of their produc-
tive facilities. In recent years, therefore,
the expansion of power facilities has not pro-
ceeded at a rate as rapid as that of general
industrial expansion.
(2) The Japanese Government has undertaken
a tremendous program of industrial expansion,
and these new and expanded industries demand
large quantities of power.

The basic reason for the shortage of coal
appears to lie in the lack of coordination be-
tween Japan's many economic plans. During 1938
and 1939 plans were piled on top of plans.
Elaborate regulations were promulgated; permits
were required both to buy coal and to sell coal.
Coordination was so poor that the Japan Elec-
trio Generation and Transmission Company was
effectively denied permits to obtain an ade-
quate supply of coal until the stormy, wintry
season had set in, at which time it became im-
possible to obtain delivery of coal already
mined. Transportation difficulties and the low
price policy enforced by the government have
also been important factors in the coal short-
age. Large-scale coal operators have been
forced by the government to adhere to official
prices whereas medium- and small-scale coal mine
operators have escaped government restrictions.
The result has been competitive bidding among
the mine owners for employees and the rapid
labor turnover.
The report contains certain information
in regard to the arbitrary reduction in power
consumption enforced by the government but
these facts have already been brought to the
attention of the Department and need not be
discussed further.

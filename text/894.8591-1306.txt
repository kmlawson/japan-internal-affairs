JAPANESE SHIPS RECENTLY AT MANZANILLO, MEXICO
September 23, 1941

The Japanese S.S. "Heiyo Maru" arrived at Manzanillo on September 16, 1941 from South America and departed on September 17, 1941 destined for Yokohama, Japan. This ship unloaded 2,652 bags of mail in Manzanillo.

On September 12, 1941, the Japanese freighter S.S. "Noto Maru" arrived at Manzanillo from Japan destined for Peru. The only passenger on board this freighter was one Captain H. Sato who disembarked at Manzanillo and proceeded to Mexico City. Colonel Katsuki Semi Sima, the Japanese Military Attache at Mexico City boarded the "Noto Maru" destined either for Eten or Callao, Peru. A Captain Yamahi, a former Japanese Naval Attache in Canada who presumably had a transit visa through the United States, also boarded the boat. This freighter unloaded in Manzanillo about 200 drums of German aniline dyes, and quantities of mail which was principally addressed to individuals in the United States.

During the time that the "Noto Maru" was at the port of Manzanillo, no one was allowed to board her and even the stevedores who unloaded the cargo were not allowed anywhere on the ship except in the forward hold. This boat departed Manzanillo September 14, 1941 and it may have been carrying Japanese war materials to Peru. Prior to its arrival, General Enriquez, head of the Mexican Immigration Department at Mexico City, sent telegrams to the Chief Inspector of Immigration at Manzanillo and instructed him that all courtesy and attention should be given to the Japanese ships, "Heiyo Maru" and "Noto Maru".

On the S.S. "Heiyo Maru" which arrived at Manzanillo on September 16, 1941 from South America and departed on September 17, 1941 for Yokohama, Japan, there were approximately 103 Japanese men, women and children who had boarded the ship at Manzanillo. It was said that the majority of these were Japanese nationals and their families from all parts of Mexico who were returning to Japan, ostensibly because they feared an incident between Japan and the United States. Others who boarded the ship and who could not be included in such a category were as follows:

Two Japanese officials and five clerks from the head office of the Nippon Yusen Kaisha Lines in New York City and three Japanese from the Japanese Legation in Mexico City, among whose baggage, four large boxes with diplomatic seals were noted.

In addition to an enormous quantity of water and oil sufficient for the direct crossing to Japan, the "Heiyo Maru" took on the following:

An undetermined amount of Bronze
Several railroad cars of Rosin and,
Approximately 250 "liter drums" of Snow White Petrolatum, all of which was consigned by A. Z. Phillips, the petrolatum having been bought in the United States by the Mexican Government owned company, Petroleos Mexicanos and resold in Mexico to Phillips. It is pointed out that 200 flasks of Mercury belonging to Phillips were for some reason not allowed to be shipped on this boat.

Manzanillo seems to be quite pro-Japanese and all courtesies are extended to Japanese entering or departing Mexico through this port, as is evident by the instructions issued by General Enriquez, mentioned elsewhere.

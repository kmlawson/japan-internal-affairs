Before proceeding to a discussion of your c se, it
will probably be well to explain some of the fundamental
features. You state in your letter:
" I understand that, by the terms of the Treaty
made between the Imperial Government of Japan
and the United States, the income derived from
rents of buildings situated on (Eitai) Perpetual
Lease Title Deed land is exempt from National,

No treaty has ever been concluded between the United
States and Japan defining the status of perpetual lease
property or the liability of lessees to taxation of any kind.
The system of perpetual leases grew from arrangements made
between the local authorities and the representatives of the
foreign nations in each port. The only reference to the per-
petual leases in any of the treaties between the United States
and Japan is that in the Treaty of 1894( the treaty arranging
for the abolition of extraterritoriality and the rendition of
the foreign settlements). In this treaty a clause provides
that:

that:
" When such incorporation( the incorpora-
tion of the foreign settlements into the
Japanese communes) takes place existing
leases in perpetuity upon which property
is now held in the said Settlements shall
be confirmed, and no conditions whatsoever
other than those contained in such exist-
ing leases shall be imposed in respect of
such property."
It will be observed that this clause does not expre ly
provide for exemption from taxation of any kind, but throws
the matter back upon the terms of the original leases. Iden-
tical clauses are contained in the treaties of other countries
concluded with Japan at the same time.
The claim for exemption from taxation, therefore, depends
upon the terms of your lease, not upon any explicit treaty
right. If you will examine your leases ( which I presume
are the standard form of Yokohama perpetual lease), you will
find there no specific reference to exemption from taxation
of any kind. The claim for exemption from taxation grew
out of the fact that the leases, in addition to a lump sum
payment which evidently is the purchase price of the land,
provide for the payment of an annual ground rent, which was
undoubtculy intended to provide for streets, drains, policing,
etc. After the rendition of the Settlements in 1899, the
Japanese Government contested the claim that the ground rent
was collected in lieu of all taxation, although it did admit,
after some controversy, that the ground rent was collected
in lieu of the land tax. The Government did not admit that
perpetual lease property was exempt from other taxes and tried
to collect the house tax. Eventually this question of the
house tax was referred to the Hague Tribunal, which rendered
a decision in favor of the lease holders. The Hague Tribunal
did not, as you seem to think, pass upon the liability of the
lease holders to payment of income taxes, either national,
prefectural or municipal. However, such exemption would seem
to be implied in the Hague Award, which reads as follows:
" The provisions of the Treaties and other
engagements mentioned in the Protocols of
Arbitration exempt not only the lands held
in virtue of the leases in perpetuity grant-
ed by the Japanese Government or in its
name, but they exempt the lands and the
buildings of every kind erected or which

imposts, taxes, charges, contributions or
conditions whatsoever other than those ex-
pressly stipulated in the leases in ques-
tion."

The Japanese Government, as a result of the Hague
Award, has relinquished its claims for taxes dircctly attached
to the land and builjings of perpetual lease property, such
as the various land taxes, house taxes, registration fees,
succession taxes, and stamp taxes. The Japanese Government has
never, however, admitted the exemption from liability to pay
income taxes of perpetual lease holders, maintaining that
such taxes pertain to the individual and not to the land.
As will be seen from the above, therefore, the claim to
exemption from payment of income taxes upon incones derived
from perpetual lease property (such as your claim) is not
based upon har and fast treaty rights, but upon ill-defined
interpretations of the meaning of vari us documents.
As your claim for exemption from payment of income
tax on that part of your i come derived from the rental of
perpetual lease property is not based upon an explicit treaty
right, the Embassy cannot intervene on your behalf on the
ground of a violation of an existing treaty. Moreover, the
United States Government has never treated the perpetual
lease problem as a public question, but has rather taken the
stand that each lease is an individual contract between the
lessee and the Japanese authorities and should be treated as
is any other private contract. In other words, there is no
general contract between the United States and Japan govern-
ing the status of perpetual leases or the liability of Ameri-
can perpetual lease holders to taxation, and therefore the
Embassy is not in a position to take any general stand vis-a-
vis the Japanese Government on the question. The representa-
tives of the American Government are, however, always will-
ing to help American perpetual lease holders with advice and
such friendly assistance as may be possible under these con-
ditions.
While the Embassy cannot, as was stated above, adopt any
general policy regarding the question of perpetual leases,
it can insist that the same treatment be accorded to American
citizens as is accorded the citizens or subjects of any other
nation.
The information available to the Embassy would indicate
that you are not a subject of discrimination , and that the
citizens and subjects of other nations are being compelled
to pay income taxes on incomes derived from perpetual lease
properties, on threat of distraint. If, however, you can
adduce any definite, legal evidence that you, as an American

The first treaties of commerce and naviga-

and Japanese jurisdiction, but to the laws and the jurisdio-

found it inexpedient to establish such special areas, known
as concessions, within the limits of the existing munici-
palities, and at what are today the two principal ports of
the Empire, Kobe and Yokohama, the concessions were laid
out at some distance from the native towns. The areas set
aside for the concessions were not at that time considered
as ever likely to be desired by the Japanese, and the in-
trinsic value of the land within such areas was virtually
negligible. The former concession, around which the modern

Government annually by the leaseholder or his "heirs,

the only charge expressly stipulated in the deeds of lease.
In view of differences in customs, manners and modes

the Japanese Government consented to the administration of
the concessions by the foreign communities. In return for
being relieved of the cost of such administration, the

drains, lighting the streets or other municipal purpose".

rentals stipulated in the deeds of lease were, at the time

CONCLUSIONS
It seems necessary, if any settlement is
to be made, to cut across the legal argu-
ments of both sides, consider the intent
of the various parties in the establishment
of the concessions and leaseholds, and give due regard to
the vested interests of the present leaseholders as well
as to the rights of the Japanese Government and munici-
palities. That there is no other likely method is
demonstrated by the fact that notwithstanding the efforts
made over a long period of time by the various interested
parties to find some adjustment which would satisfy the
legal rights of all concerned, the question is actually
farther away from being satisfactorily solved than it
ever was. It seems to me that consideration of the prob-
lem from the point of view of equity rather than from
the point of view of law offers at the present time the
only hope of dealing conclusively with the matter.
It is to be noted from the Embassy's despatch
No. 1269 that the "Japanese Government could not enter-
tain the idea of the payment of compensation for the
cancellation of the perpetual leases", and that that
Government may perhaps summarily end the controversy by
cancelling the leases and replace them with deeds in fee
simple. Whether it will do so is a matter for conjecture,
but it is probable that persistence by the foreign lease-
holders in a position which is being construed as a claim
for

=== Page 73 === (confidence 0.99)
- 16 -
for privileges growing out of the extraterritorial system
will result in increasing popular demand for the taking by
the Japanese Government of drastic action (See Annex B).
In general, the protracted argumentation has had at least
one result, and that is to dispose the Japanese Government
to become more refractory; and it may be doubted whether
it would agree today to extend the offer of settlement which
it made in 1909 (see page 11 of this memorandum).
The following facts are apparent:
1. None of the private American leaseholders
(with the possible exception of one leaseholder in
Yokohama) was in possession of leaseholds prior to
the abolition of extraterritoriality. The rentals
paid by the leaseholders, who are not called upon
for the payment of land, house, and other taxes, are
extremely low, if not negligible, in relation to the
present value of leasehold property;
2. It is open to doubt whether the leaseholders,
in paying such rentals, have fully discharged their
obligation to contribute toward the cost of main-
taining municipal services;
3. When the question of abolition of extra-
territoriality was discussed by the Japanese Govern-
ment with other foreign governments, it was the in-
tention of all governments concerned that the lease-
holds would be terminated with the abolition of
extraterritoriality or as soon thereafter as
practicable;
4. The rights which the leaseholders possess
arise out of agreements made by them with the Japa-
nese Government and are private rights.

Your Excellency,
Tokyo, March 25, 1937 (12 Showa).
I nave the honour to acknowledge the receipt of your Excellency's
note of to-day's date in which your Excellency has informed me as
follows :—
With reference to the conversations which have recently been
conducted between this Embassy and the Imperial Japanese Ministry
for Foreign Affairs with a view to liquidating once for all in a spirit
of friendship and conciliation the system of perpetual leases which
originated in former treaties between Great Britain and Japan, I have
the honour to confirm to your Excellency the following understanding
between His Majesty's Government and the Imperial Japanese
Government :
(1) That the said system of perpetual leases shall come to an end
on the first day of the fourth month of the seventeenth
year of Showa, corresponding to the 1st day of April, 1942.
when the leaseholds shall without compensation be
converted into the rights of ownership in accordance with
the provisions of Japanese laws and ordinances. Such
conversion shall be effected free of registration taxes in
respect of lands under perpetual leases and buildings
thereon.
(2) That until the thirty-first day of the third month of the
seventeenth year of Showa, corresponding to the 31st day
of March, 1942, the present position as regards tax
exemptions shall be maintained, and no further claims
shall be made by the Japanese authorities for arrears of
such disputed taxes as may still be uncollected.
I have the honour to inform your Exeelleney that I hereby
confirm the above understanding for a final settlement of this
question.
I avail, &c.

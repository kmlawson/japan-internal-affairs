AMERICAN CONSULATE,
Dairen, Manchuria, July 11, 1930.

SUBJECT: Showa Steel Works of South Manchuria Railway Company.
Division of FAR EASTERN AFFAIRS AUG 15 1930
The Honorable
THE SECRETARY OF THE WASHINGTON OFFICE
WASHINGTON

SIR:

I have the honor to refer to my despatch No. 59 of April 17, 1929, transmitting a report on the programme of the South Manchuria Railway Company for expanding its iron and steel production, and to inform the Department of the progress and present status of this scheme.

In the report above referred to, it was stated that the Railway Company had given up its plan of manufacturing finished steel at Anshan and decided instead to increase its output of pig iron and manufacture billets and sheet bars for remanufacture in Japan in a plant of its own. The annual output of the Anshan blast furnaces was to be raised (from 225,000 tons) to 400,000 tons, of which 140,000 tons would be left in pig iron form and the remaining 260,000 tons converted into billets and sheet bars for export to the proposed plant in Japan, where they would be remanufactured into black sheets and wire rods.

Following the approval of this plan, the Railway Company early in July 1929 formed a new company, the Showa Steel Works, Limited, with a subscribed capital of Yen 100,000,000, to carry it out. The formal incorporation of

of the new company took place one day after the fall of the Tanaka Cabinet in Japan, and this circumstance, as well as the omission of numerous important details of operation, suggests that the plan was rushed through to avoid possible rejection by the Hamaguchi Government. Among other things, the site of the new steel plant in Japan had not been determined; nor had any agreement been reached as to the assistance to be received from the Government. In fact, about all that seems to have been done was to incorporate the company and order the necessary machinery.

If not actually opposed to it, the Hamaguchi Cabinet did not appear to share the Tanaka Government's enthusiasm over the new project nor to be in any haste to execute it. The expenditure of vast sums of South Manchuria Railway Company money and payment of new subventions were no doubt contrary to the retrenchment and fiscal policies of Premier Hamaguchi, and it is not improbable that conflicting views were and still are held regarding the practical advantages of the enterprise. In any case, a year has elapsed with much official deliberation but without any progress having been made toward realization of the scheme.

In the course of a recent conversation with Vice-Admiral Godo (Imperial Japanese Navy reserve-list), a Director of the South Manchuria Railway and President of the Showa Steel Works, I was informed that "politics" and "politicians" were the cause of the delay in executing the

-3-

the project, and that it was merely a matter of time before the scheme would be carried out. Admiral Godo added that the economic aspects of the enterprise had been carefully studied and found practical in every respect. President Sengoku of the Railway Company, however, with whom I had occasion to converse early in June, evidently thinks differently. He doubts whether the enterprise can be worked at a profit with world prices of steel at their present low level, and is not so confident that it will be eventually carried out. He hinted in fact that the Government would decide shortly whether to proceed with or give up the project.

Mr. Sengoku expressed the opinion that the previous administration of the Railway Company had acted too hastily and carelessly in the matter, and that no action should have been taken until every political detail has been settled. Questioned as to the obstacles in the way of execution of the scheme, Mr. Sengoku explained that one of them was the loss of Yen 8,000,000 of revenue.

Although Mr. Sengoku's remarks were general in nature, it is understood that the political details alluded to refer to (1) Japanese customs treatment of Anshan ingots, (2) Chinese customs treatment of such exports, and (3) the source of the necessary subsidy.

Anshan ingots, being foreign goods, would have to be assessed the regular tariff duty. If they were to be exempted from such taxation, the same privileges would have to be extended to similar products originating

in

in countries enjoying most-favored nation treaty relations with Japan. Anshan ingots too, being Chinese products, would have to pay the Chinese export duty, which under Chinese tariff autonomy might later prove a handicap on profitable operation.

The loss of revenue mentioned by Mr. Sengoku refers, of course, to the subsidy of Yen 8,000,000 considered necessary. This will no doubt be met partly by a refund to the Steel Works of the import duty on its products and partly by a grant from the National Treasury.

Practically the entire machinery for the new Works has arrived in Dairen and is lying on the wharves. It includes equipment for producing sheet bars, billets and black sheets. This plant was purchased last year from the Krupp and "Demag" (Deutsche Maschinen Aktiengesellschaft) companies and cost $3,000,000.

According to the press, the fate of the Showa Steel Works is being settled in Tokyo at this moment, and will be known shortly. The impression has been gained that the present government of Japan regards the project as a problem if not as a great mistake, and that it would not hesitate for one moment to abandon it were it not for the three million dollars worth of machinery on its hands.

I have the honor to be, Sir,

Your obedient servant,

Wm. R. Langdon,
American Consul.

At the same time there were rumours current that the enterprise might be abandoned, reasons for which were succinctly set forth in despatch No. 894 c. 6511/14 115 of July 11, 1930, of the American Consul at Dairen. Of the most important officials concerned Doctor Sengoku, President of the South Manchuria Railway Company, seemed to be the only one favoring Anshan. The press reported that the Minister for Foreign Affairs preferred that the site should be in Japanese territory; that the Minister for Finance also favored Shin Gishu, establishment at Anshan preventing any exemption of products from duty and availability of Government bounties; that the Governor General of Chosen, not unnaturally, was strongly in favor of Shin Gishu as the site. It was then said that the selection of Shin Gishu would depend upon the possibilities of Tashito, below Shin Gishu, as a good harbor. While in Tokyo Doctor Sengoku conferred with certain engineers on this subject, and although most reports indicated that, in the opinion of these experts, Tashito would prove satisfactory, at the end of the month Doctor Sengoku left for Dairen for further research in respect to this matter.

THE 400,000 SHARE

KAWASAKI ANXIOUS TO BUY THEM BACK
INTENSE RIVELY

Nearly ten days have passed since the transfer of 400,000 preference shares of the Kawasaki Shipbuilding Company to the Osaka Shosen Kaisha was announced and now a serious situation is developing in connection with the case.

When the transfer of the shares was announced, the Kawasaki Steamship Company who had been negotiating with the Fifteenth Bank for the repurchase of the shares strongly opposed the transfer. Mr. Itani, president of the Kawasaki Steamship Company, hastily summoned the executive staff of the company and of the Kawasaki Shipbuilding Company and explained to them the complications which would arise out of the transfer and asked them to support him in opposing it.

Shortly after this Mr. Itani went up to Tokyo with a few executive directors of the company to make arrangements to buy back the shares, with the result that sharp differences have arisen between the Kawasaki and O.S.K. concerns. Anxious over the serious situation, Baron Go and Mr. Hirao, former Education Minister, decided to take it upon themselves to mediate between the contending parties.

The developments of the case are being watched with interest by shipping circles as well as by industrial quarters.

The Shipbuilding Industry Law, composed of some 53 articles, may be divided for convenience into three parts: the first and most important part, consisting of Article 1 - 16, relates to government control of the shipbuilding industry; the second portion of the law, dealing for the most part with government control of shipbuilders' associations

associations, contains little that is not already familiar in connection with the control of other Japanese industrial associations, and accordingly, is believed to warrant only passing mention here. The last part, Articles 42-53, is made up of the penal provisions of the law.

The salient features of the Shipbuilding Industry Law may be summarized as follows:

1. Government approval must be obtained before anyone may engage in the shipbuilding business, which by the terms of the law includes construction and repair of vessels, the manufacture or repair of hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof.

2. Changes in the organization of a shipbuilding company must have the approval of the government.

3. The government may encourage the construction of hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof of a type hitherto not manufactured in Japan through the granting of subsidies.

4. The government is empowered to order shipbuilding companies to use domestic articles in the construction or repair of vessels, hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof.

5. The government may grant direct subsidies to shipbuilding companies or to individuals ordering ships from shipbuilding companies.

6. The government is empowered to determine specifications of hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof.

7. A shipbuilding company may with government approval issue debentures in excess of the limits prescribed in the Commercial Code, provided such amount of debentures is not greater than twice the amount of the paid-up shares.

8. Whenever it is deemed necessary in the public interest, the government may order shipbuilding companies to alter prices of construction, sale or repair of ships, hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof.

9. Whenever the government deems it necessary, orders may be issued to shipbuilding companies with regard to the installation of equipment, with regard to the construction or repair of vessels, hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof, and with regard to research on ship construction.

10. Shipbuilding companies, shipbuilders' associations, or federations of shipbuilders' associations are required to submit operating and financial reports to the government for approval and supervision.

The present law like most recent Japanese legislation may be regarded as an outcome of the China incident. The need has been recognized for some time by those connected with the transportation problem in Japan for a greatly implemented fleet of commercial vessels both for the purposes of the trade with China and for international competition.

As

=== Page 15 === (confidence 1.00)
-4-

As a result of activities on the continent a great many Japanese commercial vessels have been taken off normal shipping routes; a goodly number of these have been employed in shipping routes in China hitherto dominated by Chinese or British shipping. The shortage in other routes created thereby is regarded as more or less permanent and therefore it is argued must be filled by new vessels. To this end the government in the present law takes the initiative in granting direct subsidies to shipbuilding companies or individuals for the construction of ships. Moreover, the shipbuilding companies are to be permitted to exceed the limits of the Commercial Code with regard to the issue of debentures. A second consideration in the formulation of the Shipbuilding Industry Law has been that of construction costs as compared with other countries. It is felt that high prices of materials in supply industries have contributed not a little to the comparatively high costs of construction of Japanese ships. To offset this tendency the prices of ship construction, repairs, etc. are to be controlled by the government whenever it is deemed necessary and the use of domestic (therefore cheaper) products in ship construction is to be encouraged. Finally the conclusion is drawn that the shipbuilding industry is in its unrestricted state affected too closely by the shipping industry, leading naturally to the third and no less important consideration that the shipbuilding industry must be brought directly under government control and supervision, a step in keeping with the general movement toward the regimentation and socialization of

of all industry in Japan. Meanwhile plans are being rapidly worked out for the control by the government of the shipping industry itself which now stands almost as an anachronism in a rapidly developing realm of controlled industry in Japan. The Embassy will endeavor to obtain an outline of these plans and forward it to the Department at the earliest opportunity.

Article 1. The shipbuilding industry within the meaning of this law shall denote the business of building or repairing ships by persons provided with equipment prescribed by order.

The manufacture or repair of hulls, marine engines, fittings or parts and accessories thereof, by persons engaged in the business described in the preceding paragraph shall be construed to be a part of the aforesaid business.

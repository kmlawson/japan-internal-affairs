A touchatone to some
Pacile Affinities in the Far East
(A Gentile's View)

The attempt of the Nazi regime in Germany to eliminate
Germans of Jewish blood from public office and influence is
reported to have stimulated in Japan a movement to provide
for distinguished Jewish exiles a refuge with opportunities
for gainful employment and service. Simultaneously, Japanese
news agencies circulated, during September, a report that Jew-
ish fur buyers in Manchukuo had decided to boycott the Leipzig
fur fair in consequence of reports that the fair was discrimi-
natory toward Jews. The latter report proved premature when
the fair authorities denied such discrimination. The former,
however, though accompanied by a type of publicity in the press
which smacks of passing effervescence, may mature into something
of broad interest to Jews generally, having potentialities con-
ceivably of material importance to the scientific, social and
political life of the Far East. Germany's withdrawal from the
League of Nations in October raises the question whether in this
Japanese-Jewish-German rapport there may not be affinities, real
or imaginary, of peculiar interest to Jews and perhaps of even
broader interest.

In mid-September the Osaka Mainichi, a leading Japanese
newspaper, which until about a month before the Japanese mili-
tary occupation of Mukden in 1931 was generally considered to
be rather liberal in its point of view, published a three-column
article under the caption "German Scholars 'Guilty of Being Jews'
May Be Welcomed in Japan". The Mainichi announced that

"Scores of eminent German scientists, artists and
scholars of Jewish blood will be in exile in Japan
when the good offices exerted by the Japan-German
Cultural Society bear fruit. These men and women
of ability, who through no fault of their own have
been or are about to be made homeless, will be given
a chance to prove their worth in Japan."

Dr. Naruhiko Sata, president of this Society, was stated to
have been in communication with Dr. Wilhelm Solf, former German
Ambassador to Japan, with Dr. Haber, described as the inventor
of poison gas, and Dr. Aschopf, the celebrated medical man who
has many followers in Japan.

Dr. Sata announced that many Jewish savants, scientists, technologists and artists had already accepted invitations to come to Japan. These include, according to a list published by Dr. Sata in the Mainichi, acceptances from Professor Jaffe, physicist, of Giessen University; Professor Ehrenberg, physicist, of Stuttgart Technical University; Professor Traube, chemist, of Berlin Technical University; Professor Levy, chemist, of Aachen University; Professor Flager, applied chemistry and mechanics, of Karlsruhe Technical University; Professor Arthur Saltz, economist, of Heidelberg University; Professor Franz Aisnel, economist, of Hamburg University; Professor Bergen, biologist, of Freiburg University; Dr. Rudolf Stern, neurologist and neuropathist, of Berlin; Frau Hilderhofchek Geiringer, lecturer on mathematics in Berlin University; and the musicians and composers, Messrs. Arnold Schoenberg, Herman Reichenbach, Abraham Witch and Rudolf Kastner.

There may be value, beyond describing those factors in this situation which have particular interest to Jews, in an attempt to judge the genuineness of those facile affinities between European and Far Eastern situations which this invitation to Jews suggests. Does it describe inferentially the attitude of the Japanese and their government toward the Nazi regime in Germany, toward Germans, or even toward Jews? Or is the invitation merely prompted by a presumed affinity with a persecuted people, since the Japanese themselves are just now suffering from an inordinately pronounced case of paromania? Or should the fair-minded be more generous when imputing motives?

Does the land of the Yamato race breath the spirit of liberty which prompted the Dutch to give refuge to the forebears of that master mind, the incomparable Spinoza? Or is the star of hope that points to Dai Nihon a meteor, made flashy by eclectic publicity, perhaps but a nebulous thing, naive, immaterial and momentary? If so, may not this report of a refuge for Jews in Japan be a luminescent touchstone to the dominant mores that just now move the Japanese in their well-launched program of expansion, cultural as well as political, in Eastern Asia? For in the state of Manchukuo, and in the Japanese program of seeking in China a limitless goal of political primacy the Japanese will continue to be confronted with perplexing problems so urgent that they have a need to have among them those with vision to know what to do and those with technical ability to know how to do it. There may even be a demand for new martial music.

One reason why it is so extremely difficult to know just exactly what the Japanese are thinking about this or that situation arising from their international isolation and their grave internal problems is the peculiar quirk of their people, peasant and potentate alike, for forming snap judgments based on no better evidence than quick impressions as to the sameness of things, quick judgments, therefore, as to differences. This tendency explains, in part, the facility with which the press shifts from anti-Amer-

ican to anti-British vilification under stimuli which clearly come from immediate and perhaps very temporary purposes of the Government. If, for example, the Japanese Military desire a particular appropriation bill to pass the Diet it may be accompanied by a campaign of publicity concerning "a certain foreign power", but when the appropriation is voted the campaign is called off as abruptly as it was started. The close observer should not take these periodic epidemics of publicity too seriously. By the same token they have in them potentialities for sudden reappearance.

The superficial sameness of things, therefore, determines even very consequential political decisions in the diplomatic field. So also in everyday life where foreigners are concerned. When a foreigner carries a camera, particularly a moving picture camera, he is widely regarded as a potential if not active spy. The spy mania is just now in its hey-day in Japan.

A humorous story will illustrate this peculiar twist of the mind which is more awry now because of the pent-up emotions of the people. It illustrates, also, that not only the police, but other officials of the Government are at times victims of this tendency to snap judgments. One of the technical experts who was attached to the Lytton Commission of Inquiry during the period of its investigation into the Sino-Japanese dispute in 1932 was credited in the Japanese Foreign Office, especially by the "official spokesman", with having written the entire report. His reasoning was as follows: "The Lytton Report is written in American-English (sic!). This expert was the only one attached to the Commission who was capable of writing the report's official style in the American language (sic!). Therefore, this expert wrote the entire report (sic!)." It took many months for this individual to upset the ridiculous conclusion, however patently erroneous, which was derived from a most facile formation of superficial affinities in the minds of a few officials of the F.O. There is, of course, the chance that the F.O. did not take their own argument seriously. If so, it was evidently considered palatable to others as propaganda.

Jews residing in the Far East know how frequently this characteristic way of thinking to wry conclusions has affected individuals of their own race. Time and again Jews have been made subjects of ridiculous allegations in the Japanese press, the object being in each case to discredit them for very passing reasons. The method is a photostat of the shallow reasoning by suggestivism and superficial affinity described. The same type of thought has long been evident in the situation suggested by the fact that in the Government of the U.S.S.R. there have been quite a few Jews in the Far East.

The following syllogism describes the reasoning of a good many, high and low, in Japan: "Soviet Russia, like Tsarist Russia, is a potential enemy of Japan. The Revolution, which made this potential enemy more dangerous because it was communistic, was participated in by Jews. Jews, especially Russian Jews,

The easy association in the Japanese mind of Russian Jews with Jews generally is illustrated by a particularly naive, not to malevolent, attack upon M. Robert Haas, a French Jew, while he was acting as secretary-general of the Lytton Commission of Inquiry in Manchuria. The Japanese press publicized a story, quite without any foundation, to the effect that M. Haas had had frequent conversations in Harbin, Manchuria, with local Russian Jews; that it had been ascertained "on a certain authority" (a phrase used ad nauseam to describe a secret source or sheer propaganda) that these conversations had for their object to obtain the support of the League of Nations for the well-known experimental colony of Jews in Biro-Bidjan, Siberia, which adjoins the Manchurian borders. It was said that since the Biro-Bidjan Jewish colony was largely financed by American Jews, and the United States was co-operating with the League of Nations to oppose Japan in Manchuria, the possibility that the colony would soon be militarized, and become a base for military operations against Japan and "Manchukuo", was viewed with deep concern in Japan.

This story, though it was intended merely as a device to discredit M. Haas, therefore the Lytton Commission, and the League of Nations, illustrates how easily Russian Jews and their interests may be identified in the Japanese mind with Jews generally. The story was false and entirely irreconcilable with the fact that few, if any, of the permanent technical officials of the League of Nations are so internationally-minded as M. Haas, though he is a Frenchman and served with the French forces in the Great War.

Similarly, the Japanese press, including the most reputable news agencies of the country, have carried dispatches which can hardly have for their purpose any object but to discredit the League by alleging that there is in the Secretariat a "Jewish bloc", including M. Robert Haas, the French Jew, and Dr. Ludwik Rajchmann, a Polish Jew. Such allegations have now been taken seriously, outside of Japanese circles, in the Far East.

The motives for this attempt to discredit two Jews spring from other causes, however, than the fact that they are Jews. The result, however, is to throw in for good measure the presumably useful additional fact that they are Jews. The case of Dr. Ludwik Rajchmann illustrates this very well.

Dr. Rajchmann incurred criticism from official Japanese sources even before the appointment of the Lytton Commission, for he was at the time in Shanghai in close association with leaders in the National Government of China, especially Mr. T. V. Soong, the Finance Minister. His work as technical expert of the League in China required such intimate association. The Japanese, however, and not without justification, alleged that he practically identified himself politically with the Chinese cause in a controversial issue. Consequently, when he was sent out to China by the League in the summer of 1933, after the League's judgment against Japan on the basis of the Lytton Report, Dr. Rajchmann was again made the subject of repeated attacks in the Japanese press. Dr. Rajchmann and M. Haas are both identified in the Japanese mind with the League's judgment against Japan in the Manchurian dispute. The fact that they are Jews would be more or less inconsequential were it not for the resulting reference occasionally in the press that they are Jews, as if to add insult to injury. The fact that Dr. Rajchmann is a Pole has probably tempered reference to him as a Jew. So also with M. Haas. And were their duties other than as League officials, but more acceptable to Japan, the fact that they are Jews would be conveniently overlooked, even extolled.

There is, also, a Jewish phase of the situation presented by the presence of something in the neighborhood of a hundred thousand Russians in Harbin, in northern Manchukuo. Their presence complicates the perplexing minority problems which confront the Japanese in Manchukuo. Their presence, including the Jews, suggests also a source of certain of the Japanese ideas toward Russian Jews and therefore Jews generally.

The Russian colony in Harbin dates from the closing years of the last century. By the time of the Russian Revolution Harbin numbered about a hundred thousand Russians; their political, commercial and social interests centered largely around the Russian-constructed, financed and controlled Chinese Eastern Railway. After the Revolution, this Russian colony became a most polyglot thing, including Russians of various and irreconcilable political and religious views, "White Russians", the majority, who were opposed to the Soviet Union, "Red Russians", a minority, who supported the Soviet Union and, in the main, were employed in the Chinese Eastern Railway (especially after 1924), and others conveniently called "Pinks" or "Carrots" ("red" outside, "white" inside), because to describe them more exactly was so inconvenient.

In this colony of Russians, there have always been many Russian Jews, many more certainly than in the whole of the Japanese Empire, their numbers giving a much higher ratio to the Jewish element in the entire foreign colony than in any other city in the Far East. Among them are both "Reds" and "Whites". The latter

includes those who came to Harbin in its mushroom days as merchants and traders, many of whom have become wealthy, and those who, as refugees from the Soviet Union, came across the Manchurian frontiers, probably a very small minority. The former includes, in the main, officials and employees of the Chinese Eastern Railway and its adjunct institutions. So polyglot is the resulting picture of the Russian colony in Harbin that the Japanese, whose influence now dominates Harbin, must be baffled even to find facile affinities between this or that Jew and the U. S. S. R. There is, as a result, little mention of the Jews in the Japanese news from this city. Moreover, the Soviet Government does not seem to have given any special prominence to Jews in their official personnel either in Harbin, or in the Far East generally, at least in recent years.

There are, however, potentialities in this Harbin situation, linked as it is with the future of the Chinese Eastern Railway, recently renamed the North Manchurian Railway by the Manchukuo Government, which do suggest that the presence in Harbin of thousands of Jews will some day be of world-wide interest, especially to Jews. Just now the Japanese officials of Manchukuo, supported and abetted by the Japanese Government under the influence of the Japanese Military, are conducting a determined campaign of military, diplomatic, political, publicity and propaganda pressure to dispossess the Soviet Union from influence and control in the railway, the better to buy it cheaply, or obtain it for nothing. In this campaign several Soviet Russian officials of the railway have been arrested and incarcerated. Searches and seizures are being made with the object of obtaining incriminating evidence to justify closure of offices and organizations where Soviet influence controls or dominates, including trade unions, commercial, cultural and recreational institutions, such as the Communist Youth Organization (Consomol), whether directly affiliated with the railway company or not. The danger that some Jews may be involved in this situation is, of course, great, and that not because they are Jews but because they are citizens of the U. S. S. R.

At first thought it might seem that this situation has no particular significance to Jews elsewhere, and no bearing whatsoever on the report that Japan is now about to offer asylum to German Jews. The Jewish race should not, indeed, be held responsible for the political acts of those of their faith who have this or that political nationality. But the intelligent Jew, familiar with the history of his people, with the suspicion and persecution to which his people have been subjected through the centuries in Europe, needs hardly to be urged to consider even the most irrational mental associations which such a situation as exists in Manchukuo, particularly in Harbin, may create in the minds of responsible Japanese authorities on the spot.

The present dispute between Japan and the Soviet Union over the Chinese Eastern Railway is grave, and that in spite of what is generally believed to be the unpreparedness of the U.S.S.R. for a war with Japan. An open military clash may come in the future. In that case Harbin would be a bedlam of a hundred thousand Russians,

Jews and Gentiles, Reds, Whites and otherwise. Race cuts deep grooves in the mind in times when war psychology reigns. It is even conceivable that a most irrational conclusion may be drawn from one that is rational: Russian Jews are Russians; therefore, Russian Jews are Reds. It is not certain, then, that those Russian Jews who are now considered to be "Whites", and opposed to the Soviet régime, would continue to be thought of as allies. Nor is it certain, for that matter, that all of them would in fact continue to be pro-Japanese when actually a conflict broke with the Soviet Union, wherein not only Soviet, but distinctly Russian, interests and aspirations were at stake. The Russian, too, has patriotism, which is love of country transcending a particular government in power.

To-day there are, however, numerous Jews in Harbin who are frankly favorable to the new Japanese régime in Manchukuo. Many are wealthy, or were wealthy in more prosperous times a few years ago. They welcomed the entry of the Japanese troops into Harbin after February of 1932, especially because under the old Chinese administration they were at times subjected to discriminatory treatment accorded "White Russians" generally by the Chinese officialdom. They were and are men without a country, so-called emigré "White Russians".

Of these "White Russian" Jews some are politically disinterested, others quite naturally are favorable to the Japanese in their own private and professional interest, while others are actively engaged in aiding and abetting the Japanese cause against Soviet interests in Manchukuo. There are evidences, however, that many of these White Russians who welcomed the advent of the Japanese troops and influence in Harbin have cooled in their advocacy of the Japanese cause. They have met with disillusion, born of continued political and economic uncertainty, and signs that the Japanese penetration tends toward nationalistic and paternalistic monopoly. In cases they have been subjected to economic pressure and what amounts to capital levies. Their economic future is by no means certain even under the Japanese banner.

An interesting sidelight on the condition of these Russian Jews is the fact that a few of the wealthier among them have been singled out by under-cover organizations of Chinese opponents of the new Manchukuo Government as objects for terroristic activities and kidnapping. It is believed that these Chinese organizations operate in league with those armed bands or irregulars who refuse to submit to the Manchukuo regime and the Japanese army. Reasoning that certain wealthy Russian Jews in Harbin are well-disposed toward the Japanese, they see in them a "logical" object of their anti-Japanese movements. A case in point is that of the recent kidnapping in Harbin, and holding for ransom, of the son of Mr. Kaspe, the Russian Jew, considered to be a "White Russian", who is the proprietor of Harbin's leading hostelry, the Hotel Moderne.

Such constraint might reasonably serve the conclusion that one terrorized in this manner would be the more convinced in his pro-Japanese proclivities. The conclusion is uncertain, however, for the very constraint might well be attributed to the changed

Turning, then, to the motives which possibly underly the reported Japanese invitation to Jewish savants, scientists, technologists and artists, the discerning observer readily sees them to be both manifold and very mixed. The Japan-German Cultural Society, which is sponsoring the invitation, nurtured as such societies are in an internationalism qualified by their limited purposes, has evidently acted from motives at once altruistic and utilitarian. "Japan", in the words of Dr. Sata, the society's president, "would do wisely to make good use of the knowledge and ability" of these distinguished German Jews, who are about to become exiles "through no fault of their own".

Pure altruism is very rare. Accepting the possibility that there may be considerable refined, if not pure, altruism behind the reported Japanese welcome to these German Jews, it is more than likely that the invitation is a byproduct of a feeling, part of the dominant mores of the Japanese of 1933, that they themselves, though nationals of one of the Great Powers, are grudgingly granted a nominal equality -- racially, culturally, politically -- which ill-conceals a general occidental attitude of superiority and discrimination resulting.

I suspect, therefore, that there is in this situation much that bears out the truth of an analysis of altruism contained in one of the few books read in college days which "sticks" in my mind: George Herbert Palmer's *Altruism, its nature and varieties*. Altruism, as commonly identified with manners, giving, mutuality and even love, is surcharged with a surprising amount of egoism. And that which falls short of justice, which Palmer describes as superpersonal love, exhibits a generosity interblended with selfishness. In Japan, religious toleration officially prevails. Nor does the practice belie the law, except for reoccurrent anti-foreign vagaries and insofar as the demands of the national cult, or state religion, Shintoism, impinge upon complete tolerance. Manifestations of such intolerance are, unfortunately, not uncommon in the present "national emergency" state of Japan. A recent example is the trumped-up campaign, widely heralded in the press, against a foreign mission school which refuses to encourage its students to make an annual pilgrimage to the Shinto shrines at Ise. A distinguished Greek Orthodox prelate, long a missionary in Japan, was recently forced to discontinue his missionary work in certain interior portions of Japan, because of a ridiculous allegation that he, in common with his kind, made inroads upon the Japanese "national spirit". Highly reputed missionaries of many sects, whose long residence in Japan gives them authority to speak to the point, albeit they tend to be very generous to the Japanese point of view, frankly admit that never in their experience in Japan, not even during the Russo-Japanese war, have the people been so aroused to a consciousness of "the national spirit", with the result that their non-political activities have been severely prejudiced.

But, whatever the condition of lay-thought and tolerance among the masses, and whatever the noble letter-aims of Japanese institutions of higher learning which posit religious tolerance, it cannot be said that Japanese official education exhibits in practice a broad tolerance of non-conformists tenets, religious, political and social. The laws and regulations against so-called "dangerous thoughts" destroy much of the liberal purpose behind the magnanimous Imperial Rescript which/breadth of view and benevolence to all. extolls

Freedom of the press and of public expression do not exist to-day in Japan. By 1930 there were evidences of a rapid growth of tendencies toward tolerance of liberal ideas in the political field. But, by 1932 the Japanese Military, who now practically dictate policy on fundamental questions, such as defence and military preparedness, had effectively gagged the press and platform, whether by direction or indirection, the latter through the effective device of intoxicating the nation with a propaganda that has created what amounts to a war psychology.

Nor does there exist in Japan a tradition of liberty even remotely similar to that of the Anglo-Saxon, the Dutch or the Swiss. Subservience to canonized norms of conduct, to authority as such, to paternalistic standards in state and family, and the survival of feudal conceptions of family relationships, social and state life, persist to modify the rote respect for liberty. And liberty as a social standard is not held in so high esteem as in the West.

Japan today is in the throes of a social and political struggle which is all but revolutionary. Compared with it the stress and strain of the Russo-Japanese war was but mild and superficial. In political life there have been powerful tendencies toward what in 1932 came to be described as Japanese Fascism. This is not the place to describe its causes, its general manifestations, or to give an adequate judgment as to whether the title is justified, nor to prognosticate the future. Suffice it merely to note those of its characteristics which suggest similarities with Fascism in Italy or Germany.

When the Military came into real power shortly after the military occupation of Mukden, Manchuria, in September of 1931, dissentients were gradually weeded out of the government departments; willing spokesmen among the politicos were substituted; and, as the military campaigns in Manchuria and around Shanghai created new pretexts for convincing the nation that a crucial emergency confronted the state, conscientious objectors were progressively prevailed upon, by persuasion, propaganda and terrorism, to accept what was described as a fait accompli both in Manchuria and in Japan. The result was the dictatorship of the Military which prevails today in Japan. The term "dictatorship" is justified, and will continue to be, until dissentient political groups definitely reject, or materially alter, major policies of the Military. Just now there seems to be a new phase, probably to reach its zenith in the next Diet session about February, after which the tendency may be either to more rigid dictatorship or its gradual liquidation. At all events, the influence of the Military will continue to be the principal factor in Japanese politics for a long time to come. In passing, one should also recall that that influence has been coextensive with Modern Japan.

In these circumstances of dynamic nationalism, led by the Military, Japanese liberalism, political and intellectual, has been blighted in the bud. The pressure upon the press, however, seems to have been relaxed recently, but in all probability because those who exercised the power came to believe that their program of publicity to the masses had already been so successful that rigid repression of contrary political thought was no longer necessary. The masses have been indoctrinated with the Machtpolitik of the Military.

Moreover, many Japanese associate Jews, other than Russians, with the economic progress of the Soviet Union during the last few years, noting also that the Jewish colony in Biro-Bidjan, Siberia, on the borders of Manchukuo, has elicited support and wide-spread interest from Jews in the United States. ## Note 2.
In October it was reported in Japan that the Nazi Government in Germany approved or tolerated cases of discrimination against Japanese on the ground that they, like Jews, were branded as "colored peoples". My dear Mr. Corrigan:

The receipt is acknowledged of your letter of January 29, 1936, in which you state that you wish to obtain information in regard to the development of scientific education and research work in Japan. You enclose with your letter a brochure entitled "National Scholarships as a National Investment."

I regret that there are no reports at hand on this subject and that no materials are at my command in such form as to make possible my sending them to you.

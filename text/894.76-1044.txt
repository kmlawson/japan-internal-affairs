Radio in Japan -- General Survey
Since the inauguration of broadcasting with the
opening of the JOAK station in Tokyo in 1925, the popu-
larity of radio has spread rapidly over the whole of
Japan. There are now twelve broadcasting stations be-
ing operated and seven more are being installed and
should be completed before March 1932. The Japanese
Government exercises strict supervision over the pro-
grams with the double purpose of instructing the people
and avoiding the introduction of ideas which might be
considered undesirable for the public safety and wel-
fare. The number of programs and the total number of
radios in private houses and shops is steadily increas-
ing•
While more and more broadcasting is being done
from the local stations, the majority of material broad-
cast originates in Tokyo and Osaka and is carried over
land wires to the minor stations. Each station runs
its own program of local news, weather reports, etc.,
but the feature items on the programs originate chief-
ly in Tokyo and Osaka, the two largest cities in Japan.
The broadcasting companies have been faced with
several difficulties, one being a lack of funds. The
income derived from the fees paid by owners of receiv-
ing sets is not large, particularly due to the fact that
a number of owners do not register their sets and there-
fore do not pay the monthly subscription fees. Another
difficulty lies in the fact that complaints are often
made that the radio programs are uninteresting. This
is particularly due to the preference of the older
generation

- 4 -
generation of Japanese for the old style Japanese
drama and music, while the younger generation prefers
foreign music. The two types of entertainment are usually
mixed in the programs with the result that neither the
older nor the younger generation is satisfied. Since
the feature items are limited to two in number, the
choice is not great, particularly in view of the fact
that the majority of sets are so small that they are
unable to tune in except on local stations.
The broadcasting programs which attract the
greatest crowds to all shops where broadcasting is dif-
fused through loud speakers are the broadcasting of
accounts of baseball games and other sporting events.
It is understood that the most popular set used
by the average Japanese consists of a crystal aetector,
two or three tubes for amplification, a battery elimi-
nator and a magnetic speaker. Such a set is incapable
of fine tuning but there is no necessity for fine
tuning when there are only two programs to which one
can listen. Most of the cheaper sets are now manu-
factured completely in Japan. Many, however, are
constructed with imported parts.

casting and Radio Transmission
The regulations relating to private wireless tele-
phone stations to be used for broadcasting service are
contained in Ordinance No. 98 of the Department of
Communications, dated December 21, 192l (Revised Febru-
ary, April and December 1925, April 1927 and Decem-
ber l929). This ordinance,composed of nineteen arti-

(1) All broadcasting stations must be licensed.

Wireless Telegraph Law
Censorship and other general matters are provided
for in the Wireless Telegraph Law (Law No. 26 of June
1915, revised Apri1 1921 and apri1 1929). Extracts
from this law in unofficial translation are given in
appendix No. 2. Article 8 provides that no subject
matter shall be broadcast which might disturb the public
peace or be injurious to public morals.
In view of the fact that the kinds of subject mat-
ter to be broadcast must all be stated in the applica-
tion for a license, it is not possible for & company
to broadcast material outside of those provided for and
for which permission has not been granted without being
subject to a fine. Article 17 of the Wireless Tele-
graph Law provides for a fine for persons using private
wireless telegraphs or telephones for purposes other
than those for which they were established. Another
fine is imposed where the officials are hampered or
interfered with in their inspection of radio stations,
etc.
This law, together with local police regulations,
is considered sufficient to provide against the dif-
fusion of "dangerous tnoughts", communistic propaganda,
and information liable to disturb the peace or lower
the morale of the population. While there is no pro-
vision

vision in the general wireless laws for preventing the
inclusion in broadcasting programs of advertising ma-
terial and political campaign speeches, it is under-
stood that the Minister of Comnunications will not
grant permission to a station vhich includes material
of this nature among the purposes for which it is es-
tablished and that therefore since this material must
be deleted from all programs, the companies cannot
use such material vithout being subject to fine. The
only cases in which broadcasting muy be used for pur-

The size of most of the receiving sets is restricted
chiefly, it is understood, in order to prevent the re-
ception of programs broadcast from Soviet Russia. No
receiving sets are povwerful enough to receive programs
from the United States, except when an international
hook-up or relay is arranged for.
It is understood that no short-wave length re-
ceiving or broadcasting sets are permitted. The use of
such sets is allowed only in the case of vessels and
of persons engaged in experimenting in connection with
wireless telegraphy or telephony. Under no circum-
stances are foreigners pemitted to use short-wave
length sets for broadcasting or reception.

Broadcasting Stations in Japan
The first broadcasting station to be established
in Japan was put into operation in Tokyo in 1925. Dur-
ing the same year, stations were also established at
Osaka and Nagoya. Subsequently ten more stations have
been established throughout Japan. The twelve stations
now in operation are to be supplemented in the near
future by new stations which will be installed at
Kochi, Matsue, Niigata, Akita, Hakoate, and Kokura.
Station Jook at Kyoto is reported to be nearly completed.
All these broadcasting stations in Japan are owned by
the Nippon Hoso Kyokai (Broadcasting Corporation of
Japan). This corporation was organized in 1926 with a
paid in capital of V1,389,600. The main office of
this

This network of stations is now able to reach prac-
tically the entire population of Japan proper which num-
bers approrimately o0,000,000. The density of the
population is very great due to the fact that only 15
per cent. of the total aree of Japan is cultivable and
most of the population necessarily lives on this por-
tion of the land. Approximately 30,000,000 people live
in towns and cities of over 5,ooc inhabitants. A great
many stores, restaurants, use radios to attract people
to their doors. By means of loud speakers the radio
programs are broadcast to the street and large crowds
of listeners are often seen athered around the en-
trances of shops where such loud speakers are installed.
The actual number of people, therefore, reached when
important progrems are proadcast is much larger than
the total number of receiving sets would indicate. As
stated elsevhere in this report, the principal features
are relayed through local stations from the main
stations at Tokyo and Osaka, the local stations con-
fining their local programs chiefly to local announce-
ments and to information cf interest to farmers and
other special classes to which they may cater.
The output of power of the antennae ranges from
.5 kilowatts to 1o kilowatts. The wave lengths of the
various stations range from 345 meters to 44l.1. The
frequency ranges from 680 kilocycles to 870.

Purposes of Broadcasting Stations
It appears that no particular broadcasting station
in Japan confines its output to any definite matter.
The programs of all stations include the same miscellaneous
items. More time is actually consumed by all the sta-
tions in the making of announcements than for any other
one class of broadcasting. During the fiscal year,
April 1930 to March 1931, all of the stations in Japan
broadcast for a total of approximately 25,450 hours. Of
this total 9,520 hours were spent in announcements,
6,914 in lectures and speeches, 3,22l in drama and variety
entertainment, 2, 566 in Japanese songs, 2,136 for chil-
dren, 1,098 for foreign songs and music. The following
tables show the distribution of time at each station,

Short-Wave Receiving Sets
The Japanese Government regulations prohibit the
use of radio sets capable of receiving programs from
outside the country, except on special license and on
payment of a large deposit. This was done primarily to
prevent the introduction of Communistic propaganda from
the Soviet stations in Siberia. The authorities, however,
are gradually relaxing in their attitude toward the use
of short-wave length sets, and it is possible that in the
course of time the free use of such sets will be per-
mitted. At present all sets imported into or sold in
Japan are subject to inspection by government officials,
in order to determine whether or not they comply with the
regulations. A few radio enthusiasts have built short-
wave receiving sets, with which they can tune in on
Habarovsk (Siberia), . Manila and Australia.

Use of Radio for Eduoational Purposes
As shown in the tables elsewhere in this report,
the total amount of time spent on educational programs
is considerable. It is estimated that there are ap-
proximately 3,000 radio stations in schools in Japan,
but these are only used incidentally for educational
purposes, except in connection with the daily morning
calisthenics.
Meetings are held monthly by the Broadcasting Inquiry
Committee of the Tokyo Broadcasting Station (Hoso Shimon
Iinka) and the Broadcasting Conmittee (Hoso Iinka) of
the Kobe and Osaka region. At these meetings the general
broadcasting policies are discussed and investigation is
made into the usefulness of various types of radio pro-
grams for educational purposes. It is understood that
at a recent directors' meeting, a plan for the estab-
lishment of a special committee for the investigation of
the educational possibilities of radio was discussed and
an understanding was reached with the authorities of the
Department of Education which may lead to the establish-
ment of a permanent committee in the near future.

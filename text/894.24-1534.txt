Departmental Position on Sale of Residual Surplus Items in Japan
to Japanese Agencies.
The proposal made by Mr. Royall, Under Seoretary of War, in
a letter to Mr. Clayton dated September 3, attached hereto, has
been considered by an ad hoc committee consisting of Mr. Martin
(Chief JK), Mr. McGuire (Assistant Chief FN), Mr. Emmerson (Assist-
ant Chief JA) and me.
Category "C" property proposed for transfer refers to surplus
items mot included under category "A" previously transferred under
NAC action of June 4, 1946 and category "B" except $15 million of
items reserved for Korea under the $25 million credit to the U.S.
Military Government of Korea. It is roughly estimated that these
surpluses mow available amount to $45 million cost valuation and
surpluses to beoome available will amount to an additional $60
million.
As a practical matter this surplus property in Japan would
have te be abandoned, destroyed or otherwise disposed by the War
Department. It would seem that the sale of this surplus property
to Japanese agencies by FLC, when agreed by War and FLC, would
provide economic rehabilitation for the Japanese economy under the
preseribed level of industry in Japan, reduce occupation costs and re-
sult in some direct return in payment for the property transferred.
The committee considering this matter decided to recommend that the
position of the Department be:
1. That the proposed transfer be authorized when in the
judgment of War and FLC sueh transfer would be bene-
ficial to the oceupation of Japan.
2.
That the terms of the obligation for repayment should be sub-
ject to decision by the Japanese Peace Conference.
3.
That the State Department make no commitment as to
the priority of payment or terms it will recommend at
that time for this obligation in relation to all other
Japanese obligations.

Dear Mr. Clayton:
Reference is made to letter of 4 May 1946, from this office,
on the subject of disposal of surplus property in Japan to the Japanese
agencies. Further information and study on this subject discloses the
following:
a. The military property in Japan which is excess to United
States troop requirements must now be considered in three categories.
These are:
(1) Category a. - Items required for prevention of disease
and unrest among the civil population which might endanger or interfere
with military operations and items which will assist in the accomplish-
ment of the objectives of the ocoupation.
(2) Category b. - Surplus items which may be sold to China or
other countries.
(3) Category c. - Surplus items over and above those inaluded
in categories a. and b.
Category a. - The amount of this property is limited by the
restrictions of the theater commander's directive which states that the
civilian economy cannot be developed to a standard higher than that in
neighboring countries.
The National Advisory Council has authorized payment to be
obtained for such property on the same basis and on the same priority
as payment for Civilian Supply Program Imports, i.e., proceeds from
exports shall be available in the first place for payment. These
transfere are made through FLC which handles financial arrangements.
I would like to stress the point that the amounts to be
included finally in the respective categories are at present impossible
to determine. This is due to the fact that category a. is determined
by requirements of military operations in connection with the occupation.
Category b. consists of items which may be included in the
Chinese bulk sale or in sales to other countries.

=== Page 36 === (confidence 0.99)
-2 -
Category c. will consist of the residue of items which it is
not profitable for any purchaser to move from Japan. There will, therefore,
be no sale for these items unless they can be sold to the Japanese agemeies.
Some, but not all, of these items can be determined now. In order, there-
fore, that these items may be of any use whatsoever to the U,8. they would
have to be put to use in the Japanese economy at an early date. It is
possible in case of no bulk sale to China and lack of sales to other
countries that category c. may include all surplus military property in
Japan not economical to return to the United States.
I believe, therefore, that an authorized method of handling
sale of this surplus property to Japanese agencies is not only the most
feasible method of guaranteeing protection of U.S. financial interest
but that it will also insure a considerable reduction in personnel.
Personnel requirements and custodial costs cannot at present be determined
due to the present stage of the Chinese negotiations but both can be
assumed from past experiences to be a considerable commitment. This
commitment must be reduced to the minimum as quickly as possible.
The War Department is required by Executive Order No. 9630,
27 September 1945, to give FLC all required support in men and materials
in expediting the disposal problem. The War Department is at present
experiencing difficulties in overseas areas in furnishing man-power
requirements for this commitment. If disposal can be expedited in this
manner, it would result in an additional saving in men and materials.
Additional supplies put into the Japanese economy now would be
of material economic assistance to the accomplishment of the objectives
of the occupation. It would reduce inflation and make it possible to
more easily distribute available supplies. This would lighten the burden
on the occupation forces.
The stabilisation of the Japanese economy to this extent
would tend to make it possible for the Japanese to assume, at an
earlier date, the responsibility for prevention of disease and unrest
among their own population. This would relieve the occupation forces
of a burden.
Development of the Japanese economy beyond the level per-
mitted by the theater commander's directive would still not be allowed
even with this method of assistance to the military effort. It would
be a step toward the accomplishment of his mission. It would be finaneially
sound in that it would help increase and stabilize indigenous production
and thereby reduce the required military effort. This would effect a
saving in men and materials. It would also reduce the requirements of U.S.
financed imports.

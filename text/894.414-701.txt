The Treaty of 1833 largely governed the scope of the first treaty. It permitted the appointment of ministers or diplomatic agents. It granted to American citizens the right to lease ground and purchase the buildings thereon. It permitted Americans to reside in Volo. The treaty gives Japan the right to destroy any excess quantities of spium found on American cities. Customs duties into Japan are recognized, although the time was not yet come when Japan might keep such tariffs as it thought fit. As the system of Japanese justice was not then considered adequate, special consular courts were set up for the trial of American accused of crime. Freedom of religion was guaranteed. Japan was given the right to engage in the United States scientific, naval, and military men to act as instructors.

## A Surprising Clause

"One clause of this treaty is astonishing to an American of today. It reads as follows:

The President of the United States at the request of the Japanese Government will act as a friendly mediator in such matters of difference as may arise between the Government of Japan and any European Power.

From the point of view of the Senate today this would indeed involve the United States in matters of political concern outside of its own territory and therefore would never receive the Senate's consent to ratification. I am very glad, however, that it appeared in this early treaty because it was proof of the real friendship of America for its sister nation.

"This Treaty was made on behalf of His Majesty the Tycoon. Apparently Townsend Harris, the first American Consul and later Minister, whose tact and discretion are still remembered in Japan, knew little or nothing of the Imperial Court at Kyoto in whose name the Shogun acted. During the coming few years, when the agitation against foreigners often became acute, Harris probably dreaded the growing influence of the Court which was apparently the center of anti-foreign feeling. Little could he have foreseen that this foreign consolation was working toward full restoration of the Imperial Power. Still less could he have known that the young man who was to assume in 1900 the real government of the country would become the friend of those foreigners because he would so clearly recognize the benefits they would bring to his country.

## A Great Statesman

"The Emperor Meiji proved himself one of the really great statesmen of the 10th century. It was under his firm and wise guidance that Japan took advantage of the various clauses of the Treaty of 1855, brought in advisers in science and in military affairs, revised its system of jurisprudence so that in time a whatever's curiosity became an anomaly and he to do so even, so gradually, through the war efforts, took its rightful place as an agent in the family of modern nations. These years are a period of the response of achievement which has never been surpassed in world history. Because of this, future historians will expect the Meiji Period to be of the greatest advantage to all those.

## To Be Used for Study

"In expressing our sincere thanks for the generosity of the United States Government as manifested by these presents, I might add that they will most certainly contribute to the promotion of the friendship between our two countries. These documents will also be profitably used by the Historiographic Institute of our University which has been compiling historical records of our country, now for over 40 years, and these precious documents, I assure you, will be preserved in the safety vaults of the new library for years to come.

"Just here may I be allowed to make one remark. I regard it as of special interest and significance that today's presentation is made in this memorial room of the new Library which was the gift of an American citizen, Mr. John D. Rockefeller, Jr. As you know, Mr. Rockefeller's generosity has enabled us to hasten the reconstruction of our University Library and there is no need to expatriate upon how deeply gratified we are for his help and sympathy.

"Furthermore may I state that the gifts from universities, libraries and individuals in the United States have reached the considerable number of almost a hundred thousand volumes, and this collection is now consummated by the precious documents which I have just received from Your Excellency. I wish to take this opportunity to renew our expressions of appreciation for thanks for these tokens of the sympathy of your nation toward us and beg that Your Excellency will kindly convey our gratitude to the nation you represent.

"Finally may I thank Your Excellency for your presence here today and also our distinguished guests who have no kindly come to witness this significant presentation."

Tokyo, April 17, 1931.
No. 203.

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to inform the Department that the Embassy has been in negotiation, un officially, with the Foreign Office for a period of over a year, regarding the method employed by the tax authorities to assess the excess income tax on American insurance companies doing business in Japan. The salient features of this controversy between the insurance companies and the tax officials are as follows:

According to Japanese law, foreign insurance companies doing business in Japan are required to deposit with the Japanese

=== Page 6 === (confidence 0.99)
-2-

Japanese Government Japanese currency or Japanese national bonds, to protect the interests of the policy holders in Japan. In conformity with this law, the Home Insurance Company of New York, and the Continental Insurance Company of New York, deposited with the Japanese Government Japanese bonds which bear a clause exempting the bonds and the interest thereon from all forms of taxes.

Article 7 of the Income Tax Law provides that the method of assessing the income tax on (A) companies which do not have their principal office in Japan and (B) companies which have a taxable income and a non-taxable income, shall be determined by Imperial Ordinance. Accordingly, Article 2 of the Regulations for the Enforcement of the Income Tax sets forth methods to calculate the capital on which a foreign company operates in Japan. It should be explained that the excess income tax varies with the rate of the profit made on the capital; for example, an income representing 30 per cent on the capital is liable to a higher rate than an income representing a profit of only 10 per cent on the capital. Article 3 of the same Regulations provides for a method of separating the taxable income from the non-taxable income of companies, whether they be foreign or Japanese companies. The practice of the taxation authorities in fixing the excess income tax of each of these American insurance companies is first to determine the capital of the local branch by multiplying the entire capital of the company by the ratio between the premiums taken in by the Japan branch and the total premiums taken in by the company throughout the world. This amount is then multiplied by the ratio between the non-taxable income in Japan and the total income in Japan. The result of the second formula is, of course, to decrease the capital of

of the Japan branch and correspondingly to increase the rate of profit.

The insurance companies have protested that the formulae above described operate to bring about an indirect tax on bonds which are admitted to be free from taxation.

A number of so staff has recently had conferences with officials in the foreign office, and in the Department of Finance, and it is his opinion that the method of assessing the excess income tax is authorized by the various laws and regulations. The insurance companies do not claim that any discrimination has been practiced between them and other foreign or Japanese companies. I am, therefore, of the opinion that the Embassy is not in a position to render the companies further assistance at the present conjucture. It appears to me that the companies should be advised, if they believe the method of taxation now in use constitutes a violation of the engagement on the part of the Japanese Government to except the bonds and the interest thereon from taxation, to bring the case before that Government through the usual channels open to those who have a claim against it.

I have outlined only the salient features of what is a very complicated question. If, however, the information obtained in this despatch is not sufficient to permit the Department to form any judgment in the matter, I shall be pleased to describe the circumstances in detail.

Tokyo, March 20, 1931.

W.W. Glass, Esquire,
Acting Manager or Japan,
The Home Insurance Company,

My dear Mr. Glass:

At the suggestion of an official at the Foreign Office, I went to call yesterday at the Department of Finance to discuss the question of the excess income tax imposed on foreign insurance companies. I spent the entire morning there, and the following is briefly a summary of the conversation which I had with officials of the Bureau of Taxes, the principal one of these officials being Mr. Hoshino.

You will remember that in my last letter to the Foreign Office I put forward three arguments against the formulae used by the Tokyo Tax Office in calculating your excess income tax.

1. If it were proper to employ the interest on the bonds deposited by you, the capital amount of the bonds should be included in the computation of the working capital of the Japan branch.

2. As the Department of Finance had admitted that the deposited bonds were the property of the head office and not

not of the Japan branch, the interest on the bonds should not be included in the income of the Japan branch.

3. The formulae used by the Tokyo tax office were not authorized by Articles 2 and 3 of the Regulations for the Enforcement of the Income Tax Law.

With regard to the first point, the officials stated that, under Article 2 of the Regulations, they could compute the capital of the Japan branch by totalling the various assets which the company had in Japan, such as the principal amounts of the deposited bonds, cash in hand, and various other assets; but they said that this amount would be so small that the insurance companies would have to pay a very large sum in excess income taxes. It was out of consideration for the foreign insurance companies that they had adopted the second method authorized by Article 2 of the Regulations, which is, as you know, the multiplication of the capital of the entire company by a ratio between the premiums taken in by the Japan branch, and the total premiums of the company. They could choose only between these two methods, and they said that, if the amount of the deposited bonds was to be included in a computation of the capital of the Japan branch, they could only do it by using the first method.

The argument which we had over the second point, that is to say the impropriety of using the interest from the deposited bonds in a calculation of the income of the Japan branch because the bonds were not the property of the Japan branch, revolved around

Tokyo, Japan, February 27, 1935.

SUBJECT: Transmitting, and Comments on, The Annual Report of Bureau of Post Office Life Insurance for the Fiscal Year 1933-1934.

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE.
WASHINGTON.

I have the honor to transmit herewith as of possible interest to the Departments of Labor and Interior a copy of The Annual Report of the Bureau of Post Office Life Insurance for the Fiscal Year 1933-1934.

The report shows the conditions of the Post Office Life Insurance and the Post Office Life Annuities Business for the fiscal year April, 1933 to March, 1934, and for certain items gives comprehensive figures for previous years. There are details of administrative accounting supplemented by graphs and charts covering geographical distribution, occupation, sex and age of policy holders, and schedules for borrowing and surrender values. While the minutiae of the data concern the specialist only, there are facts and concrete figures that even the casual student of Japanese national economy will find interesting.

The business of the Post Office Life Insurance and Post Office Life Annuities is conducted by the Bureau of Post Office Life Insurance which is under the direction of the Minister of Communications and is executed by the Post Offices. Whole Life, Endowment and Infantile Insurance are written. Three kinds of annuities are provided: Immediate Annuity, Single Premium Deferred Annuity, and Instalment Premium Deferred Annuity. The system of the Life and Annuity Insurance embodies features common to life and annuity insurance generally, with special preventative measures for the lapsing of policies. In its incipiency in 1897 the business management was under the direction of the Administration of Postal Money Orders and Savings Bank and brought under its present management in 1920.

A nationally wide free welfare service is maintained by the Bureau of Post Office Insurance for the benefit of policy holders. The service embraces free consultations, prophylactic and visiting nurse services. Under an arrangement with the Japanese Physicians' Association 32,000 medical practitioners throughout Japan are available.

The law requires a triennial survey by industries of Post Office Life Insurance Policy holders. The latest survey for which returns are available, that of 1930, indicates that 42.9 percent of the policy holders were scheduled as having no industry; 42.5 percent as agriculturists; 13.6 percent, manufacturers; 14.1 percent, commerce; 8 percent, civil service and the liberal professions. The law provides the manner in which the funds of the Post Office Life Insurance and Annuities are to be invested: either Government bonds or loans to local self-governing bodies for regional public works and improvements. The extent of these loans is fairly large, and so, in the last analysis, many public works and social welfare institutions in Japan are provided and maintained in part by funds more or less contributed by those directly benefited. This is another instance of the group system that permeates the social structure of Japan and promotes the economic soundness of the internal affairs of the nation.

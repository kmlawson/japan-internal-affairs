Washington, D. C.

Sir:

It is believed that the following information may be of interest to your Department. On April 4, we were approached by a reputable firm of Philadelphia tanners who stated that they had four cargoes of goatskins en route from Calcutta to the United States via Kobe, Japan in the following Japanese bottoms, "Toba Maru", "Truruga Maru", "Tottori Maru" and "Dakar Maru". They approached us as agents of Lloyd's London in an effort to secure insurance against war risk including British seizure, this having been demanded by their banking connections and being unobtainable in the American insurance market. We advised them that we were not in a position to speak for Lloyd's, being merely claims agents for that organization, and that to our knowledge this form of insurance was not available in the English market either.

We stated, however, that we represented also as claims agents, among other companies throughout the world, a Japanese company, the Nippon Fire Insurance Company, Ltd. and would if they wished cable that Company to ascertain whether or not such insurance was available in the Japanese market. This we did, as follows:

"CAN YOU COVER AND WHAT RATE WAR INCLUDING BRITISH SEIZURE FOUR SHIPMENT? GOATSKINS NOW AFLOAT JAPANESE BOTTOMS ENROUTE CALCUTTA TO NEW YORK VIA KOBE VALUED TOTAL HUNDRED TWENTYFIVE THOUSAND DOLLARS AMERICAN CONSIGNEE OTHER SHIPMENTS TO FOLLOW"

The succeeding day we received the following cable:

"CANNOT COVER WAR RISKS ALONE STOP COVER RATE THREE PERCENT AGAINST NORMAL AND WAR RISKS JAPANESE BOTTOMS INCLUDING BRITISH SEIZURE PREMIUM IN ADVANCE"

The going rate in the American market excluding British capture at this time was approximately 3½%. You will note that the rate quoted by the Japanese for the same risk including British capture was but 3%.

The value of these four cargoes was in excess of $100,000. From our experience in marine insurance, we know that underwriters investigate every possible source in measuring the risk including frequently their own Foreign Office and Admiralties. The implication therefore is that the Japanese underwriters at least do not consider there is likely to be any immediate difficulty with the British which would involve the seizure of these cargoes.

Respectfully

J. Brawn T. Parker

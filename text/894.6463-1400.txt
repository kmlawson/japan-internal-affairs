FAS: CG: WAA:
Kobe's despatch no. 115, March 15, 1941.

The Consul reports that newspapers at Kobe have reported "an all around alleviation in the power restrictions". This has been partially due to increased hydroelectric power, but largely to increased supplies of coal. A larger supply of sulphate of ammonia is reported.

Japanese reports make no reference to imports of Chinese coal. The Consul refers to a shortage of coal in Shanghai which he attributes to heavy exports from China to Japan, aggregating 3,846,368 tons for 1940 an increase of over 600% in six years. An "extraordinary" export of almost a half million tons of coal to Kwantung Leased Territory is reported. The Consul considers as significant the trend in exports of coal and salt to Japan from China and emphasizes the "extraordinary increases in recent years" which he believes indicates the growing dependence of Japan on China, ~~which results~~ in the Japanese Government exhibiting a "callousness" toward the Chinese people and even toward Japanese nationals in their requirements for coal in Shanghai.

SUBJECT: Improving Japanese Electric Power Production Noted in Kobe
1-1085

THE HONORABLE
THE SECRETARY OF STATE
WASHINGTON

SIR:

I have the honor to report that the Kobe press has recently noted "an all around alleviation in the power restrictions", and that as a result of this alleviation the municipal tram service has been improved. Certain alterations in the operating schedules which became necessary in the winter as a result of the curtailment in the distribution of electric power production have now been canceled; the tram services are practically normal at present. A larger rainfall has resulted in an improvement in hydroelectric power, but increased coal supplies are also responsible for the better electric power supply.

The improvement in respect to power has been noted in other directions in Kobe. Particularly significant is the report of an increase in sulphate of ammonia the shortage of which has been directly related to the decrease in the 1940 rice crop in Japan.

THE JAPAN TIMES &amp; ADVERTISER in its issue of February 20, 1941, reported that the favorable trend in the sulphate of ammonia production was attributed to "bigger supply of coal, electricity and other factors".

The press in Japan has recently referred to the success of the Japanese Government's program for increased output of coal. THE JAPAN TIMES &amp; ADVERTISER (March 13, 1941) reported that large collieries indicated a production increase of 5 percent over the scheduled amounts for January and 7 percent for February. It was stated that

"Clearly reflecting the new favorable coal distribution trend in Japan at present, the coal stock of the Japan Electric Generation and Transmission Company has increased from 300,000 tons to 800,000 tons.

"In the chemical and fiber industries, one can find no trace today of any difficulty in obtaining coal, as was the case at the beginning of last year.

"At the same time ... there are many indications that the supply of coal to iron and steel manufacturing circles will also improve markedly in the near future."

**No References to Imports of Chinese Coal**

In these recently reports of the improvement in the coal and electric power supplies of Japan, the absence of any comment on the availability of coal from China is conspicuous and perhaps significant. Yet coincident with the newspaper publication of articles dealing with the increased coal and power production prominence was given to the curtailment of power in Shanghai and the consequent dissatisfaction aroused among the Japanese cotton spinning and weaving mills in Shanghai. As a result of this dissatisfaction appeals

appeals are reported to have been addressed to the Japanese Government "for official intervention". It was stated that the power supply problem in Shanghai had been created by the inability of the power company to obtain because of the shipping situation its monthly supply of 40,000 tons from British India, that amount being required in addition to 25,000 tons monthly of coal from the Kaiping mines in North China.

**Exports of Chinese Coal to Japan**

The shortage of coal in Shanghai is notorious and of course known to the Department. That shortage and its effect even on the most important single activity of Japanese economic interests in China, the spinning and weaving industry, is of special interest in examining Japanese policy at present. For in spite of the urgent need by Japanese interests in Shanghai of power, and in spite of the coal resources of North China, the figures for exports of coal from China to Japan in 1940 clearly illustrate that the Japanese Government considers those coal resources and the need thereof in Japan as even more vital, because of its present "national defence" and industrial programs. There is evident a readiness to sacrifice Japanese interests in Shanghai for the sake of fulfilling requirements in Japan.

Coal exports from China to Japan proper in 1940 reached a figure of 3,846,368 tons. In 1935 exports were only 615,745 tons. In six years, in spite of the hostilities, the export of coal from China to Japan has increased six times.

Not only has Japan proper obtained such important supplies of coal from China, but it is notable that the Chinese Maritime Customs returns show that extraordinary exports of coal to the Kwantung Leased Territory and to Korea have also taken place:

The export of almost a half million tons of coal from China to Dairen, the great port of Manchuria, a region noted for its coal resources, is extraordinary.

Of China's total exports of 4,838,009 tons in 1940, Japan, the Kwantung Leased Territory and Korea took a total of 4,681,912 tons or 96.7 percent of the total.

On occasion in discussing with Americans, official and otherwise, Japanese economic policies of exploitation, there has been observed a disposition to minimize the results of Japanese policy in regard to coal exploitation (likewise salt) in China and to predict failure. Admitting the relatively small quantities of coal being obtained by Japan, it appears that such persons ignore entirely

entirely the trend and its significance not only to Japan but to other nations which must be vitally concerned with the naval strategy of the Pacific area and the growing military-economic dependence of Japan on China. As little as the value of China's total exports of all commodities to Japan in 1940 was (£2,072,000 as calculated by the Oriental Economist), and as little as is the percentage of exports to Japan from China, 6.4 percent in 1940, the Shanghai power situation and the 60 percent increase in coal exports in 1940 are sufficient to indicate that Japan is placing the greatest importance on China as a source for its present needs of coal, the demand for which is obviously related to the armament industries.

The same is true as regards salt from China. With the supply of salt from North Africa a difficult problem, Japan has had to look to the West Indies for salt. In December 1940 the freight rate on West Indian salt was quoted by the JOURNAL OF COMMERCE (New York) at $16 U.S. per ton. In February 1941 the freight rate had risen to $22. It is hardly to be wondered then that figures for the exportation of salt from China to Japan have shown a similarly extraordinary increase as was shown in the case of coal; in fact the increase was more remarkable, as the following figures show:

The increase over 1939 was 347,744 tons, or 107.6 percent of the 1939 exports of salt from China to Japan.

And just as the Japanese Government in its inexorable demand for coal from China for its armament program shows itself ready to sacrifice even the important interests of its own nationals in Shanghai, so does that Government exhibit a callousness as regards the Chinese people, for in the CHRISTIAN SCIENCE MONITOR of February 14, 1941, in an account of conditions at Hankow appeared the following:

"Officially the sales price for salt, an important necessity in the Chinese diet, is set at 40 cents in Japanese military notes and 60 cents in Chinese national currency. But the secret and actual price is much higher, a catty (1 1/2 pounds) of salt costing well over a dollar--and even then it is difficult to obtain. The Chinese investigator records that many people near Hankow said they had been without salt for days."

This callous, ruthless and inexorable policy of the Japanese Government must be considered when the strategy and economy of the Pacific are studied in relation to American, Chinese and Japanese interests. Opinions may differ as to the standard of success for the Japanese, but the trend should not be ignored.

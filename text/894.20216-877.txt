San Salvador.

Sir:

The Department has received information from various sources that individuals of the Japanese race have in recent months been engaged in what appear to be topographical surveys along the Pacific and Caribbean coasts of certain Latin American countries. In some cases such surveys appear to have been undertaken by individuals alleged to have represented themselves as fishermen.

At the request of the Director of Naval Intelligence, the Department desires you to investigate discreetly and report promptly upon these and such similar activities as may come to your notice. Comments are desired as to the appearance of any Japanese fishermen and their vessels: do they seem to be bona fide fishermen, or are they more highly educated men in disguise? Are the vessels equipped with survey, sounding, or other scientific instruments?

You

The Honorable

Sir:

I have the honor to inform the Department that the local newspaper LA PRENSA, in its edition of August 7, 1935, gives prominence on its first page to an article entitled "Japanese Spies Were Here a Few Days Ago". The main body of the story consists of an apparently verbatim quotation of an article which appeared "recently" in the newspaper LA TRIBUNA of San José de Costa Rica. This original article reported the departure from Tegucigalpa for San Salvador and

and Mexico on June 2 of two Japanese named S. Mijuo and T. Furuta. They had spent three weeks previously at Amapala, Honduras, selling Japanese and Mexican goods (one was said to be established in business in Mexico City), but spending a remarkable amount of their time and money in excursions on the Gulf of Fonseca in a gasoline launch. These excursions had taken them to Tempisque on the Nicaraguan shore, and to La Unión, El Salvador, as well as up various estuaries on the Honduran coast. Furuta said he was a botanist, in search of medicinal plants, and took copious notes. A week after they had left Honduras by plane for the North, two Americans, who were said to be members of the Government Secret Service, arrived in Tegucigalpa by Pan-American Airways from David, Panama, and proceeded to Amapala to investigate the actions of the Japanese. They later continued by air to Guatemala, and presumably to the United States. LA TRIBUNA then went on to report that it was practically certain that the two Japanese were spies of the Ministry of War in Tokyo. One of them had been operating in the Philippines in 1931, until the nature of his activities were discovered by the American authorities. In April they spent some time at David, then a fortnight in Costa Rica, and for a time returned to Panama, visiting various bays and estuaries along the coast. Their American pursuers had look for them fruitlessly in Panama City, Colon, and Buenaventura, Colombia, before coming on their trail at David. In conclusion, LA TRIBUNA announced its apprehension that Costa Rica was falling into the meshes of a network of international espionage, probably because of its

The article states that two vessels would be sent, and that they would operate chiefly along the Eastern end of the
country's

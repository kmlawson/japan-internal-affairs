# Report on the Police Organization of Japan.

The police power in Japan is, like most governmental functions here, concentrated in the hands of the central government. The police are all administered from Tokyo and no chiefs of local governments or local governments themselves exercise independent control over them except in a very limited degree. The police are administered in the name of the Emperor by the Minister of Home Affairs through the Superintendent General (Chief of the Metropolitan Police Board) in Tokyo prefecture and the governors of the other prefectures and of Hokkaido. The chief of the Metropolitan Police Board in Tokyo takes his orders direct from the Home Minister as the Board has many political responsibilities unknown in other prefectures. He thus occupies a position in relation to the police organization of Tokyo prefecture which in the other prefectures falls to the governors and the importance of his office is indicated in the fact that he, like prefectural governors, is an officer of "chokunin" rank. In Hokkaido and all the prefectures except Tokyo the highest police officer is the Chief of Police Division, an officer of "sonin" rank who is subordinate to the governor.

Under the Chiefs of Police Division are the police superintendents, who are officers of "sonin" rank, (which corresponds to our "commissioned officer") and inspectors, assistant inspectors and ordinary policemen, who are all of "hansin" rank (which corresponds to our "warrant officer" or sub-commissioned personnel). A police superintendent is

is appointed chief of a police station or secretary of a Police Division and in Tokyo and Osaka prefectures he may be appointed inspector over several stations. A police inspector or assistant police inspector may be appointed chief or secretary of a police station. Policemen are divided into sergeants, indoor and outdoor service men, special service men and police detectives.

The scope of police functions in Japan is unusually large. The police, in fact, tend to absorb the great bulk of administrative and minor judicial functions which in other countries are performed by other governmental agencies.

In the performance of their judicial functions, police courts, e.i., police officers, have summary jurisdiction over offenses classified as police offenses, involving not more than twenty days' detention or fines not exceeding twenty yen. The offenses listed as cognizable by the police are many, among them vagrancy, prostitution, intimidation, mendicancy, obstruction of traffic, indecent display, and maltreatment of animals.

In their relations with other courts, the police act under the direction of the public procurators executing warrants of arrest or detention and arresting persons in flagrant offense. They may seize private possessions or search a house by order of a court of justice, an examining judge or a public procurator, or help a procurator in the investigation of criminal cases.

Individual policemen wear sabres. Pistols are carried only in special cases though in the police force there are troops of armed constables. If matters become

The Higher Police Training School is located in Tokyo and is attached to the Department of Home Affairs under the direct control of the Minister. As police officers of the grade of inspector and assistant inspector in the fire brigade service are merged into the police organization, there are no separate training facilities for them, and they, together with officers engaged purely in police business, attend this same school.

Officers who are to study in the Higher Police Training School are selected upon the recommendation of their superiors. The length of the course is one year and officers from all the prefectures are admitted; 17 are chosen annually from the Metropolitan Police Board in Tokyo, 4 from Osaka prefecture, 3 from Kyoto prefecture and one or two from each of the other prefectures.

The curriculum includes study of the Constitution, Criminal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure, Administrative Law, police regulations, police drills and lectures on various administrative and legal matters which will be of value to the officers. The teaching staff consists of four professors, two assistant professors and a varying number of advisors and lecturers, including professors from the Imperial University and from technical schools.

Not only is there no other such school in Japan, but police officers who have not been graduated from it may not be appointed police superintendents regardless of whatever other educational qualifications they may possess.

The Police training schools for the lower grade police are attached to and are under the direction of the Metropolitan Police Board in Tokyo and the police division of the prefectural governments throughout the country.

The great majority of those attending this school are young men who have passed their entrance examination and are entering upon their police service. The entrance examination consists of a physical examination and an examination on the following subjects: arithmetic, Japanese composition and reading and interpretation of selections from the criminal law and regulations. This examination is reported to be relatively simple and so designed that persons who have completed the higher elementary schools should be able to pass.

The curriculum of the training schools consists of the following subjects: Japanese Constitution, Administrative Law, Criminal Law and Law of Criminal Procedure, but special emphasis is laid upon the study of the Police Regulations, and training in Japanese wrestling (judo), fencing and police drills (including salutes, official etiquette, etc.) A number of the above-mentioned subjects are likewise included in the curriculum of the Higher Police Training Institute, but these given in these schools are

and of course treated in a more elementary manner.

Instruction is given by experienced higher police officers, there being no professional instructors on the teaching staff. The term of training is four months at the end of which there is a final examination and those who pass become ordinary policemen. Those who fail in the final examination on their first attempt are permitted to take the course once more and are then graduated at the completion of the next term if they pass the final examination.

The number of candidates who are admitted to the Police Training Schools is not fixed and varies among the several prefectures but in Tokyo the number is approximately seventy or eighty.

Volunteer civilian firemen, acting under police direction, constitute the large majority of the personnel of the fire brigade service in Japan. In Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, Kanagawa, Hyogo and Aichi prefectures, however, fire stations exist separate from the local police organization, employing full time professional firemen. Even in these prefectures, however, the volunteer brigades supplement the work of the professional firemen.

In all prefectures the training of the fire brigades is under the direction of the police division of the prefectures (Metropolitan Police Board in Tokyo). In certain prefectures regular firemen training schools are conducted for training professional firemen but the training of the volunteer corps is conducted under police supervision and consists principally of fire drills for the local volunteer firemen.

# DIRECTIVE'S COMMENTS
## POLICEMEN INDICTED

Three Detectives and Chief Charged With Torturing Election Suspects

## ATTORNEYS BRING ACTION

Reports Suppressed Because of Dissatisfaction Felt Among Associates

As indignation over the matter among associates in the police service had subsided, the ban was lifted last evening on news of the indictment in July and August of the chief of the Uraga police, Kanagawa Prefecture, and three detectives on charges of having tortured men suspected of violating the election law.

On March 12, Mr. Katsutaro Takaki, 44, head of the fire brigade and village assemblyman of Kita Shimoura, Maura-gun, Kanagawa Prefecture, committed suicide by leaping from a room on the third floor of the Yokohama District Court. This moved members of the Tokyo Bar Association to take action with regard to the alleged torture of more than 40 men suspected of having violated the election law in connection with the candidacy of Mr. Yoshihisa Kawaguchi, of the Seiyukai, Domei reports.

To investigate the charges made by the lawyers, Procurator Ijasku Takehira of the Tokyo Court of Appeal went to Kanagawa Prefecture. As he was transferred to Himeji, Procurator Yumeizure Murakami, of the same court, considered his work. As a result of the inquiry, steps were taken on June 6 to detain Eizo Namekawa, detective of the Kanagawa prefectural police, Tokusai Shimoda, detective of the Uraga police station, and Yutaka Kori, detective of the Misaki police station. Shizuma Miyakawa, Uraga police chief, was summoned on July 21. The detectives were formally indicted on July 15 on charges of violence and outrage, and the police chief was billiard August 22 on the same charges.

## Lawyers Make Claims

The members of the Tokyo Bar Association who brought the charges made the following claims, according to the 25th's agency:

1. The officers charged made a police map at the village substation bring Mr. Wakamatsu Iwasaki, of Kita Shimoura, to the Uraga police station on March 4. He was left in the station until about 3 o'clock at night without being questioned, and then he was surrounded by two detectives who said they were from the prefectural police headquarters, the chief of police and several other detectives.

The man was made to squat on a mat of rags and planks. Back of his knees, the officers inserted a long piece of wood, and he was forced to hold up by his hands an inverted chair on which was piled about 40 pounds of fencing equipment.

"If you cannot stand the pain," he was told, "say that you received money (in connection with the election)." Then began what is known as the tickling torture. The detectives made Mr. Iwasaki lay with his face to the floor. One sat on his back and raised his head by pulling his hair. At the same time, other detectives, one on each side, tickled one rib after another, repeating this many times. Iwasaki eventually frothed at the mouth and vomited blood. As he still refused to yield, the detectives started to puff tobacco and incense smoke into his nostrils and mouth. As he was not a smoker, the agony was beyond description.

The police repeated this process the next day, and Mr. Iwasaki, thinking that otherwise he would be killed, said he had taken money.

# Police Case Disgraceful

## NICHI NICHI

In this enlightened age, the recent infringement of personal rights by police in Kanagawa Prefecture was disgraceful. Nobody can hear such news without being shocked. It has special bearings on public interests, moreover, as it originated in the situation following the campaign for election purification, which the public has been harshly criticizing as really aimed at withering the public mind. The issue was raised in the 69th Diet session, many members citing infringements of personal rights and undue election control by the police. Some of the persons tortured by the police, it was claimed, committed suicide because unable to bear the third-degree methods. The public at first doubted these stories, but the judicial authorities have found them to be true.

Some sympathy should be felt, however, for policemen indicted on the charge of infringing personal rights in connection with the election reform campaign. They were highly dissatisfied with the predicament in which they were placed by the Government's instructions. The election reform campaign must have been based on the self-satisfying policy of the bureaucrats, and this the public should reject. It is because of this disgusting policy of the bureaucrats that the deplorable Kanagawa police incident has been exposed. Justice Minister Raizaburo Hayashi has been advocating leniency in dealing with suspects, but his view is not shared in practice by the procurators at large.

## Bureaucrats Go Too Far

## YOMIURI

The startling revelation of infringement of personal rights by policemen in Kanagawa Prefecture has attracted public attention. A police chief and three detectives tortured in an inconceivable manner men suspected of having violated the election law, and because of this they are to be placed on trial. Like medicine, power cures illness but sometimes brings death. The recent attitude of bureaucrats in exercising authority is highly deplorable. Taking advantage of the background support of the military, they go too far in using their power.

As the scandals involving two former Railway Ministers, Mr. Heikichi Ogawa and Mr. Shoya Uchida, and Lieutenant-General Haruhiku Uemura, chief of the army arsenal bureau, have shown, high officials of the Government have often abused their authority. They are also trying to trespass on national rights and freedom in enforcing control of the electric power industry. The Kanagawa police incident is an instance of abuse of authority by officials of low rank.

The existence of police tortures is an open secret. Leaders in social movements are subjected to tortures far worse than those revealed in the Kanagawa affair. Public criticism has been lacking because the men involved are without much influence. Under no circumstance is torture justified. If the police maintain that election reforms cannot be achieved without strict punishment, the public must reject such reforms. We are absolutely against torture under a constitutional regime.

# READERS IN COUNCIL

## Japanese Policemen and Foreign Visitors

To the Editor:

In the interesting interview which you published yesterday Mr. Ogawa, chief of the Foreign Section of the Metropolitan Police, discussed some of the causes of the friction which occasionally develops between foreign visitors and the police. According to Mr. Ogawa that friction is due to the foreigner failing to understand the reasons for the inquisitions to which he is subjected. But one wonders if the police themselves clearly understand those reasons.

He says the police are puzzled by the fuss that tourists make over the examinations at the ports. The foreigner is equally puzzled to understand why such investigations should be thought necessary. Every traveler landing in a Japanese port comes with a vise furnished by a Japanese consul and duly paid for. He carries a passport containing all the information that is legally required. Why, then, is it necessary to subject him to a long interrogation at the port? And why, after he has answered all the questions at Kobe, should he be required to answer another series at Yokohama? Might not one such examination be regarded as sufficient and the traveler, once scrutinised and admitted, left alone to enjoy himself?

Tourists, it seems, are sometimes aged 32 at one inn and 49 at another, which Mr. Ogawa thinks such a poor joke that he sends a policeman to admonish them and perhaps fine them. Is not this an example of making mountains out of molehills? What does the exact age of an adult foreigner matter to the police? If they like to keep that unimportant information to themselves why not let them?

Much of the friction is caused by the fact that officers who question passengers do not confine themselves to the particulars the law requires, all of which (including age) are stated on the passport. They enter into rambling interrogatories about his past life or his future intentions or his political opinions and the tourist does not understand such peculiar questions. He thinks he is being treated as a suspicious character and he becomes angry—which is the worst thing he can do since it often leads to the "problems" which Mr. Ogawa regrets. When tourists are told they need not be alarmed at such questioning. It might be remembered that there have been several cases recently in which foreigners have had painful experience of the "third degree" methods which have been censured not by foreign critics alone but by Japanese judges from the bench.

Japan is preparing to invite all the world to her shores. The visitors should take back impressions of a lovely land and a kindly people. Too often that impression is spoiled by the alarm and irritation caused by questioning which seems to show that the visitor is regarded as a suspected person, or a Communist or a spy. Of course, if the visitor has the patience which Mr. Ogawa recommends he will have no trouble except some irritation and loss of time. But why irritate him? Why waste his time? What do the police get in return for all the trouble they take and give? Have they ever caught a spy? Have they ever confiscated a photograph for which any foreign Government would give five sen? I suggest that if these questions were honestly asked and answered much of the police energy now wasted on catechising visitors would be released for its legitimate work of repressing crime.

HUGH BYAS.

Tokyo, October 31, 1936.

The Japan Bar Association sent two of its representatives to investigate the complaints of more than 100 residents of Samukawa village against the tortures of the Fujisawa branch of the Kanagawa police. In the past there have been many such charges and some were successfully proven but others failed because of the lack of evidence. However when an entire village complaints about torture methods used by the police there is certainly reason for it. Even though the evidence may be cleverly concealed, and even though the Bar Association may not find sufficient evidence to bring suit against the police, the complaint of a hundred villagers is good reason for warning the authorities against such disgraceful measures made possible because of the authority they possess and the difficulty that the average citizen has in making a case against them.

JAPAN TIMES - December 6, 1936.

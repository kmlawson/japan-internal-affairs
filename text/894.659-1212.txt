in the past several years along with the recent prosperity of

pharmaceuticals, 10 per cent for soap, 8 por cent for magnesium
cartonate and the balance for dyeing and other miscellaneous
purposes.
The weakest point of the local soda industry is undoubtedly
its extreme dependence on the foreign supply of industrial salt.
Following the outbreak of the China Affair the difficulties in
importing industrial salt from Africa and the Near East became
considerably agravated due to the import control by the Govern-
ment and the shortage of bottoms, and the import cost of foreign
salt and accordingly the production cost of soda were boosted
seriously. Inasmuch as cauctic soda is indispensable for the

THE JAPANESE CHEMICAL INDUSTRY
1. The Japanese have a large and well-equipped heavy
chemical industry which has under-gone a remarkable
expansion in the ten years commencing 1925.
2. The basic industries of alkali and nitrogen are large
and adequate for Japanese requirements. Both suffer
from shortages or potential shortages of the main raw
materials that they require - salt and pyrites respect-
ively. Japanese controlled salt production is, however,
equivalent to at least 55% of the pesk alkal1 production
roached in l938 and at this rate, it 1s not thought
Japanese war industries will suffer.
Pyrites is widespread over Japan and in recent years
smaller and less profitable mines have been brought into
production. A pyrites shortage sufficient to affect
sulphate of ammonia for fertilisers is not, therefore,
anticipated, particularly as a arop in the demand for
sulphuric acid for rayon and super-phosphate is expected.
3. The main by-product of the electrolytic alkali industry
is chlorine and on suitable measures for its utilisation
the electrolytic alkali production deponds. The Japanese
disposed of most of their chlorine as bleaching powder,
but a proportion 1s used as liquid chlorine and for the
manufacture of hydro-chloric acid. No figures for the
production of these chemicals are available, but whereas
there would not appear, theoretically, at any rate, any
limiting factor in Japan to the production of hydrochlorio
acid from the available chlorine, lack of suitable
cylinders has proved a limitation on liquid chlorine
production. It is not known to what extent the Japanese
have been able to remedy this position. The following
were manufacturera of liquid chlorine:-

of sulphate of ammonia, and the source of hydrogen which
initially was derived almost entirely f rom the electrol-

seasonal variation in hydro-electric power output has
been the cause of this development. In fact, Japanese
nitrogen plants have never produced up to reputed
capacity, probably largely due to these hydro-eleotric
power supply fluctuations.

April 11, 1940.

Tokyo's no. 4595 of March 15, 1940 reports the suppression by the Japanese Home Office of an issue of a Japanese language magazine containing an article by a Foreign Office official which deals critically with Japanese thought in regard to settlement of the "China Incident". Subsequently, the official was forced to resign his position. He notes that while the Japanese speak of the hostilities as an "Incident" the Chinese speak of "long-term resistance". If the hostilities are an "Incident" and not a war, it follows that Japan has no enemy and that the natural outcome should be a restoration of the status quo ante, but the China affair, he says, is a war unprecedented in Japanese history and the prize to be gained should be one of corresponding magnitude.

He argues that in disposing of the conflict in China there must be ability to conciliate as well as power. Wang Ching-wei has never possessed real power and China has never been conquered through the device of combining with powerless men.

While the Asiatic continent is a convenient reservoir into which the Japanese race may overflow, peace within every corner of its confines is not essential to Japanese peace and security. On the contrary, an attempt to maintain such a peace may endanger the very existence of Japan itself. According to the writer, "Asia for the Asiatics" and the "new order in East Asia" are only a snare and a delusion. Why should Japan fight for such ideals which would be repudiated by China's millions? Doubt is expressed whether Japan has enough reserve to give thought to the other races in Asia.

The writer concludes with the warning: "Should we make enemies out of Europe and the United States? Isn't our enemy closer at hand? There must be some people who can answer these questions."

The Embassy regards the article as important as showing a crystallization after long confusion of thought in certain Japanese quarters with regard to the outcome of the conflict and as speaking eloquently of the struggle between different elements in the Government as to policies to be pursued. The Embassy believes that although outwardly the "new order" will be maintained, there may expand and take shape a realization that the

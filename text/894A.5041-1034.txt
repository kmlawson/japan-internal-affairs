Time Period Covered By the Figures.

The figures presented in this report are the latest available, according to officials of the Taiwan Government General.

The figures for manufacturing industries are average figures for the first half of 1930 in Taihoku, Koolung, Shinshiku, Taioku, Tainan, Kagi, Yakuo, Taite, Karonko, and Makao.

The figures on mining are averages for the whole of Taiwan for 1929.

The figures for forestry are the average for each province and prefecture for the latter half of 1929.

The figures for agriculture are taken from various official records of 1929, and, where available, from records for the first half of 1930.

In various interviews with government officials, the question was repeatedly asked as to what changes, if any, have taken place in the local wage scales since 1929 and 1930. The answer was invariably that very few, and slight, changes have occurred, and that any revision in the scale has been downward.

Unemployment: Anticipated Effect of Resumption of Work on the Disinfectants Hydro-electric Policies.

Did have already been let for resumption of work on the Jitsugatsutan hydro-electric project. Actual operations are expected to commence late in October, 1931.

The renewal of this enterprise, after a lapse in activity of nearly ten years, is expected quickly to wipe out all unemployment in the island.

Unemployment has not been a serious problem in Taiwan. In September 1930, there were approximately 5,000 unemployed, distributed as follows:

|  Clerk and salaried classes | 1,000  |
| --- | --- |
|  Unskilled laborers | 2,000  |
|  Skilled laborers | 2,000  |
|  Total | 5,000  |

These figures had increased about 20% by September, 1931.

Every effort is to be made by employers to continue wages in general throughout Taiwan at their present low level, but the current opinion is that when circumstances bring about the absence of all conditions of unemployment, labor will be in a position to demand a higher scale of pay, and that the demands, when made, will probably be successful.

Unskilled labor is not to be imported into Taiwan. The possibility of the need for imported labor with which to continue work on the project was seriously discussed before the development of the existing unsettled

=== Page 12 === (confidence 1.00)
-10-

unsettled relations between Japan and China. At that time, the question resolved itself into two definite angles: Chinese labor could be imported at lower wages than the prevailing scale in Taiwan; this would further the economic completion of the project, but would react adversely on the interests of the general public of Taiwan; or labor could be imported from Japan, necessarily at a wage scale higher than local standards; this might unbalance conditions from another angle. The conclusion which has now been reached, is that no unskilled labor is to be imported, all labor requirements will be filled as completely as possible from the local mart, and only certain skilled labor, locally unobtainable, will be imported, and that will come from Japan.

Confidential.

Source of Information.

It is felt that the data contained in this report is not as complete as might be desired by the Department of Labor.

Vice Consul Charles G. Reed II, in his confidential despatch No. 45 of September 12, 1930, covered in detail the difficulties frequently encountered in obtaining information concerning the industries of Taiwan, and he explained the necessity for obtaining them through government channels.

Upon receipt of the Department's instruction of August 17, 1931, File No. 600.5041/35, calling for this report, a memorandum was prepared containing such questions as would cover the requirements of the survey. This was presented to Dr. Haraguchi, Chief of the Encyclopedic Division of the Taiwan Government General, during an interview with him.

Dr. Haraguchi, who has been exceptionally friendly to this office and who has on past occasions supplied valuable information which might not have been available through regular channels, agreeably complied with the request, and a few days later submitted a report which, he stated, encompassed the situation in so far as pertinent material was available to his office. The Encyclopedic Division, which he heads, collects, compiles, and officially records various statistics for the Taiwan Government General.

Further

Further information was sought, but without avail, from the Chief of the Social Bureau of the Taiwan Government General.

An interview was next held with the Chief of the Peace Preservation Division, in the Federal Police Bureau. This official made available brief data concerning unemployment in Taiwan.

This office is therefore of the opinion that the report herewith submitted is as complete as is feasible under existing local conditions, and that, unless certain additional information is urgently required, no attempt should be made to force its enlargement.

In addition, it may be observed that since the labor question in Taiwan has never been a serious issue, it is quite possible that no extensive data on the subject has been collected.

The scale of wages in Asian varies considerably in the various cities of the island for the same kind of labor, this being due to the small scale of industry in general; but when there are large numbers of workers employed in any one industry, the differentiation in wages paid in each city is small.

Formosan Chinese furnish the vast majority of the labor in Taiwan; Japanese being more generally employed as foremen or as skilled labor. This accounts for the difference in wage scales of between Japanese and Formosan Chinese; the former average from 80 per cent to 100 per cent more than the foremen in the same trade.

Formosan women usually receive about half as much as the men.

There are few large factories in Taiwan, factories often having but 6 or 8 workers. Hours of labor average about 10 per day - including Sundays, although most employees get two days off in a month.

There are no labor laws or Workman's compensation laws in Taiwan as there are in Japan. Neither are there any organized unions recognized, such as exist in Japan. The Government General does not encourage unionization of labor.

Excepting in the case of government laborers, as in the

=== Page 19 === (confidence 0.92)
-3-

the Monopoly Bureau or the Taiwan Government Railways,
there are no provisions for bonuses or overtime payment
of labor, but most Japanese employers follow the Government
example and pay labor for overtime work.

The government provides free housing for certain classes
of government laborers, but this does not exist in private
industry.

The government also has a scheme for social insurance
contributions of government employees, and retiree long time
employees on small pensions. Private industry makes no
such provisions.

If a laborer is injured on the job, it is customary
for the employer to pay no. 111 ex ensues, and if killed or
totally disabled to make a money contribution, called consolation
money, to his family or to the injured man.

Thirty days notice is usual in the working employees,
in lieu of notice, 20 days wages is generally paid.

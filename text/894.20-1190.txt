NAGASAKI CONSULAR DISTRICT
SUMMARY
There is taking place considerable activity in the
Nagasaki Consular District in the direction of mobiliza-
tion for national defense. The activities are particularly
marked along the following lines:
894.
I. The development of munitions industries
and the accumulation of military and naval stores;

894.

people "defense conscious";

movements of foreigners.
I. INDUSTRIAL LEVELOPMENT WHICI HAS TO DO WITH IMPERIAL
JAPANESE DEFENSE
The ports of Tokuyama and Kudamatsu, Yamaguchi Ken,
are used extensively as storehouses for naval supplies,
particularly oil. Many oil tankers, Japanese naval as
well as foreign, carry this commodity to these ports.
The port of Moji, which includes Shimonoseki on the
Hondo shore, is fortified, defense regulations are strictly
enforced, and not only is sketching and photography forbidden,
but foreigners have come to find recently that their
business, their hikes, their hunting, and all their outdoor
F/HR
activities are curtailed by, and subjected to careful
sorutiny on the part of, Japanese police and gendarmerie

stored in mountainous heans along the shores of Moji,
Tobata, Kokura, Yawata, and Hakata. Many of these heaps
are said to be covered with earth or sand.
The Yawata-Kokura region is the scene of great

equipment are manufactured to the exclusion of ordinary
commercial business, and that profits on defense

maximum.

II. METHODS OF BUILL JNG NATICLAL LEFEIHSE SOL ARITY
Six years of careful control of nress articles have
enabled those entrusted with the national defense to build
up, among the avid-reading Jananese public, an enviable
solidarity conducive to the defense program. Daily military
training in educational institutions above primary grade,
nightly drills by the personnel of Young Men's Associations,
sporadic and well-planned air-raid defense manoeuvres with
the extinguishing of every light, the billeting of enlisted-
and officer-personnel in private homes in port cities like
Nagasaki for a couple of days before defense troops are
seen off to the accompaniment of booming guns and
screeching sirens (the vessels proceeding to "unlmown"
destinations), and the attendance of school, precinct,
police, and other representatives at ports and railway
stations upon the arrival of the ashes of heroes who have
died in the Imperial defense, the solemn procession to
the shrine or burial plot, the interment of the remains,
and the deification of the heroic spirits have developed
in the public a strong individual as well as group sense
of

III. MANIFESTATIONS OF AI TI-FOREIGN SENTIMENT
The manifestations of genuine Japanese interest in
and fellowship with other nationals, which followed the
World War, are believed to be fast becoming manifestations,
on the part of the general public, of widespread suspicion
and distrust. School-teachers, missionaries, tourists,
and business men report their activities the subject of
official scrutiny, meddling, and intclerance to the point
where their very presence sometimes seems obnoxious to the
populace, and further state that the program and purpose
of the individual often ends in utter futility. It is
understood that all American and Canadian missionaries
have found it necessary to cease active work in Loo Choo,
and that all male Canadian and American missionaries have
found their physical presence in the southern prefectures
of Kyushu such a detriment to the cause of the Church
in those prefectures that they have withdrawn therefrom.
Both American and Canadian women remain, but are subjected
to handicaps hitherto unexperienced in work and life.
The arrest, confinement, and "silly" quizzing of a
party of British-naval-enlisted-personnel bathers from
the aircraft carrier HERMES (1936) caused much discomfiture
as well as amusement in the foreign community at Nagasaki,

IV. SOURCE AND MEANS CF ANTI-FOREIGN-ISM
So far as has been ascertained the central source
of the anti-foreign-ism manifested in the lagasaki consular
district has been in the office and person of the
commandant of the gendarmerie whose headquarters are at
Kumamoto, Japan. This individual is said to be personally
animated by a fiery patriotism which manifests itself in
anti-foreign feeling. It is reported that his staff
supports him in his efforts to banish fron his country
all foreign influence.

1. Coordination of National Defense (See Chart, page 2).
a. Armed Forces. The armed forces of Japan consist of an army and a
navy, each with its own air corps component.
b. Supreme Commander. The mperor is the supreme commander of the
armed forces.
c. Superior Councils of National Defense. There are three superior
bodies which advise the mperor on national defense ratters and coordinate
the activities of the armed forces.
(1) The Board of Marshels and Admirals was created in 1898 as the
mperor's highest advisory body on military and naval affairs. It comprises,
to-day, two field marshals and one fleet admiral -- all imperial princes.
This board, as such, exercises little or no influence on important military
and naval matters, but concerns itself for the most part with the granting
of awards and decorations. Its personnel exercises authority by virtue of
membership in the larger igh Military Council.
(2) The High Military Council was created in 1887. Its membership
includes that of the Board of Marshals and Admirals, the Ministers of ar
and Navy, the Chiefs of the Army and Navy General Staffs, and certain dis-
tinguished officers of the army and navy appointed by the Emperor. There
are at present 16 members, nine from the army and seven from the navy. The
function of this council is to advise the Emperor on mstters pertaining to
national defense. The council is independent of contr: by the civilian
branches of the government and possesses tremendous preo:ige and power. It
can make or break any cabinet. It normally determines ti, nation's military
and naval policies but frequently has the predominating  fluence in deter-
mining national policy.
(3) Imperial Headquarters, first created in 1894, mes into being
on the Emperor's order in time of war or similar emergency. Its organization
includes the Emperor, as Comnander in Chief, the Chiefs of the Army and Navy
General Staffs, the Ministers of Jar and Navy and such cabinet officers and
other officials as the mpercr may appoint. The function of this headquarters
is to coordinate the efforts of the army and navy and to advise and assist
the Emperor in the exercise of supreme command of the armed forces.

e. Services.
(1) Chemical Warfare Service. In July. 1937 no separate chemical
warfare service had been organized in the Japanese Army. A modern chemical
warfare research establishment was in existence, however, staffed and
operated by some 80 officers, 50 enlisted men, and 60 civilians. There
were also a chemical warfaro manufacturing plant, and a chemical warfare
school for the training of selected officers of all arms except military
police. Graduates of this school gave chemical warfare training through-
out the army. A gas mask was part of the individual equipment of combat
troops.
According to reliable reports the Japanese, during the course
of the war in China, from time to time, and on various fronts have used
tear and sneeze gas against the Chinese. The method of projection is un-
known.
In January, 1939 a small chemical corps had come into exist-
ence in the Japanese army. Its organization and equipment are as yet un-
known.

7. Theory of Combat.
a. Conception of the Conduct of War. The national objective being to
secure and maintain hegemony over Eastern Asia, particularly Northeastern
Asia, the mission of the arned forces is to prevent the organization in or
penetration into this area of any hostile force capable of challenging
Japan's domination.
Careful coordination of the maximum military, naval, and civilian
resources of the nation are essential to the successful conduct of war.
The mission of the navy as the first line of offense or defense is
to prevent the penetration into Japan's sphere of control or predominant
influence of a hostile force by water or by air, when naval aviation can
be employed, and to control and keep open water routes essential to attain-
ment of the naticnal objective.
The mission of the army is to destroy or drive out any hostile air

Of the principles of war the offensive, movement, and surprise are

Infantry is the principal aim. Supported and assisted by the others
it obtains the final decision by neans of the assault with the bayonet - the

Artillery,from positions close behind the infantry, supports it by
fire and by the moral effect of its presence.
Air Corps. The air corps performs distant reconnaissance; inde-

and against works and utilities; und in cooperation with the infantry and

Cavalry. Cavalry's main roles are reconnaissance and security. Ad-

Engineers assist the other arms with demolitions, construction of
works and routes of transportation, and maintenance of communications. They
fight, when necessary, as infantry.
c. Method of Conducting Combat. The Japanese Army is organized, trained
and eq ipped for combat on the mainland of Asia acainst Chinese or Russian
forces or a combination of the two.
A war of movement in which the Japanese forces will gain and retain
the advantage of the offensive is the basic concept.
Japanese tactical doctrine recognizes three principles of war - the
offensive, movement, and surprise. It slights or ignores the principles of
the objective, mass, economy of force, security, simplicity and cooperation.
On the offensive care in plannine an operation is often sacrificed
to speed in execution. Night marches end attacks are the rule rather than
the exception.
Training in defensive werfere is perfunctory. Little or no pro-
vision is made for proper utilization of defensive fires. Plans for im-
mediate counterattacks are made by all units. Intrenched troops are
trained to meet the enemy's final assault, not with a concentration of all
remaining fire power, but with a bayonet attack delivered in front of the

As previously reported, the stated purpose of the law
is to preclude the divulging to foreign countries of informa-
tion concerning personnel and material resources which are
now being used or may hereafter be used for military purposes.
The substance of the law is contained in Article 2, the 15
paragraphs of which cive in detail a wide category of types
of information, the divulging of which is prohibited. This
range is so broad that it might well be interpreted to cover
almost every form of economic activity. The preaable of
Article 2 provides that the Defense Mini ters are empower-
ed to specify what articles ere considered to fall within
the scope of the law. The present law does not supplant
the likewise important Military Jecrets Law which was re-
vised shortly after the outbreak of the h stilities in China
but is complementary to it. The provisions of the Military
Secrets Law deal, it will be recalled, only with military
affairs in the narrow s nse of the word. Consequently
the need was felt, and presunably has now been fulfilled,
for a law which would prevent there falling into the hands
of foreign Powers information not strictly military in
character concerning personnel and material resources of the
nation. The law is, in effect, a recognition of the fact
that the successful prosecution of a modern war depends upon
every element in the State, thet the civilian elements are
no less important than "the men at the front".
The present legislation may also be looked upon as
a logical development of the national mobilization move-
ment and, in fact, it does closely resemble Article 44,
not

=== Page 85 === (confidence 0.99)
-3-
not yet invoked, of the National General Mobilization Law.
Hitherto, control over industrial espionage of a general
character has been exercised somewhat haphazardly through
ordinary administrative channels, either as the result of
prefectural order or at the independent direction of the
gendarmerie (Kempei). While the actual task of enforcing
the new law will continue to be the work of the civil police,
for matters coming under the jurisdiction of the Home Office,
and the work of the gendarmerie, for matters of particular
military significance, these two enforcing agencies have
now been provided with precise directives, the character of
information which private institutions or individuals are
forbidden to divulge having been clearly defined. To that
extent the new legislation may be welcomed by Japanese
industrialists and businessmen who have been under some
apprehension as to what information they could give out
and what they could not, and who have stood in terror of
the civil as well as the military police.
The punitive provisions of the new law, of which there
are some 16 in number, are relatively light for Japanese
legislation of this character, the maximum penalty for
wittingly divulging military seorets to foreign countries
or to foreign agents or to the general public being im-
prisonment not to exceed ten years. It may also be noted
that the law has no provisions for penalties in cases

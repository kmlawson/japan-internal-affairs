One of the businesses most seriously affected by the present hard times is the picturesque and characteristically Japanese profession of the Geisha. With the increasing popularity of western manners and customs, the geisha have long felt the intrusion of the cafés and dance halls into their province of entertainment. With the coming of the present business depression the numbers of those who can afford to pay the high prices which geisha must charge for their entertainment have become increasingly fewer. Both the geisha and the proprietors of geisha are in a very difficult

=== Page 3 === (confidence 0.99)

difficult situation with very little prospect of relief in sight. It is reported that the actual number of geisha is decreasing and 1931 is expected to see another drop in their number. At the end of 1939, according to data just released by the Home Ministry, there were 80,717 geisha in Japan, of whom 10,485 were in Tokyo. The Tokyo figure is 166 less than at the end of 1929 and it is expected that there will be a greater decrease during 1931. Many of the geisha have been forced to pawn their kimones, which are an important part of their profession, hoping to be able to redeem them upon the return of better times. As better times seem long in returning to the geisha many of them are in want or have been forced to leave the profession.

The suggestion that the President initiate
measures to contribute towards the relief of
the sufferers from the fire at Hakodate is
entirely impracticable in the present circum-
stances.
I have always been somewhat doubtful as
to the wisdom of the American people having
contributed so largely at the time of the
earthquake in 1933. When the so-called Exclu-
sion Aot was passed in 1924, there was serious
talk in Japan of colleoting an equivalent
amount with a view to returning the contribu-
tion of the Amerioan Red Cross. In view of
the present international situation, I am in-
ED
contribution for the relief of the people of
Hakodate would run serious risk of being
refused. In any event such an offer would
be regarded as balm for hurt feelings; and I
think it very important that we do not do
anything to give the impression that the
conscience of this Government is in any way
troubled.

The Typhoon of September 21, 1954.
In the early morning of September 21, 1934 a
typhoon of unequalled violence devastated the port
of Osaka, Japan. A wind which at its apogee reached
a recorded velocity of l35 miles an hour tore vessels
from their moorings, ripped the roofs from storage
sheds, twisted steel cranes as though they were made
of lead pipe and, worst of all, hurled against the
vulnerable port a wall of water fifteen feet higher
than normal flood tide.
Fifteen steamers of more than 1,000 gross tons
were torn from their moorings by the wind and sea, or
else dragged their buoys after them until they were
beached or sank. One large freighter, the Batavia
Maru of 4,392 gross tons, actually dragged her mooring
and its huge mushroom anchor for a distance of a mile
and a half, until she was hurled ashore. Even larger
vessels were beached, while small steamers of 1,500
tons were contemptuously tossed on wha: ordinarily is
dry land. This havoc to shipping resuir. 1 not only
because the typhoon was of unusual severity, but also
in some measure it was due to defects in the construc-
tion of the harbor, which were pointed out in this
Consulate's basic shipping report under reference.
It would appear needless in this supplementary
report to repeat the conolusions given on pages 58 and
59 of the basic survey of March 1, 1934. The mistaken
placing of the entrance to the harbor at the one point
most vulnerable to prevailing wind and sea, the
inadequate protection afforded by the low breakwaters,

Demage to the Port of Osaka.
The principal damage to the port of Osaka was
the loss of the main landing pier or so-called "Great
Pier" (see map frontispiece, Basic Report). This
strueture, which oxtended from the Customs mole into
the inner harbor, was 455 meters in length and 27.3
(1)
meters wide, rising 3.2 meters above flood water. It
was used as the wharf for small passenger vessels in
the coasting trade. After the typhoon the "Great
Pier" had completely disappeared, save for a stump
projecting a hundred feet from the Customs mole.
The modern docks (Nos. 1, 2, 6, 7, 8) withstood
the gale with only nominal damage. The principal
dostruction was caused by the flood water and the
battering action of the debris-laden waves against
the cargo sheds. Material damage was caused by sea
water inundating these storage places. Cranes were in
some cases destroyed by the waves and wind.
Next to the loss of the "Great Pier" the most
sorious destruction was wrought at the Mitsui Bussan
Kaisha ooal docks at Sakurajima. Of the nine gantry
cranes reported in March as pertaining to this huge
bunkering depot, but one was left intact after the
typhoon had passed. The Sakurajima coal piers were
diroctly in the path of the prodigious seas, as the
low breakwaters of the port were utterly ineffectual
in opposing the onrushing waves. The principal cause

Reconstruction.
As pointed out in the basic shipping report under
reference, the City of Osaka has manifested undiminished
zeal for wresting a harbor from an inimical environment
in spite of all obstacles. Although discomfited by
proof that their sea port was not effectively designed,
the municipal authorities undertook immediately the
work of reconstruction and appointed experts to
•laborate sohemes which will in the future prevent or
minimize similar disasters.
On October 8th a joint commission, composed of
representatives of the municipal and prefectural
governments, the Ministries of Home Affairs and
Communications, local warehouse firms, shipping
companies and insurance organizations, met to consider
ways and means of improving the harbor. The principal
itom of the agenda was a project for the construction
of breakwaters which should in the future prevent
inundation of the port by seas sweeping down the length

Roturn to Normal Conditions.
Normal shipping conditions at Osaka were partially
restored during the first week of October. By October
8th there were 65 commercial vessels working cargo or
taking on passengers at the port. On October 17th
normal conditions had practically been regained save
for orowding at the berths for small passenger liners
due to the loss of the "Great Pier", a shortage of
lighters and the wreckage at the Sakurajima coal docks.
The resiliency with which Osaka came back after such a
terrific buffeting is a welcome augury of the future
progress of the port. The lessons taught by the typhoon
of September 2lst were terribly applied and might have
been partially obviated; but they have been learned
and there will result a Port of Osaka more worthy of
the name.

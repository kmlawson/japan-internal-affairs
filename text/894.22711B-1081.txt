The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington.

Sir:

I have the honor to report that one Jose Sotero
Laurel, III, came to the Embassy on April 16, 1935, and
asked the Embassy's assistance to enter the Imperial Military
Academy in Tokyo. He presented a letter from the Acting
Governor General of the Philippine Islands, a copy of which
is enclosed. The young man stated that he desired to study
military science, that there is no opportunity to do so in
the Philippine Islands, that he has been unable to obtain
an appointment to the United States Military Academy and
that

=== Page 2 === (confidence 0.98)
-2-

that he came to Japan to enter the Academy in Tokyo because it is near to his home.

As a rule, Japanese institutions of learning are willing to admit foreign students with a minimum of formality. It seems that the Army, however, admits foreign students to the Military Academy only upon formal request by the representative of a foreign country. In these circumstances, I feel that it would be unwise to initiate action without instructions.

A copy of my letter to the Acting Governor General is enclosed.

Respectfully yours,

Joseph C. Grew.

Manila, March 13, 1935.

Sir:

This letter will be presented by Jose Sotero Laurel, who wishes to enroll in a Japanese school for the study of military science. Embassy of the United States of America.
Tokyo, April 18, 1935.

The Honorable
J. R. Hayden,
Acting Governor General of the Philippine Islands,
Manila.

Sir:

I have the honor to acknowledge the receipt of your letter of March 13, last, in regard to the desire of Jose Sotero Laurel to enter a Japanese school for the study of military science. The letter was brought to the Embassy by the young man in person.

The only military school in Tokyo is the Imperial Military Academy, which corresponds closely to the United States Military Academy at West Point. Admission to this institution, I understand, so far as foreign students are concerned, is obtained only upon formal request by the representative of a foreign country in Japan. Being unable to initiate action on behalf of Mr. Laurel without express authority from Washington, I am referring the matter to the Department of State for instructions.

Very truly yours,

(Signed) Joseph C. Grew.
American Ambassador.

American Consulate,
Taihoku, Taiwan, Japan,
February 28, 1939.

Memorandum Re Mr. Sotero Escarrilla's Attempt to see Managanese Factory in Keelung, Taiwan.

On February 24, 1939, Mr. Sotero Escarrilla, bearer of Philippine passport No. 29838 issued in Manila on February 9, 1939, called at this office to state that he had come to Taihoku at the request of the Pacific Mining Company on behalf of the Taiwan Denka Kaisha to examine the first shipment of manganese ore which his company, the Negros Mining Company, 4th Floor, Wise Building, Manila had sold to the Taiwan Denka Kaisha and which the latter company claimed was not up to specifications. He was accompanied to this office by Mr. Teijiro Kobayashi, Manager of the Keelung plant of the Taiwan Denka Kaisha, who speaks excellent English.

They stated that unsuccessful efforts had been made to have Mr. Escarrilla go to the 12,000 KW. Keelung plant to see the operations in processing the ore, and that the Japanese companies both wished to have Mr. Escarrilla see the plant where manganese, electro ferro-silicon and carbide are made and had invited him here in the expectation that he would be permitted to do so. However the Military authorities in the fortified area of Keelung had effectually refused permission by shunting responsibility from one to the other and Mr. Kobayashi believed it best if the Consulate would see the head military authorities in Taihoku, since nothing more could be done in Keelung. They both stated that examination of part of the ore in question had been made on the wharf at Keelung and believed a settlement of difficulties might be possible without visiting the factory. However, since half of the ore was in the factory and also from the standpoint of seeing just why the ore was not suitable for the machinery used in the factory both parties desired that an inspection be made of the works.

They both requested that I do whatever might be possible to obtain such permission.

On February 25 Mr. Matsuo and I called on Lieutenant-General Kodama Commander-in-Chief of the Taiwan Forces and explained circumstances to him, stating that as far as the Consulate was concerned this was purely a business matter, and one of mutual benefit in that it would enable the companies to have a better understanding of difficulties involved. He stated that he understood the situation perfectly and did not know why permission had been refused to enter the factory but that it may have been either that it was in the class of some factories into which the entry of foreigners is absolutely prohibited, or it might have been in a prohibited area within the fortified

=== Page 12 === (confidence 0.99)
CONFIDENTIAL
-2-

fortified zone. He said that there was nothing he could do about it in any event if regulations were specific on the subject and suggested that we find out from the Keelung authorities on what ground permission had been refused. Lieutenant-General Kodama said that war time brought about some rare and complicated situations for which there was no help, and told of experiences he had had when he was military attaché at the Japanese Embassy in London during the Great War. A Japanese friend who spoke no English had just arrived in London and wishing to make a trip around to various cities had written a timetable and notes about each city in Japanese in his notebook. Unfortunately he lost this and the police found it and called him and his friend in about it and Kodama had to translate the notes which the authorities looked upon as containing military secrets. Kodama then saw a copy of the London Times in the office and pointed out a map of London and articles in the paper which contained many more "military secrets" than his friend's innocuous travel notes, and the whole matter was laughingly dropped. He also told about being asked to enlist as a volunteer in the British Army and he said "If you make me a Brigadier General I will gladly enlist" They asked why and he replied "Because I am already a Major General in the Japanese Army." He said that the authorities then apologized for the error of subordinates in even asking him to enlist in the British Army.

I said that speaking of questions, the police had called me that morning for confirmation of a report that Mr. Escarrilla had dined at the Consulate the night before. I said that such questioning and the constant suspicion with which the Consulate was regarded made it very unpleasant at times in endeavoring to carry out one's duties which are mainly commercial. He laughed and observed that police interference was a product of war conditions. I did not press the point that interference had been going on for years before the China Incident began.

Returning to the Consulate we called Captain Yamahashi in Keelung, in direct charge of the granting of permission to see the factory in question. He said that entry into the factory itself was permissible under regulations, but that the factory was in an area into which the entry of foreigners might be prohibited, but that in view of the circumstances as explained he would reconsider his previous decision and call back. He called back in 15 minutes to say that he had consulted with the Keelung Gendarmerie Commander and they could still see no good reason for permitting Mr. Escarrilla to enter the factory, and permission was therefore denied again.

CONFIDENTIAL
-3-

Since nothing more could be done Saturday, on Monday February 27, I talked with Mr. Escarrilla about the situation and he said that Mr. Kobayashi was making further efforts to have him admitted to the factory, since it was not possible to settle matters satisfactorily unless the factory were visited. He felt that his company would feel his visit had been in vain.

I had Mr. Matsuo telephone to Lieutenant General Kodama's aide Kitao and explain matters as far as they had developed and ask if Lieutenant General Kodama had any suggestions for a solution. He referred Mr. Matsuo to Staff Officer Nakai and Nakai said that while he understood the situation perfectly, decision in the matter rested wholly with the Keelung authorities and there was nothing that could be done here.

Mr. Matsuo again called Captain Yamahashi in Keelung who was quite evasive about saying anything other than that he understood the words which were being spoken and would decide about things by the following day.

The question of bringing the foreign Affairs Department into the picture was dropped as a further useless waste of time, since decision rested with Keelung alone.

Mr. Matsuo telephoned to Captain Yamahashi this morning and was informed, as we expected, that although reconsideration had been given to the question of Mr. Escarrilla's proposed inspection of the factory, such permission could not be granted, and the request was denied for a third time.

At least it cannot be said that opportunity was not given to the army to consider the facts in the situation, and if they later find that they have injured their own interests thereby they will have only themselves to blame.

March 7, 1939

Mr. Escarrilla called at the office this morning to say that the Ganges Maru, originally scheduled to sail from Keelung on March 4 for return to Manila was now probably going on the 8th, and that he would be glad to leave Taiwan, since he had spent most of his time here in police stations being questioned rather than being permitted to attend to his business. For instance, every day upon reaching Keelung although accompanied by a Japanese, he was met at the station by police who took him off to the same police station every day where he was questioned, through an interpreter,

about

=== Page 14 === (confidence 1.00)
CONFIDENTIAL
-4-

about his business, by a different policeman every day. He said this consumed at least two hours per day.

He said that he was greatly handicapped by not knowing a word of Japanese, since all business conferences were carried on in Japanese and he was informed of practically nothing that had been said, and at these he was only asked such inane questions as "Was he lonesome here", "Was he feeling well" etc.

He said that his trip to Rato to see the Taiwan Denka factory there had been fruitless since he had not been permitted to enter the small, 300 kw. works, where much of his company's ore had been taken.

He said that the Pacific Mining Company had arranged for ore sorters to sort the manganese ore left on the wharf in Keelung, and he had had to tell them how to work through an interpreter and that while he had been informed that prevailing wages would be charged he had been given a bill of Yen 10 per day for the interpreter and Yen 2 per day for male and Yen 1 per day for female labor regardless of age, the total coming to over Yen 1400. He said that he believed that wages here for such work should not have been over Yen 1 per day for a man, and less for women and boys, since in the Philippines coolies don't get over one peso per day. I assured him that his idea of coolie wages here was correct, but if he made no prior agreement he could not do much about it now.

He said that his trip here had at least showed him the difficulties of trying to do business with the Japanese, especially under war conditions, and that he had been frustrated at nearly every turn. I addition to the foregoing difficulties he said that his company had asked Taiwan Denka to advance him the Yen equivalent of Pesos 300, and they had given but Yen 564, compared with the rate in Manila of Pesos 45 to Yen 100, and the Japanese had furthermore informed him that he could not take more than Yen 100 away from Taiwan with him, so that he had arranged to leave the balance with Mr. Unida (?) of the Pacific Mining Company, Taihoku for transfer in company accounts.

He said that his company, the Negros Mining Company would be fortunate if it got back Pesos 4,000 on the Pesos 12,000 shipment of manganese ore he had come to examine, and that it would be naturally reluctant to do future business on this basis.

He said that Mr. Kobayashi had finally said to him that there was really no reason why he should have visited the factories, which shows that Kobayashi,

while

=== Page 15 === (confidence 0.99)
CONFIDENTIAL
-5-

while telling Escarrilla and myself that he was doing everything possible to facilitate such a visit, had perhaps informed Captain Yamahashi that such a visit was not necessary, and he would just as soon see permission denied. Mr. Escarrilla said he could not have much confidence in dealing with a person on such a basis. He also said that Taiwan Denka claimed they had received only 521 tons of ores while his company had shipped 600 tons, but that with the ore scattered between the wharf in Keelung and the Keelung and Rato factories there was no way he could check this discrepancy. He said his company had the worst end of the contract with the Taiwan Denka since the contract allowed Taiwan Denka to determine the percentage of ore in a shipment, and if his company believed it had shipped 54% ore and Taiwan Denka said it was only 45% ore there was no recourse.

Mr. Escarrilla telephoned later in the day to say that it had been impossible to agree on any settlement of the mix-up at the conference that afternoon and he would have to report matters to his company for decision and would write to Kobayashi.

March 15, 1939.

In connection with wages, the following excerpt from the Japan Adventizer of March 9, 1939 is part of a Diet interpellation, and the statement was made about naval arsenal workers (Japanese) by Paymaster Captain Ushinosuke Yamamoto, Chief of the 1st section of the Navy Ministry's accounts bureau:

"The highest wage for a male worker in the current fiscal year is ¥6.48; for a female worker, ¥1.56. The lowest for males is 38 sen; for female, 31 sen. The average work out at ¥1.30 for males and 83 sen for females."

If Japanese male arsenal workers in Japan Proper average but Yen 1.30 per day, Formosan coolies can hardly expect to be paid Yen 2.00 per day.

Gerald Warner
American Consul.

Mr. Escarrilla said he could not have much confidence in dealing with a person on such a basis. He also said that Taiwan Denka claimed they had received only 521 tons of ores while his company had shipped 600 tons, but that with the ore scattered between the wharf in Keelung and the Keelung and Rato factories there was no way he could check this discrepancy. He said his company had the worst end of the contract with the Taiwan Denka since the contract allowed Taiwan Denka to determine the percentage of ore in a shipment, and if his company believed it had shipped 54% ore and Taiwan Denka said it was only 45% ore there was no recourse.

Mr. Escarrilla telephoned later in the day to say that it had been impossible to agree on any settlement of the mix-up at the conference that afternoon and he would have to report matters to his company for decision and would write to Kobayashi.

March 15, 1939.

In connection with wages, the following excerpt from the Japan Adventiser of March 9, 1939 is part of a Diet interpellation, and the statement was made about naval arsenal workers (Japanese) by Paymaster Captain Ushinosuke Yamamoto, Chief of the 1st section of the Navy Ministry's accounts bureau:

"The highest wage for a male worker in the current fiscal year is ¥6.48; for a female worker, ¥1.56. The lowest for males is 38 sen; for female, 31 sen. The average work out at ¥1.30 for males and 83 sen for females."

If Japanese male arsenal workers in Japan proper average but Yen 1.20 per day, Formosan coolies can hardly expect to be paid Yen 2.00 per day.

Gerald Warner
American Consul.

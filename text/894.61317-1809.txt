## Memorandum of Conversation

DATE: November 29, 1948

SUBJECT: Allocation of Rice to Japan by IEFC

PARTICIPANTS: Army - Mr. Fraser (FAOA), Mr. Johnstone (SCAP)
ECA -
IR - Mr. Linville, Mr. Hougen
NE - Mr. Jenkins
CP - Mr. McCornick

COPIES TO:
ITP - Miss Brewster
DRF - Mrs. Sanderson

The meeting was held on November 29, 1948 to consider allocations of rice to Japan, shortly to come up before the IEFC Rice Committee. It was made quite evident by Mr. Linville that Japan will receive an allocation, although perhaps not the 200,000 to 300,000 tons SCAP desires; there will be dollar rice in excess of demand, and Japan is one of the few nations that can purchase it (with the use of GARIOA funds).

The Army men brought up a new proposal which appears to be ingenious, but perhaps potentially dangerous politically. It involves ECA and its dollars doing double duty. Japan, or rather the Board of Trade (Boeki Cho), would buy rice from, say, Siam, paying for it with manufactured products such as railroad equipment, and would then sell the rice to ECA to be used to meet its commitments to China. By this means SCAP would, in effect, be able to sell the manufactured products to a scarce dollar area for dollars. Siam is said to want those goods at least in part as much as it wants dollars. A possible objection to the plan, however, is that China probably would feel that Boeki Cho had obtained a monopoly over China's sources of rice supply, and would tend to cling to its position of procurer of China's rice.

The ECA officers present were attracted to the idea, but said that it would have to be considered in ECA on a higher level. Immediately, they saw no legal bars to such a plan, but they were aware of the possible political repercussions from the Chinese. They pointed out that the United States might declare rice surplus; in such case ECA is obliged by law to supply U.S. rice to participating countries.

The meeting was held merely to indicate to interested divisions that Japan would probably obtain a rice allocation and to introduce the above-outlined plan and no decisions were reached.

A-289, November 2, 1949.

Following news item released October 24, 1949, by Public Information Office, General Headquarters, (U.S.) Far East Command, Tokyo, under heading "Vice Crop Estimated at 9,832,000 Metric Tons":

"Japan's 1949 rice crop was estimated by the Japanese Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry today to total 9,832,000 metric tons. The estimate was reviewed by the Agriculture Division of SCAS's Natural Resources Section.

"Figures for the estimate were gathered by the Ministry from all prefectures. They allow for 'rice blast' and other damage occurring up to Oct. 10. Production was expected to be substantially larger earlier in the season but damage from typhoon, and late-season damage from 'rice blast' and other pests reduced the crop drastically in some areas, especially in Kytsu, Tohoku, and Kinki.

"The 1949 crop was described as five percent above the 1931-1940 average production which is considered to be representative of prewar years. This year's crop was also two percent above the 1948 rice harvest which totaled 9,617,000 metric tons. Last year's crop total was revised upward by 264,000 metric tons on the basis of final harvest data obtained at the end of harvest last year.

"Estimated production figures were arrived at by determining the area under cultivation and evaluating that production area according to recognized statistical crop sampling procedures.

"Crop records indicate that this year's harvest is the largest since 1942 when a bunker 9,999,000 metric tons of rice were grown.

A-357, December 13, 1949.

Reference is made to this Mission's A-289 dated November 2, 1949 on rice crop estimates. Following information was released by General Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, in a press release dated December 6, 1949 under the title "Record Food Production Reported":

"At the termination of hostilities of World War II staple food production in Japan was at its lowest level since the turn of the century. The staple crops in 1945 yielded only 9.9 million metric tons, in brown rice equivalents, compared with a 1931-40 average of 13 million metric tons and a wartime peak of 14 million metric tons in 1942.

"Japan has required food imports to feed its people even in periods of high production. The low 1945 crop yields placed the country's food supply in jeopardy.

"Agriculturists in the Japanese government and specialists in appropriate staff sections in Occupation headquarters moved immediately at the beginning of the Occupation to stimulate crop production.

"Fertilizer was an urgent problem. The supply had dropped far below Japanese requirements. Domestic production of this item was given a high priority, and large amounts were imported. By extraordinary efforts on the part of everyone concerned fertilizer supplies increased each year, until at present the consumption is approaching prewar levels.

"Seed improvement, more effective control of insects and diseases, and an increase in the supply of other necessary production materials also have aided farmers in increasing production.

"Preliminary estimates of the Crop Reporting Service, Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, place 1949 staple food production at 14.4 million metric tons brown rice equivalents. This is a slight increase over the 1948 production.

"Rice at 9.8 million metric tons and the combined wheat- barley crops at 2.4 million metric tons respectively are estimated to be the highest since the end of hostilities. Sweet potato production at 1.7 million metric tons, brown rice equivalents, is the second highest on record, exceeded only by the 1948 crop. White potato production at 430 thousand metric tons, brown rice equivalents, is the highest on record, slightly above the previous high of 1948. These estimates are subject to revision with the receipt of later information.

"The progressive increase in food production, together with the central food imports, has brought about a marked improvement in Japan's food position. A major share of the credit for this belongs to the farmers of Japan, who are traditionally hard working and who have given their full cooperation in attacking the country's food problem. The Japanese farmer has proved that he will produce if given the materials for production. The supply of those materials has been steadily increasing, and should continue to increase.

"Farmers and the government must continue to work together if agriculture is to progress. The government must keep faith with those who fill the soil and whose industrious and patriotic contribution to national rehabilitation has been second to no other element of society. Thus, the government should continue to make every effort to insure equitable allocation of quotas and the continued development of a crop reporting organization form of political pressures.

"Production incentives should be provided, consistent with the need for a stable farm economy and food production needs of the country. If these things are done, there is no doubt that farmers will continue to respond unstintingly to the challenge of Japan's requirements."

CONFIDENTIAL.

SUBJECT: Disturbances among the Savage Tribes in Taiwan.

THE HONORABLE
    THE SECRETARY OF STATE,
        WASHINGTON.

SIR:

I have the honor to report that conditions in the district of Musha have again become disturbed as a result of the massacre, in that area, on the morning of April 25 in inter-tribal conflict, of more than 200 members of the Seepow and Rodof tribes by the Tatsua tribe.

Although this incident is peculiar to the savage tribes and reflects no anti-Japanese feeling, such as characterized the revolt of last October, a rather serious aspect is cast upon the matter by the fact that the tribes attacked were utterly defenseless and had been deprived of means of defense by the Japanese authorities whereas the attacking tribe was armed with rifles and ammunition furnished by the same authorities. The former are known as the "protected" tribes, having been disarmed and segregated for participation in the uprising last October; the latter are called the "allied" tribes, having assisted the Japanese in quashing the rebellion and being

On April 21st, two [women?], [both?] [members?] of the "protected" tribes, were [killed?] by a party of savages belonging to the Tatsua tribe, one of the "allied" tribes. Although this and other indications pointed to a possible general attack by the "allied" tribes upon the defenseless "protected" tribes, no urgent measures of precaution were taken by the Japanese police and therefore the raids on the morning of the 25th were met with almost no opposition. It is reported that some 80 members of the Tatsua tribe participated in the attack on the village of Rodof and some 100 of the same tribe in the attack on the village of Seepow, these villages being the centers of the "protected" tribes.

Latest bulletins indicate that the bodies of 197 savages have been recovered but that over 100 members of the Seepow and Rodof tribes are still missing and the majority of these are presumably dead. Practically all the villages of the "protected" tribes have been destroyed, Poarun, Rodof, Seepow, and Swuk.

Circumstances lead one to believe that the Japanese authorities might have foreseen & consequently forestalled this trouble. It is said to be a fact that the Tatsua tribe for months has been asking permission of the Japanese police to kill the Seepow and Rodof tribes and as it is undoubtedly true that numerous incidents since the Musha uprising carry the conviction that the Tatsua tribe and other "allied" tribes were but waiting for a favorable opportunity to attack and kill their traditional enemies. Yet, notwithstanding this general agitation, the Japanese police permitted these tribes to carry rifles and allowed them large quantities of ammunition, in the meantime segregating the threatened tribes in a defenseless position. That the latter were defenseless and that the Japanese police seemingly made little attempt to protect their charges would appear from the fact that of the attackers only 1 was killed and 11 wounded.

On the whole it would seem that the charge is somewhat justified that the Japanese police in the savage districts are surprisingly unaware of what is

It is of more than passing interest to note to what extent the slaughter of so many members of the "protected" tribes works to the advantage of the Japanese authorities. This results from the lesser number of members of these tribes to be transported from their villages in the Musha region to other parts of Taiwan, for it is understood that this enforced emigration was adopted as a measure of punishment for participation in the revolt last year and as a measure of precaution to prevent further anti-Japanese disturbances in the district. One of the local newspapers, in commenting upon the losses to the Seepow and Rodof tribes, stated that "Emigration was made easy".

This massacre will undoubtedly furnish considerable fuel for political opponents of the present central Government and will probably result in changes in the personnel of the Taiwan Government General and the Taichu Prefectural Government. The present administration, both in Japan Proper and in Taiwan, is confronted not only with a political attack for mal-administration but also with the very difficult and delicate task of finding a solution

Although the Minshuto Party has been disbanded (Despatch [underlined: No. 71] dated February 28, 1931), it is expected that anti-Government parties and press will foster rumors and issue statements condemning the Japanese authorities for their actions in this circumstance. While much of this matter will be baseless and serving only a denunciatory purpose, there may come to light certain features which will be edifying as to what is really going on in the savage districts, facts which are suppressed by the Japanese authorities.

In business and financial circles this affair has caused considerable uneasiness as to the possible effect on the negotiating a loan for the Lake Candidius hydro-electric project.

EMBASSY OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA

No. 342

Chungking, March 31, 1942

Subject: Chinese Publicity Regarding the Formosan Question.

Air Mail

The Honorable
The Secretary of State,
Washington, D. C.

Sir:

I have the honor to refer to recent publicity from China concerning the future status of Formosa, some of which has undoubtedly come to the attention of the Department, and to enclose for the Department's information copies of typical samples of the material which [has, inserted by hand] reached the Embassy, commenting briefly thereon.

The enclosures are the following:

(1) "Inauguration Manifesto of the Formosan Revolutionary Federation" and "Outlines of Policy of the Formosan Revolutionary Federation" - undated but forwarded under cover of a despatch dated December 1, 1941, from the American Consul at Foochow, China.

(2) "Manifesto of the Formosan Revolutionary League (or Federation) Concerning the War on the Pacific" - dated December 12, 1941 and received by the Embassy without covering letter.

(3) Clipping from CENTRAL NEWS (official Chinese news agency) dated Chungking, March [2?], 1942, reporting holding of a conference of the "Formosan Revolutionaries League" at Chungking on March 20 and 21.

(4) Article from Bulletin no. 297, March 24, 1942, of the China Information Committee (an organ of the Kuomintang's Publicity Board), entitled: "Formosans Seeking Reunion with China."

In addition I refer to the speech made by Dr. SUN Fo, President of the Judicial Yuan, on March 22 (the subject of the Department's telegram no. 218, March 25, 12 midnight and my no. 287, March 28, 11 a.m.) stating, inter alia, that "after the war Formosa will come back to China", a statement typical of those recently expressed by other public figures and appearing from time to time in the press.

As far as is known to the Embassy the Formosan independence movement in China has not been strong or active in the past. There have been, of course, many Formosans of Chinese race living along the Fukien coastal area, where they have been much criticized for their apparent willingness to utilize Japanese extraterritoriality to protect their businesses and often questionable activities. But the number of Formosans in unoccupied China is undoubtedly very small and it is hardly likely that even all of the thirty said to have attended the conference referred to in enclosure no. 3 were actually Formosans.

The apparently rather sudden public interest in Formosa on the part of the Chinese authorities seems to date from the outbreak of hostilities in the Pacific in December, 1941: before that time little was heard of the matter even in statements of Chinese war aims.

It seems obvious that Chinese ambitions to have Formosa returned to China are encouraged by the involvement in the war of the United States and Great Britain. At the same time the Chinese may feel that the recent international attention given to the question of future Korean independence makes it advisable for China to lose no time in forestalling any similar suggestion of independence for Formosa by (1) fostering organizations claiming to represent the Formosans which will call for the reunion of Formosa with China and (2) stressing in propaganda, as is done in enclosure no. 4, the Chinese racial ties of a large part of the Formosan population.

Respectfully yours,

[signature]
C. E. Gauss

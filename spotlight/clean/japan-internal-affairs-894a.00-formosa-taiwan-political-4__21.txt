“KO-MIN-KA” OR THE JAPANIZATION OF TAIWAN

INTRODUCTION

The Governor-General of Taiwan has stated publicly on various occasions that the three issues most vital to Taiwan at the present time were: 1) expansion to South China and the South Seas, 2) industrialization of the island, and 3) ko-min-ka¹ or the Japanization of the people of Taiwan. The first two problems have been treated in previous reports of this Consulate.² In the present discussion, an effort has been made to outline the various organs, both official and unofficial, which Japan is using as means to inculcate those principles of patriotism and Japanese ideology believed necessary to Japanize the Formosans. In addition, a study has been made of the measures taken by the Japanese to remodel the culture and religion of the people of Taiwan after a Japanese model. While no definitive conclusions can be drawn, an evaluation of the progress made by the Japanese in their campaign to Japanize the Formosans has been attempted.

The campaign of Ko-min-ka in its present vigorous and intensive form dates only from the beginning of the China Incident. Not only was it felt to be a measure necessary to forestall any pro-Chinese sympathies which might result from the hostilities,

I. ORGANS OF POLITICAL CONTROL

In order to understand the Japanization movement in Taiwan, it is necessary to examine the administrative organs, official and unofficial, which are being utilized in the process. These include the local assemblies as well as the urban and rural systems known as chokai and hoko, the latter an ancient Chinese inheritance permitted to remain in existence.

Local Self-Government

The present system of local self-government was established April 1, 1935 and elections for village, town, and city assemblies were held in that year. The terms of local elected officials are four years; thus, the second election in the history of Taiwan was held November 22, 1939.

The franchise in Taiwan is granted to Japanese male subjects¹ 25 years of age or over, with independent means, resident in the city, town, or village for a specified period, and who have paid a city, town, or village tax of at least ¥5.00. Incompetents, bankrupts, and persons with prison records are ineligible to vote.

Municipal "Chokai"

The chokai system, which is similar to that in existence in the municipalities of Japan Proper, provides a convenient network of unofficial control extending from the Mayor's office to the individual homes of the city inhabitants. A city is divided into a number of districts (ku) and a head of each district (ku-cho) is appointed by the Mayor. This office is honorary and carries no compensation; however, a certain sum of money is appropriated by the municipality for expenses of maintaining an office. Sub-committee members (ku i-in) are appointed in each district by the ku-cho and carry out his instructions.

Each ku consists of a number of streets or blocks (cho) in which are found the chokai or

neighborhood

=== Page 102 ===

neighborhood councils. These are voluntary organizations, whose chairmen are elected by the inhabitants of the neighborhood. The purposes of the chokai are to increase the welfare and prosperity of the local inhabitants, to promote friendship among them, and to assist the municipal administration. One rather surprising activity of the chokai, at least in Taihoku, is its responsibility for street lighting within its district, a function not undertaken by the city. Expenses of the chokai are met through contributions from the inhabitants; these range from ¥2.40 to ¥60 per year per family, according to financial ability.

Very often the head of a ku and the chairman of a chokai are one and the same person. In such instances, combined offices are maintained.

The councillors of the chokai (hyogi-in) are appointed by the chairman and assist him in administering neighborhood activities.

This system provides a quick and convenient method for dissemination of instructions, information, and public notices. Each committee member under a ku represents approximately twenty houses; when he receives a notice from his chief, it is his duty to circularize it among the houses in his jurisdiction. The notice is usually taken from house to house and the seal of the head of the family obtained as acknowledgment of his having read the instruction.

The "hoko" system

The hoko system, of Chinese origins, has been permitted to continue in existence in the towns and villages of Taiwan. It was legalized by decree of the Taiwan Government General in 1898 as a subsidiary organization to assist police administration. In 1909 its duties were widened to include assistance to local civil administration.

One ko is a group of approximately ten households; a ho is ten ko, or approximately 100 households. The head of each ko is elected by the householders, but this choice must be approved by the head of the county (gun) and the respective heads of district and local police stations. Election of the head

of the ho, or hosei, is subject to the approval not only of county and police officials but also of the provincial or prefectural governor.

The duties of the heads of the ko and ho are defined as follows:

1. Maintenance of peace and order in district.
2. Taking of census in district.
3. Encouragement of good morals and conduct; punishment of offenders (morals and conduct).

The law provides that rewards and punishments may be meted out by the heads of ho and ko as incentives to the maintenance of order and welfare.

Expenses for the hoko are obtained from levies on the households; these amount to an average of 60 sen per month per household. The chiefs of the various ho organize associations which may meet for discussion of local problems.

The law provides further that under the auspices of the ho, young men's associations (sotei dan) may be organized, primarily for the purpose of guarding against banditry and natural disasters such as flood, fire, and typhoons. Members of the association are men between the ages of 17 and 40 of good standing and strong physique. They are under the supervision of the local police and are sometimes given training at the police station. The soteidan is not to be confused with the seinendan, a purely Japanese organization, described in a later section of this report.

III. SOCIAL CONTROL

Japan is attempting to abolish the Chinese customs of the Formosan people. Since the outbreak of the China Incident, this attempt has been intensified and in addition, numerous and varied activities have been sponsored among the people as part of the "national spiritual mobilization movement".

Renovation of customs

The daily lives of the people of Taiwan are to be influenced through a change in language, social customs, and religion.

Abolition of many customs is the result of modern civilization, dictated by sanitation or economic necessity. Others are matters of minor importance, the prohibition of which is irritating to the people. As one Formosan remarked to the writer, "Many such reforms are changes in outward appearance only. They do not reform the spirit within."

Foot-binding of women. No longer practiced, this is seen only in old women.

VI. THE PROBLEM OF THE ABORIGINES

A special problem in "Japanization" is created by the presence in Taiwan of more than 150,000 aborigines, or Takasagozoku as they are popularly called.1 As recently as 1930 a serious insurrection broke out among the savages in the district of Musha resulting in the massacre of 107 Japanese men, women, and children. There have, however, been no fatalities since 1934 and the results of police administration and pacification are said to be satisfactory.

Administration

Although there are numerous ethnological classifications, the aborigines, for administrative purposes, fall into two classes: plains dwellers, and inhabitants of savage administrative districts. The former are considered assimilated into the Formosan population and receive no distinctive treatment. The latter are outside ordinary legal administration, live in segregated districts, and are completely subordinate to police authority.

The policeman in a savage village fills a unique position. Not only is he guardian of the law, but teacher, counsellor, economic and agricultural advisor, often doctor and religious guide. His aim is to educate the aborigines in Japanese ideals, to discourage old customs and traditions, in short to carry out, to the best of his ability, the process of ko-min-ka. Difficulties have occurred in the past due to offense given the savages through the policemen's relations with native women. In general, however, officers assigned to the special work of savage administration seem to have done creditable work.

VII. THE FUTURE OF KO-MIN-KA

Success of Ko-min-ka

The youth of Taiwan are being educated as Japanese. The language of the schoolroom is Japanese, although the language of the home remains Chinese. The young Formosan is drilled in the precepts of the Yamato spirit (Yamato-damashii) and is urged to forget his past. This he undoubtedly does, especially since he realizes that economic advantages will depend upon the degree of his Japanization.

Chinese customs and religions are gradually being replaced and a younger generation which understands nothing of such customs will not resist their passing. Needless suppression of minor customs and rites, such as burning gilt papers and presenting puppet plays, will continue to irritate..

The spread of the Japanese language will continue, although it may be many years before mothers can be "mobilized" to use only Japanese to their babes in arms. Compulsory education, to be effective from 1943, will speed up the process of language learning. Certainly rural districts will be the last to change their language habits and it takes vivid imagination to picture the time when one can ask directions in Japanese of the farmer in the field or on the mountain

path.

VIII. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

The conclusions drawn in this report are not definitive. They are based on observations in Taiwan during the period of a six months assignment, general reading, and conversations with Japanese, Formosan Chinese, and foreigners, the latter including missionaries, teachers, and business people.

Summary

1) Self-government in Taiwan is limited to participation in city, town, and village government. Suffrage is granted to male subjects of 25 years or more, who may vote for members of city, town, or village assemblies, one half of whose members are elective. Elections are held every four years. The percentage of Formosan members of city and provincial assemblies is small.

2) A national campaign, accelerated since the beginning of the China Incident, has been undertaken to spread the use of the Japanese language. 45% of the Formosan population is said to possess a knowledge of Japanese, although this figure may be an exaggeration and may indicate only a superficial knowledge. Compulsory education is to be in effect from 1943, which will make a six year primary course obligatory. Education for Japanese and Formosans is separate through the primary school, and comparatively few Formosans attend middle school, high school, or the university. A large percentage of Formosan

university

3) Abolition of traditional Chinese customs is a policy of the Government. This movement has been given impetus by the China Incident and the campaign for "spiritual mobilization" started in Japan. Various activities, which serve the dual purpose of Japanization and contribution to the propagation of hostilities in China, are encouraged.

4) The traditional religions in Taiwan, symbolized by the byo or Formosan temple, are discouraged and an effort is being made to substitute the Japanese shinto Shrine. Christian missionaries are experiencing increasing difficulties in carrying on their work.

5) The psychology behind the Japanese efforts at social and religious reformation is illustrated in the movement to set up a model district and a model village, in which the principles of ko-min-ka are to be applied.

6) Japanization of the aborigines, of which there are more than 150,000, presents special problems. Administration is carried out through police authority which seeks to control the life of the savages, increase their economic usefulness and teach them Japanese ideals.

1) Formosan resistance to Japanese authority is inconceivable, not only because of the rigidity

3) Use of the Japanese language will increase and will became indispensable to success in most trades and professions, and to participation in civic life.

4) Business and professional opportunities for Formosans will remain limited in Taiwan, due to the monopoly of positions of responsibility by Japanese.

NOTE ON THE ADMINISTRATION OF TAIWAN

The chart prepared for this report shows the various administrative organs of the village, town, city, county, and province, and their relationships. It should be noted that Taiwan is divided, for purposes of administration, into five provinces and three prefectures (cho). The cho, Karenko, Taito, and Boko (Pescadores), are of relatively small area and population. Local administration within them is similar to the system shown in the chart, in the case of the provinces. Election of half the members of town and village assemblies has been established only recently; the elections held November 22, 1939, were the first to be held in the prefectures.

The following differences are noted in the administrative organization of the prefectures as compared to that in the provinces:

1. The rank of the Governor of a prefecture is lower than that of the Governor of a province.

2. The powers of the prefectural assembly are advisory only, whereas the provincial assembly may make decisions.

WASEDA INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTE

There are a large number of American-born Japanese, and Americans of Japanese lineage, who graduate from high schools but give up their college careers because of the depression and other economic problems that make it difficult to secure positions after graduation. Recent industrial developments in Japan offer new opportunities that are drawing many of them to Japan, and their peculiar qualifications in language and culture, for positions requiring an understanding of both America and Japan opens up for them many places in companies engaged in trade between the two countries.

However, both for the requirements of business, and for the needs of those who wish to come to live in Japan, boys and girls in this group find themselves lacking in knowledge of both Japanese language and customs, and in need of special preparation not available through the ordinary school. Two years ago Waseda University, the largest university in Japan, opened its doors to Japanese from abroad, and foreigners of Japanese lineage, but, in order to take advantage of this opportunity, many need special language preparation.

To meet this need Waseda Hoshien, at the request of the President of Waseda University, has organized its International Institute. In this Institute courses will be given, beginning in September 1935, which will prepare high school graduates from abroad for entrance into Waseda University.

Students will be admitted at the beginning of the spring and fall terms, and, though the Institute seeks to serve the needs of those who intend to enter Waseda, students preparing for other universities will be accepted. (See Regulations of this Institute).

This education of Japanese from abroad and of foreigners of Japanese lineage is in the pioneer stage. For this reason this Institute is anxious to make the opening years

successful ones, and only students who wish to go on with their higher education, and whose school and character records are satisfactory, will be accepted.

Waseda does not admit students without examinations, though all students must be graduates of high schools (see Catalogue of Waseda University for entrance requirements), and, on the average, at least two school terms will be necessary for the language preparations for those coming from abroad. In many cases a year or more will be necessary, but for the student who comes with considerable knowledge of Japanese it should be possible to take the entrance examinations of the University the following March after entering this Institute in September.

It is not advisable for a student to decide to come to Tokyo unless he is prepared to give his best to the work here and to persevere until he is successful. The language difficulty demands a sacrifice of time and effort that is only worth while if the student is going to put it to use.

This Institute office will be glad to answer inquiries and supply information to those who are considering coming to Japan for study, and to assist them in establishing themselves in the Waseda community.

Americans of Japanese lineage, with a mastery of both Japanese and English, and with the virtues of both Japanese and Americans, have important tools which are needed to-day. Not only in America and Japan, but in the international field, they will be able to take a place of prominence and leadership. Indeed they have an opportunity to make stronger the bonds between the two countries and to establish relationships on a footing that will assure permanent peace. Waseda International Institute hopes that many will pass through its doors into this important work.

REGULATIONS
of the
WASEDA INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTE

CHAPTER 1. GENERAL RULES

Article 1. The purpose of this Institute is to prepare for entrance into Waseda University (Department of Political Science and Economics, Law, Literature, Commerce, and Engineering, Department of Specialization (Senmonbu), Higher Normal Department, and First and Second Junior Colleges (Kōtōgakuin, etc.), and to instruct in Japanese Culture foreigners of Japanese lineage and other persons who wish to enter Waseda University from foreign schools.

CHAPTER 2. CURRICULUM OF INSTRUCTION

Article 2. The school year is divided into three terms: an Autumn Session, Winter Session, and Spring Session.

Article 3. Courses of instruction are given as follows:

Japanese Language (Reading, Grammar, Composition, Dictation, Writing, Conversation).
Chinese Language (Reading).
English (English-Japanese, Japanese-English).
Mathematics (Algebra, Geometry).
Courses in Japanese Culture.

Article 4. Four hours of instruction are given in the forenoon, and one hour in the afternoon.

CHAPTER 3. SCHOOL TERMS AND HOLIDAYS

Article 5. The Autumn Session is from the beginning of September to the end of December, the Winter Session from the beginning of January to the middle of March, and the Spring Session from the beginning of April to the middle of July.

Article 6. The Summer Vacation is from the middle of July to the beginning of September, and the Winter Vacation from the end of December to the beginning of January.

Article 7. School will be closed on Saturdays, Sundays, Japanese national holidays, and school holidays.

CHAPTER 4. ADMISSION AND DISMISSION

Article 8. Regular students will be admitted at the beginning of the Autumn and Spring Sessions, but students may be admitted at other times by special permission of the President.

Article 9. Qualified students will be admitted on the following basis:

1. Students who are graduates of high schools or who have the equivalent academic standing.
2. Students who have been recognized by the Minister of Education in Japan according to Public Notice No. 266 of the Department of Education, issued in 1929, as students whose scholastic attainment is equal to or above that of middle school(chugakko) graduates.

Article 10. A candidate for entrance must submit an application for admission, a recent card-size photograph, and the following:

1. A record of past studies.
2. A diploma or certificate meeting the qualification of Article 9, 1. The certificate must give years and courses of studies, and a record of the last three years' school work.
3. A certificate of recognition from the Minister of Education in Japan meeting the qualification of Article 9, 2. But this certificate is unnecessary for foreigners of Japanese lineage.
4. An abstract of the census register.

Article 11. The student, on being permitted to enter this institute, must submit a certificate with the signature of a guarantor.

WASEDA CHRISTIAN CENTER
(Waseda Hoshien)

The activities that make up the life of Waseda Hoshien are the result of twenty-seven years of successful experimentation in student work under the leadership of Dr. Benninghoff, and with the fullest cooperation of the authorities of Waseda University.

Widely known as Scott Hall, the name of the building that houses the church and club rooms, it reaches out through its dormitory, student church, Christian study classes, clubs and sports to influence a large section of the student body.

When the Fact Finding Staff of the Laymen's Foreign Mission Inquiry visited Japan they wrote in their report on work among students, “It seems to the investigator that in the total number of students reached, and in the impact and influence upon the total life of the institution, this work directed by Dr. Benninghoff is by all odds the most significant of any piece of student work in the non-Christian colleges and universities in the communities studied.”

The Friendship Dormitory has enrolled nearly three hundred students during its history. These students have taken part in the full life of the Hoshien, conducted their own groups for prayer and study, associated intimately with Dr. Benninghoff, and carried the influence of those years of Christian fellowship into varied fields of service.

The activities of the student church are now under the leadership of Dr. Benninghoff's associate, Mr. Shinozaki. The church conducts Sunday morning worship, Sunday School and prayer meeting, and is the center around which is built the religious life of the Hoshien.

Scott Hall is a modern student center, with a main auditorium, club rooms of various sizes, offices, dining room

WASEDA UNIVERSITY

Waseda is the largest private university in Japan. With its associated higher schools, and evening schools, its total student body reaches nearly twenty thousand. In a nation where government schools have many advantages, it has achieved a place second only to the Tokyo Imperial University. In Japan its name is associated with advance in education, the many progressive movements with which it has been associated, and excellence in sports,—this last being as highly sought after as it is in America.

Waseda was founded in 1882 as the Tokyo Academy by Marquis Okuma, famous as a statesman of the Meiji Period. Within a period of fifty years the school has developed from an enrollment of sixty students to its present size.

The academy was established to meet two needs of the time; more accessible higher education, and freedom of investigation. It was also the conviction of Marquis Okuma that education should be carried on in the Japanese language, and this was practiced from the first, though instruction in all other institutions of higher education was at that time given entirely in foreign languages.

Waseda has always been popular with Chinese students, who come in large numbers. Since last year it has made its doors more accessible to American-born Japanese, and about fifty of them are enrolled this year.

The Department of Engineering has flourished since its establishment in 1909. This was a large venture for a private university to undertake, as it required a sum of money difficult for such institutions to raise. The Emperor made the first contribution to the department, and it was through his gift, and that of the princes and influential people who followed suit, that its success was assured. It is also interesting to note that a considerable sum was donated by personages of China.

To-day the Architectural Department is the best in Japan, and the Department of Literature hardly surpassed, while its other departments are of the highest order, and many of its professors are first in their fields. There are two junior colleges, the First Kotogakuin with a three year course and the Second Kotogakuin with a two year course, with two thousand students each. Graduates from these schools may enter the University proper, with its forty-five hundred students, which is a three year course, with Departments of Commerce, Law, Political Science, Engineering, and Literature. There is, in addition, a Normal Department, with over five hundred students, and a School of Specialization, a three year course, with four thousand students.

日系第二世がハイスクールは優等で卒業するのに、何故大學卒業の成績が良くないのであるか？　これ彼等が希望なき前途を知り、奮鬪生活を中止するが故である。風俗も習慣も言語も米國式に導かれし彼等は白人系米人と總ての職業戰線にたつて平等たるべきである。然し財政的、法律的壓迫と、人種的偏見の故に、この理想は實現されてゐない。こゝに米國に永住せんとする第二世の第一の悲哀がある。日本語を知らず、日本を背景としない彼等の未來は暗黑である。然らば彼等は父母の國なる日本に歸れば目的を達成し得るのであるか？　彼等の心臟には日本人の血汐が躍動してゐる。然し彼等の教育されし環境は日本の環境と同一ではない。日本を理解すべく彼等の日本語は餘りにも貧弱であり、内地での就職は至難である。こゝに日本に永住せんとする第二世の第二の歎息がある。

SIR:

I have the honor to report that on January 7, 1938 Paul King Benedict, 25, bearer of Passport No. 451740 issued January 26, 1937 by the Department registered at this office and presented credentials from Harvard University stating that he was qualified to pursue certain anthropological studies in work for his degree of Doctor of Philosophy.

[Pencil bracket and initials in left margin: W.M.?]
Mr. Benedict had the idea, as did those in Harvard who had sponsored his work that all he needed do was to settle down in some town in China or Japan and begin his measurements and consult official town records to confirm statements that might be doubtful. He explained that he had gone to Shanghai last August with the intention of working on his thesis of finding out whether there might be some connection between a person's physical characteristics and his social standing by working on the male population between the ages of about 17 and 60 in various towns in China of about 5,000 population where certain conditions

such as the homogeneity of the group would be found. He stated that he had studied Chinese characters for the past two years at Harvard and his other studies had been directed toward his present proposed year or so of field work. However he found after three weeks in Shanghai that conditions in China were not likely to be favorable to such studies for some time to come and so went back to Tokyo where he studied Japanese for 3 months and then came to Taiwan where he proposed to pursue his work on the Chinese population in various towns beginning with Tamsui. He had decided on Tamsui at the instance of Professor Nenozo Utsushigawa of Taihoku Imperial University an anthropologist who has done work among the aborigines of Taiwan, and who was greatly interested in having Mr. Benedict pursue his research here.

However, after hearing of the difficulties which would beset him not alone in the matter of proper housing and food, but also on the official score, he decided that it would be best to take his case voluntarily to the Division of Foreign Affairs of the Taiwan Government General first rather than to try to begin his work and be called in for an accounting under circumstances which might well be less favorable.

Accordingly Mr. Matsuo and I accompanied Mr. Benedict to the Division of Foreign Affairs on Monday, January 10 where we saw Mr. Koshimura by appointment in the absence of the chief, Mr. Kato.

The long preparation for and the purpose of Mr. Benedict's work was explained in detail and while Mr. Koshimura expressed personal interest in such studies, he stated that it would be very difficult to obtain permission for such research even if the present emergency situation did not exist. [Vertical pencil line in left margin alongside following sentence] He said that one thing which would not be tolerated as a subject of investigation would be the relationship between the governing and the governed, a delicate subject which would be bound to come up in spite of the intention of Mr. Benedict not to ask questions on that point. [Pencil stroke under "Mr."] Mr. Koshimura pointed out that the second Stewart Psycho-Ethnographic Expedition to Koto-sho, Taiwan from December 1936-March 1937, was to have been confined to a study of dream symbolism and yet governmental relations entered into reports and comments of the expedition. Copies of the preliminary reports of this expedition are attached as enclosures. Mr. Koshimura further added that explanation of that expedition's purpose caused no end of trouble to the Taiwan Government, and he believed that it would be loath to have any further foreign research carried on in view of these difficulties. He said that scientists seem despite themselves to cross over into fields they do not intend to enter. Another point brought out was that Mr. Benedict had said he wished to study the Chinese while it is not officially recognized that the body of the population in Formosa are

Chinese

Chinese. They are Formosans and Japanese subjects. Further that the Government, even if it granted permission for such studies could not compel people to submit to the measurements and questions Mr. Benedict proposed to ask. Mr. Benedict said he was prepared to take care of that end of things if only he could obtain the permission of the authorities to act without being molested. He offered to submit all of his notes and results of his investigations to the authorities and Mr. Koshimura remarked that he would of course have to agree to that if permission were to be granted. Another stumbling block would be freedom of access to official records of persons examined.

In view of the apparent difficulties of undertaking his proposed research independently, it was suggested that Mr. Benedict might associate himself with Taihoku Imperial University and pursue his studies as a special student. Mr. Koshimura agreed that this would be the best way to obtain permission, and since Professor Utsushigawa was interested in Mr. Benedict it was considered best that he see the authorities on Mr. Benedict's behalf to discuss the question further and in the meantime Mr. Koshimura would take up the question with Mr. Kato, Chief of the Division of Foreign Affairs and others concerned.

As a result, it was decided within a few days that permission would not be granted to Mr. Benedict under any circumstances to pursue his proposed

research in Taiwan.

Mr. Benedict was satisfied that this office could give him no further assistance, and sailed for Hongkong on the HONGKONG MARU on January 18, with the intention of going to Hanoi, French Indo-China, to try his luck with the French authorities. Before he left, I suggested that he might be able to find suitable communities in the Philippine Islands should the French-Indo China authorities not be favorably disposed toward his research.

He is informing his Harvard professors of the difficulties attendant on obtaining permission for the research he wishes to carry on, and that a five cent stamp could have obviated considerable travel expense.

An appropriate notation concerning his departure has been made on Mr. Benedict's registration at this office.

    It is believed by certain competent observers that Mr. Kenkichi Yoshizawa, Japan's representative on the Council of the League of Nations and son-in-law of the new Premier, will be offered the post of Foreign Minister. Incidentally, it is rumored that Baron Shidehara is to replace Mr. Yoshizawa in Paris.

    The outgoing Wakatsuki Cabinet, which was organized in April to succeed the Hamaguchi Cabinet, was regarded from the first as a stop-gap government. Baron Wakatsuki had been persuaded to emerge from retirement to assume the presidency of the party and the mantle of the wounded Mr. Hamaguchi

[Pencil marks in the left margin by "Incidentally".]

=== Page 221 ===
- 3 -

Hamaguchi in an attempt to bring into line the disorganized forces of the Minseito party. This party had shown definite signs of cleavage when the strong hand of the Old Lion, Hamaguchi, was relaxed, tending to split into groups around several powerful personalities, namely Mr. Adachi, Mr. Egi and the now Governor General of Chosen, General Ugaki. Mr. Wakatsuki managed to keep these various elements from open revolt for nearly eight months, but his control over them was never certain. Without a strong central leader and troubled with internal dissension as to its policies, the Wakatsuki Cabinet was conceded by many observers no chance of surviving the Diet even if it managed to live until the session opened.

    The outgoing Cabinet, during its short tenure of office, was called upon to face an extraordinary number of important issues, chief of which was the Manchurian problem. In these few months, problems of government finance and compilation of the budget of the next fiscal year have given grave anxiety to the Cabinet. The chief accomplishments of the Wakatsuki Government have been a reduction of official salaries, a reduction in the pension system, the maintenance of free export of gold, and the appointment of several outstanding men to important offices, such as General Ugaki to the Governor Generalship of Korea and Count Uchida to the presidency of the South Manchurian Railway. On the other hand, there was strong criticism of the Government. It had been compelled to abandon some of its promises. Its policy of strict retrenchment in government expenditure had been impossible of accomplishment

[Pencil check marks in the left margin.]

=== Page 222 ===
- 4 -

accomplishment, and the Ministry's budget could not be balanced without loans, in spite of its no-loan promises. There is little doubt but that the repudiation of its fundamental principles, announced at the time of the inception of the Hamaguchi regime and again enunciated at the formation of the Wakatsuki Cabinet, had a great deal to do with the downfall of the Minseito government.

    The immediate cause of the downfall of the Wakatsuki Cabinet, as stated in my telegram No. 258 of December 11, was apparently the result of a political intrigue on the part of Mr. Adachi, Home Minister, and the collapse seems to have had no direct connection with any action of the Government, nor, as might easily have been expected, with the Manchurian situation. Mr. Adachi's actions, for the past few weeks, have aroused much comment and speculation. I have on a number of occasions reported to the Department that Adachi is perhaps the most powerful leader of the Minseito, holding the allegiance of the best organized group in the party. He has long been suspected of aspiring to the premiership, and is said to have been disgruntled because he failed to be chosen to head the Government after the resignation of Mr. Hamaguchi in April. Although he is a man of unrivaled genius for political organizing, he is supposed to lack the higher qualities desirable in a statesman heading the Government.

    Early in November Mr. Adachi cast a bombshell into the political situation by announcing to press representatives that he would welcome a "national" or coalition Cabinet in combination

[Pencil check marks in the left margin.]

=== Page 223 ===
- 5 -

combination with the Seiyukai (See my despatch No. 404 of December 4). He had, from all evidences at hand, the approval of neither the Cabinet nor his Party in regard to such a plan. There are reasons, however, to believe that he had previously broached this matter to the Premier in an informal way and had at that time received some encouragement. Baron Wakatsuki may have either misunderstood the full implications of the proposed coalition, or have later withdrawn his support, because he announced publicly that he could not entertain the idea of any compromise with the Opposition. At any rate, his proposal caused consternation in his Party, and was met with determined opposition by the Minseito Party and the Cabinet. As a result of this opposition he renounced the plan, but the public continued to suspect that wires were being pulled behind the scenes.

    These suspicions were deepened when, on December 5, Mr. Adachi called on Prince Saionji, indicating, to some observers, that a further attempt at bringing about a coalition government was being made. On December 10th, Mr. Adachi brought his demand for a coalition government to a definite issue. One of his chief henchmen, Mr. Tomita, called upon Baron Wakatsuki and stated that the idea of a coalition government had been gaining wide favor and advised the Premier to agree to such a movement before it was too late to save the Ministry from collapse. A Cabinet meeting was immediately called, at which the Ministers of State endeavored to persuade Mr. Adachi to abandon his demand for a coalition government. On December 11, two Ministers, delegated by the Cabinet, called on Mr. Adachi, reviewed the situation and attempted to dissuade him from pressing his demand. Upon his refusal, they asked

[Pencil mark in the margin by "On December 10th".] [Typescript is faded or worn on some letters of this page but the text is readable.]

asked for his resignation, which he also refused to tender. Whereupon the Cabinet, deadlocked by this schism, decided to resign. The Premier stated in his resignation that his Ministry found it impossible to carry on their administrative policies by reason of a lack of unity among its members, and that the Cabinet held itself responsible for this disunity. The resignations of the Wakatsuki Ministry were submitted on the 11th.

    As a reason for [illegible]tion of a cabinet, this is practically without precedent in Japan. Wakatsuki could very easily have advanced some nominal reason for his resignation, such as ill health, the usual Japanese subterfuge, but he chose to make a straightforward statement. He thereby threw the blame for his fall directly on the Home Minister and his schemes. He had undoubtedly foreseen that a fall was imminent in any event, because of the impasse to which his policies seemed to be approaching. He also must have seen that his Cabinet would probably come to a much more ignominious end if it continued in office during the impending Diet session, whether Adachi left or remained in the Cabinet.

    Mr. Wakatsuki showed astuteness in thus making a decision which gave him at least an opportunity to resign with dignity, at the same time discrediting Adachi and his coalition schemes. He also must have calculated that he had at least an even chance of being returned to power with a reformed Cabinet. Since defection of a single Minister was given as his excuse for resignation, and not the bankruptcy of his policies, many people believed that he might be called on to form the succeeding Cabinet. As it happened, he failed to receive the order to form the new Government, but his own position remains strong. His party's reputation is unharmed, even if its power is

is impaired, while he and his colleagues have avoided the inquisition that awaited them in the Diet.

    Mr. Adachi has received the onus of the fall of the Minseito Government and has resigned from the Party. His followers have hesitated to follow him into exile, for fear that they would lose all profit in case the Minseito were returned to power. Only nine of his henchmen have resigned to follow him. His political future is uncertain, but it is rumored that he will attempt to form a new political party. In this he will have a difficult task, as he is now 67 years old and considerably discredited.

    Some attempt should be made to explain the motives behind Mr. Adachi's apparently strange insistence on a coalition government. It should be kept in mind that there had been a certain amount of jealousy of the Premier on the part of Mr. Adachi, probably accounted for by Wakatsuki's appointment to an office that he himself coveted. It is said that the Home Minister advanced his proposal with the object of forcing the Premier into retirement, in the hope that this show of his own power would bring him, in good time, the presidency of the Party. This theory would explain Mr. Adachi's desire to deadlock the Cabinet, but leaves still unexplained his insistence, highly inconsistent in an avowed party man, on a coalition Cabinet. I have been informed from various sources that back of Adachi's political manoeuvre were the Mitsui interests which had purchased vast sums of gold dollars in anticipation of a gold embargo and who stood to profit if the Minseito Cabinet with its free gold policy were forced out. The Minseito has stood for free gold, whereas the Seiyukai has advocated a replacement of the gold embargo. A coalition government could replace the gold embargo and might at the same time bring Adachi a position of authority. It may be that Mr. Adachi had been assured of rewards

[Marginal typed stamp, rotated: "STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL"; pencil check marks in the left margin; a heavy black stroke at the top left corner.]

=== Page 226 ===
- 8 -

wards, if he succeeded in ousting the Wakatsuki Government, which would compensate for the risk to his own position in the Party.

    The press reports that Mr. Adachi was betrayed, by his own henchmen, into believing that the Seiyukai had agreed to cooperate in the proposed coalition scheme before he presented it to his colleagues. It is hard to believe that so astute a politician would take so decisive a step without definite assurances as to the attitude of the party from which the cooperation was expected. The promise of reward from an outside source would, however, explain his intransigence.

    The Seiyukai's attitude toward the proposed coalition has ostensibly been unsympathetic. Mr. Inukai has, in his public utterances, from the time the proposal was first made, rejected the idea of combination with the Minseito. It is certain, though, that a section of his party had given consideration to Mr. Adachi's suggestion. Mr. Kuhara, Chief Secretary of the Seiyukai and a politician of somewhat tarnished reputation, is said to have carried on negotiations with Mr. Adachi looking toward a coalition organization, in spite of the attitude of Mr. Inukai. It seems possible that he received private instruction from Inukai to negotiate with the Adachi faction in regard to a coalition, but that Inukai, when the situation seemed to favor his party, decided to abandon the scheme. Subsequently, Mr. Kuhara resigned his position as Chief Secretary.

    In the above account, I have touched on the immediate causes

[Pencil check mark in the left margin; black triangle mark at the top left.]

=== Page 227 ===

causes of the fall of the Wakatsuki Cabinet, and have mentioned the part which the failure of the Wakatsuki Ministry to live up to its pledged policy in regard to loans and financial retrenchment, played in the fall of the Cabinet. There are other factors which contributed to the situation. Chief of these, perhaps, is the influence of the powerful financial and commercial organizations which dictate the financial policies of Japan. The long continued depression consequent upon the severe retrenchment policies of the Minseito Government had given much dissatisfaction to these interests. Some had opposed the lifting of the gold embargo, two years ago, and as the deflating effect of this measure bore down on the nation, causing a stagnation of trade, they were seeking for a change that would promise relief, however temporary it might be.

    Again, there is much to indicate that the military authorities of the Empire have of late been increasingly impatient with civilian interference, particularly in regard to the Manchurian situation. (See Embassy's despatch No. 423, December 18, 1931). The Seiyukai, with its record and tradition of a "strong policy" vis-a-vis China is said to have appealed to these authorities as more amenable to the military views than the Minseito. It is safe to say that the army had no little influence, even though possibly indirect, in the downfall of the Wakatsuki Cabinet and in the establishment of the Seiyukai Government. In this connection it may be worth noting that the new Minister of War is rumored to be the leader of that group of young officers which is said to dominate the army and which seeks to extend the powers of the Military in government.

    It is interesting again to note the absolute power of decision which the Genro retains in a country where theoretically the representative form of government has been established. It was in his power to reappoint Wakatsuki; to have created a coalition

[Pencil check marks in the left margin; a black diagonal smear at the top left.]

=== Page 228 ===
- 10 -

coalition ministry, or to choose a straight Seiyukai government.

    It would be interesting, but futile, to speculate on the reasons for the Genro's decision. The Japanese press praises the wisdom of Saionji in allowing the opposition to succeed to power after the fall of a government, thereby helping to develop the system of responsible government. It is said that the Genro before making his recommendation to the Emperor, asked Inukai if he would form a coalition with the Minseito. Inukai, it is reported, feeling his own strength in this crisis, demurred and the Genro allowed him to form a straight Seiyukai Ministry.

    The new Ministry was greeted with no great show of enthusiasm by the public. To a certain extent any new doctor who would attempt to prescribe a new cure for the ailing country was welcome, but the public has not forgotten the sordid scandals and waste of the last Seiyukai Ministry under the late General Tanaka. The hope is expressed in the press that the new Cabinet under Mr. Inukai, who himself has the confidence of a large section of the public as regards his personal integrity and honesty, will cleanse itself of the soiled elements in the Party.

    Mr. Inukai is an able politician. During a long political career he has been elected to the Diet eighteen times and has served as a Minister of State three times previously to the present. He has before him at this time, however, a path beset with extraordinary difficulties. His most immediate problem is that of the situation in the Diet, where there is a margin of about eighty seats in favor of the Opposition. There is much speculation as to whether there will be a dissolution in the forthcoming session of the Diet. Both the Seiyukai

[Pencil check marks in the left margin.]

=== Page 229 ===
- 11 -

Seiyukai and the Minseito are apparently averse to the idea of dissolution, with its risks and expenses, but the force of circumstances may precipitate the action. The Cabinet is, however, leaving nothing to chance. Wholesale changes of officials throughout the country, already announced, may be taken to indicate that the new Government intends to lose no time in establishing a strong governmental machine on which to rely in the event of an election. If the Government can effect some compromise with the Minseito, it may do so, but if the Minseito proves recalcitrant, the Government will be left no alternative but to dissolve the Diet and call for a general election. This the Minseito would also avoid, for it is a political axiom in Japan that the Government party always wins in elections.

    Aside from this problem, which is purely political, there are others of far greater importance which the new Cabinet must face. Of these the most outstanding is the Manchurian dispute, with its complications in Paris and at home. In this problem the Cabinet is fortunate to be in more complete accord with the army than its predecessor, and will be able to act without the friction and discord which marked the past. Mr. Yoshizawa, reported as chosen to be the new Foreign Minister, is an expert on Chinese matters and was Minister to China prior to his appointment to Paris. As the son-in-law of the Premier, it may be expected that he will cooperate closely with Mr. Inukai.

    In the field of domestic affairs, the most important question for the new Government is the financial situation. The first act of the new Cabinet was to reimpose an embargo on the export of gold, in accordance with the proclaimed policy

[Pencil check marks in the left margin.]

=== Page 230 ===
- 12 -

policy of the Seiyukai, reversing the policy followed by the Minseito Government for the past two years (See Embassy's despatch No. 406, December 8, 1931). This move, which was made as an expedient to afford relief to hardpressed industry, will have, it is feared, serious effects on the nation as a whole. There is still the matter of the unbalanced budget to contend with. Mr. Takahashi, the new Minister of Finance, has announced that the new Cabinet will adopt, in the main, the budget which the previous government had under preparation. The deficit is to be cared for by new loans.

    The new Cabinet is fortunate in having made no definite commitments or promises to the public, and in thus being unhampered in dealing with problems that confront it. There are rumors that the Cabinet will draw up a program of policies in the near future, but as yet none has been announced.
